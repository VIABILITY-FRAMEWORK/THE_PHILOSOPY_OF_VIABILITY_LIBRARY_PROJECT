                                              Abstract
          Hub Dynamics (HD) established a conservation framework for single mediation nodes,
      proving that inflow equals outflow plus sediment accumulation. This paper generalizes the
      single-hub conservation law to a network of interconnected hubs, deriving a global balance
      equation that governs any flow-mediation network—from gas pipelines to data networks,
      from traffic systems to supply chains. We demonstrate three key results: (1) the global con-
      servation equation provides a mathematical justification for the “atomic decomposition” of
      linear networks in the Arta framework; (2) Junction Hubs serve as boundary conditions that
      couple separate conservation domains, ensuring systemic consistency; and (3) the stationary
      distribution from the single-hub sediment model can be extended to network-wide predic-
      tive monitoring, enabling early-warning systems that outperform traditional SCADA-based
      monitoring. A practical application to gas pipeline monitoring is presented.


1     Introduction
Centralized mediation of flows is a recurrent motif across complex systems: API gateways buffer
digital requests, distribution centers aggregate physical goods, and cellular membrane trans-
porters mediate molecular fluxes. In a previous work, we introduced Hub Dynamics (HD),
a conservation-based mathematical framework that describes the behavior of a single hub—a
generic flow-mediation node—through a dimensional-invariant balance equation [?].
    However, real-world systems rarely consist of a single hub. They form interconnected net-
works: a national gas pipeline spanning thousands of kilometers, a supply chain with multiple
warehouses, a telecommunications network with cascading routers. Each node in such a network
is a hub; each edge is a flow channel. Yet no extant theory unifies the behavior of such hub
networks under one conservation principle.
    This paper bridges that gap. We generalize the single-hub conservation law to a Network
Conservation Law (NCL), proving that the sum of all inflows into a network equals the sum
of all outflows plus the rate of change of total internal sediment. We then demonstrate how this
generalization provides the mathematical foundation for the Arta framework within the HANG
meta-platform [?].

3     Application to Gas Pipeline Monitoring
3.1   The Problem with Linear Geometries
In traditional GIS systems, a national gas pipeline is stored as a single LineString geometry—a
continuous line spanning thousands of kilometers. This representation has a critical flaw: a line
has no volume, and therefore no capacity to store sediment. Under this model, Equation (??)
degenerates to vα− (t) = vα+ (t)—a “perfect conductor” assumption that never holds in reality.
    Real gas pipelines exhibit:

    • Linepack: The quantity of gas stored within the pipeline itself, varying with pressure,
      temperature, and pipe diameter.

    • Pressure drops: Frictional losses along the pipeline.

    • Compressibility effects: Transient behavior during valve operations.

    None of these can be captured by a single LineString.

5.1   Limitations and Future Work
The current framework assumes time-invariant network topology. Extensions to dynamic net-
works with reconfigurable edges (e.g., rerouting in power grids) and empirical validation on real
gas pipeline data are natural next steps.


6     Conclusion
This paper generalized the single-hub conservation law of Hub Dynamics to a Network Con-
servation Law governing any flow-mediation network. We showed that:
   1. The NCL provides the mathematical justification for the atomic decomposition princi-
      ple in the Arta framework—without atoms, the conservation equation cannot be closed.
   2. Junction Hubs serve as boundary conditions that ensure systemic consistency across
      coupled ecosystems.
   3. The stationary sediment distribution enables predictive monitoring that identifies crit-
      ical atoms before failure occurs.
    The NCL is not merely a theoretical exercise—it is the mathematical backbone of the HANG
Spatial Identity & Intelligence Meta-Platform, transforming it from a software system into
a physics-grounded theory of flow networks.
