This book is a work of philosophy. It is not a commentary, not a theological treatise, and not

a scientic monograph.   It is an attempt to reconstruct metaphysics from the ground up, on

the basis of a single architectonic principle:   that the structure of any explanatory cognition

can be analyzed through four columnsxed elements, uid elements, governing dynamics,

and contextand that the classical problems of identity, manifestation, and ourishing can be

dissolved, rather than solved, once this four-column structure is made explicit.

   The book is written in a strictly philosophical register.   It does not rest on revelation, on

authority, on empirical data, or on mathematical formalism.      Its arguments are oered to be

assessed on their own merits, and each claim is labelled with the kind of support it has and

the kind of criticism it invites. The book does not, however, claim to have come from nowhere.

Its architecture has ancestors in Greek, Islamic, and Indian philosophy and in the Kantian and

Hegelian tradition, and the Introduction says so plainly. Where the book engages those traditions,

it does so in a comparative mode in the nal part, and never as a basis for justication. The

reader who has no acquaintance with any tradition can follow the argument from beginning to

end, and can assess it on its own terms.



The Autonomy of Philosophical Justication.             The validity of no philosophical principle

in this book depends on conrmation from any outside domain. Its concepts and principles are

to be assessed through precise denition, internal coherence, and philosophical argument. Any

resemblance to formulations elsewhere plays no part in justifying them.

   The book is also written as a single, self-contained work. Every concept that it uses is dened

within the book, and every argument that it oers is given within the book. It is oered as a

proposal, not as a nished system; but the proposal is complete in itself, and it states, in the

ledgers at the end of Chapter 1 and of Part VI, which premises its main arguments depend on.

   The reader is invited to object, to revise, and to rene. The horizon is open. The conversation

continues.

The Problem
For over two millennia, the problem of identity has remained the central aporia of metaphysics.

From the Ship of Theseus in ancient Greece to the doctrine of No-Self in Buddhist philosophy,

from Locke's memory criterion to contemporary paradoxes of personal identity, philosophers have

repeatedly failed to reconcile the persistence of a thing with its continuous change.

    This book argues that this persistent failure stems from a foundational ontological error: the

conation of identity with manifestation. Identity is the invariant structure that persists through

transformation; manifestation is the appearance of that structure in a receiving realm, according

to the realm's capacity. The two are not the same; they are not even on the same level. Identity

is the source; manifestation is the expression. And between them lies the entire architecture of

reality.

    The error is not merely theoretical. It has practical consequences. When identity is conated

with manifestation, the persistence of a person is confused with the continuity of their memories;

the identity of a city is confused with the continuity of its buildings; the identity of a tradition

is confused with the continuity of its texts.   In each case, the confusion generates a paradox:

the entity seems to persist and not to persist at the same time. The dissolution of the paradox

requires, rst, the distinction between identity and manifestation, and, second, an account of

how the two are related.

    The problem is not only how an identity continues through change.           It is also how   one
identity appears in many realms, keeping its unity of essence, while its manifestations may dier

in structure.

    The title names the concept that connects the metaphysics to its practical upshot. Identity

does not admit of degrees: a thing is the same one or it is not. But an identity can express itself

well or badly. We call the success of that expression viability. The viability of an identity in a

realm has three components: the delity of its manifestations to its structure, the sustainability

of the cost of producing them, and the recoverability of its capacity to manifest. Viability admits

of degrees; identity does not; and the two are related as what is expressed to how well it is

expressed. The concept is dened in Denition 1.7, and the book returns to it in Chapters 21,

24, 26, and 37. This is why the book can be at once a metaphysics of identity and a philosophy

of ourishing.

1.1 Why Four Columns?
Every cognition that claims to understand something must answer four questions:



 (1)   What?  What are the elements that are known? (Fixed elements)


 (2)   How?  How are these elements connected? (Fluid elements)


 (3)   On what basis?  What rules govern the connections? (Governing dynamics)


 (4)   Where?  From what standpoint is the cognition carried out? (Context)


   If any one of these questions remains unanswered, the cognition is   incomplete as an ex-
planation. This chapter argues that the four questions are jointly necessary, and oers good
reasonsthough not a proofto think that they are jointly sucient.

   Two clarications of scope and of method are needed at the outset, because the rest of the

book depends on them.




Scope.    The framework does not claim to describe every mental state.       A momentary sensa-

tion, a mood, or a bare act of attention need not answer all four questions.       The framework

concerns explanatory cognition : cognition whose aim is to understand how some things stand to

one another. Whenever this book says cognition without qualication, it means explanatory

cognition in this sense.




Method.     The four-column principle is a piece of conceptual analysis. Its justication there-

fore has three parts, and the reader is invited to judge each part separately: (i) a removal test

showing that dropping any one column leaves a recognizable explanatory failure (Section 1.3);

(ii) a reducibility test applied to candidate fth columns (the same section); and (iii) the fruit-

fulness of the framework in the chapters that follow. The status labels ((A), (T), (E), (Q), (S))
introduced in the Introduction are used here with exactly those meanings. In particular, a (T)

claim in this chapter is one that follows from the stated denitions; the denitions themselves

are commitments, and are labelled as such.

           (Part II): four fundamental and three barzakhic. A realm is an ontological level, not

           a standpoint of cognition.


    The principle of the innity of contexts (Principle 1.8) concerns the rst kind; Part II

    concerns the second. They do not conict, since the innitely many frames in which a

    thing may be studied are compatible with a nite number of levels on which it may be

    realized.   A   perspective (Section 5.5) is the position of an observer within a cognitive
    context.

    An existential context does not require a cognizing subject in order to be real. A manifes-

    tation may be realized in a realm without being known, observed, or reconstructed by any

    subject. A cognitive context conditions the knowledge of a manifestation; an existential

    context conditions its realization.

    The context of negotiation denes the parties as xed elements, language as the uid

    denes districts, roads, and the laws of exchange in the same three roles.

1.3 The Four-Column Principle
   Principle 1.3 (Four-Column Principle)

   (T) (necessity) Every explanatory cognition requires the simultaneous presence of four
   columns: xed elements, uid elements, governing dynamics, and context. If any one of

   the four is absent, the cognition is incomplete as an explanation.

   (E) (suciency) No fth column is needed: every candidate fth column that has been
   examined reduces to one of the four.



Proof of necessity: the removal test. By Denitions 1.1 to 1.4, an explanation says what is re-
lated, by what the relation is carried, according to what rule, and from what standpoint. We

remove each column in turn and observe what remains.


 (a)    Without xed elements there is nothing for eect to hold between; what remains is a ow
        with no terms, which is a description of process but not of what the process connects.


 (b)    Without uid elements the xed elements are a list, not a network. Nothing is said about
        how any one bears on another. (This is the conveyor principle, Section 1.4.)


  (c)   Without governing dynamics the network is unordered : it shows that things are connected
        but not how the connection behaves, so it cannot support prediction or counterfactual

        reasoning.


 (d)    Without context the explanation is a rule without a standpoint. Such a rule underdetermines
        its own application: the same entities can be partitioned into xed and uid in dierent

        ways (Section 1.5), and the rule alone does not say which partition is intended.


  (e) Each removal yields a recognizable explanatory failure. Hence each column is necessary.




   Principle 1.4 (The Distinctness of the Four Analytical Roles)

   (T) The four questions are distinct: an answer to any one of them does not, by itself,
   supply the answer to any other.



Argument.        (1) Knowing what the elements are does not say what, if anything, passes between

        them. Two things may be fully described and yet nothing be said of how one bears on the

        other.


 (2) Knowing that something passes between them does not say by what rule it behaves.            A

        conduit may be named without any law of its working.


 (3) Knowing the rule does not say from what standpoint the elements are partitioned as they

        are, since the same rule can be applied to dierent partitions.


 (4) Knowing the standpoint does not supply the elements, the conduit, or the rule that are to

        be found from it.
8                                            Chapter 1.   The Four-Column Framework of Cognition



    (5) Hence no one of the four answers is supplied by another taken alone, and the four roles are

          distinct.




     Remark 1.13 (Distinctness is not separability)

     Distinctness does not imply absolute separability. Two analytical roles may be instantiated

     by the same entity, or one role may constrain the content of another (the rules of a cognition

     may be constrained by the structure of its elements), without the roles thereby becoming

     conceptually identical.




     Remark 1.14 (The limit of the argument)

     The conclusion is limited. It shows that the roles are distinct and all required. It does

     not show that they are carried by four distinct kinds of thing in reality; one entity can

     play several roles in dierent cognitions (Remark 1.8), and this is the point of the context-

     dependence principle below.




Support for suciency: the reducibility test. Suciency cannot be proved from the denitions,
since a new kind of explanation might always turn up. It is therefore oered as an estimate and

supported by a test. A candidate fth column is reducible when its role in explanation can be

reassigned to one of the four columns without loss. Three standard candidates:



    (a)   Purpose or goal. A teleological explanation (the knife is made in order to cut) states a
          rule of the form the system tends toward state S . That is a governing dynamics.



    (b)   Time. Temporal order enters an explanation either as a feature of the dynamics or as part
          of the frame (the scale at which the elements are individuated). In either case it is carried

          by the third or fourth column.



    (c)   The observer. The standpoint of the cognizer is the content of the context column (the
          question asked, the means used, the scale adopted).



The test would fail, and the principle would have to be revised, if an explanation accepted as

complete could be exhibited whose content cannot be redistributed over the four columns in this

way. The framework therefore makes a risky claim, and says in advance what would refute it.


     Remark 1.15

     The four-column principle is not a stipulation: the necessity part follows from what an

     explanation is asked to do. The suciency part is an estimate. This is the strongest status

     the principle can honestly claim, and the rest of the book uses it at exactly that strength.

1.4 The Conveyor Principle
   Principle 1.5 (Conveyor Principle)

   (T) No explanation of how one xed element bears on another is complete unless it invokes
   a uid element: a carrier, medium, signal, or process by which the eect passes.



Proof: Reductio.      (1) Suppose an explanation of how A bears on B is complete and invokes no

       uid element.


 (2) Then it says only that A and B are xed elements and that B changes when A does.


 (3) That statement records a correlation between two nodes; it does not say how A bears on

       B , since by hypothesis no route, medium, or process is specied.

 (4) An explanation that does not say how is incomplete as an explanation of how.


 (5) This contradicts the supposition. Hence every complete explanation invokes a uid element.




   Remark 1.16 (What would falsify the principle)

   The principle is only as strong as the breadth of the term uid element, and the term

   is deliberately broad: anything that conveys eect counts, whether a thing, a signal, or a

   process. Because of that breadth, the principle could not be refuted by merely renaming a

   mediator. A genuine counterexample must be an explanation that (i) names no mediating

   medium, signal, or process, and (ii) is nonetheless accepted as complete.      Unmediated

   action at a distance is the standard candidate: one thing bears on another across a gap

   with nothing passing between them, and the account is held to leave no further question

   about how.       The framework holds that such an account always leaves the question of

   how unanswered; whoever exhibits an accepted complete account of that kind refutes

   the principle.




1.4.1     Non-Sequential Connectivity


The conveyor principle requires that some uid element carry the eect. It does not say that

the eect must traverse, one after another, every element that lies between two xed elements

in some ordering of them.


   Principle 1.6 (Non-Sequential Connectivity)

   (T) The conveyor principle does not require that the connection between two xed elements
   pass through all intermediate xed elements.      A uid element may connect two xed

   elements directly, even when other xed elements lie between them in some ordering.



Argument. The reductio above uses only the premise that an explanation of how A bears on B
must name a carrier. Nothing in it requires the carrier to pass through further nodes. A principle
10                                         Chapter 1.      The Four-Column Framework of Cognition



that required passage through every intermediate node would need a further premise (that the

ordering of nodes is itself a route), and no such premise is part of the conveyor principle.


     Remark 1.17 (Where this is developed)

     This is stated here for cognition. Its ontological counterpart, that a presence or eect in a

     lower realm does not require passage through every realm between, is argued in Chapter 2

     and Chapter 6.



     Example 1.5 (Conversation)

     If language is removed from the negotiation, the two parties are disconnected nodes.

     Language is therefore the condition of possibility of the explanation of how one bears on

     the other.




1.5 The Context-Dependence Principle
     Principle 1.7 (Context-Dependence)

     (T) The xedness or uidity of an element depends on the cognitive context, not on the
     element's essence. A single element can be xed in one context and uid in another.



Proof: Counterexample to essentialism.       (1) Essentialism says: each element is xed, or is uid,

       in virtue of what it is, and so in every context.


 (2) But a river is a xed element (a boundary) in political geography and a uid element (a

       route of transport) in the geography of trade. A word is a xed element (an entry whose

       meaning is held constant) in a dictionary and a uid element (a conveyor of meaning

       between speakers) in a conversation.


 (3) So there are elements whose status diers between contexts. This contradicts the universal

       claim of essentialism.


 (4) Hence xedness and uidity are not essential properties of elements. They are roles, as-

       signed by the question a context asks: which relations it holds constant, and which it treats

       as carrying eect.




     Remark 1.18

     This principle generalizes category-relative predication (Chapter 18) from the level of the

     realm to the level of the cognitive context: just as a predicate is true or false relative

     to a realm, an element is xed or uid relative to a context. The principle does not say

     that every assignment of roles is equally good. That is the work of the criteria of context

     validity (Section 1.8).

   Remark 1.19 (Cognitive context and existential context)

   The principle concerns the cognitive context. It must not be read as saying that where a

   manifestation is realized depends on how it is studied. The realm in which a manifestation

   is realized is its existential context; it is xed by the identity and the receiving realm

1.6 The Innity of Contexts
   Principle 1.8 (Innity of Contexts)

   (T) Given the openness assumption below, the cognitive contexts in which an identity can
   be cognized are not nite in number: no nite list of them is complete.

   (S) Whether their number is countable or uncountable is left open.



   Denition 1.5 (Openness of Relations)

   (A) Openness of relations : for every cognitive context C and every manifestation m that
   an identity has in C , there is a cognitive context C

   related by uid elements and dynamics that are not available in C .



   Remark 1.20 (Why openness is plausible)

   A manifestation is not a mere label for the identity; it is the product of the identity's

   interaction with a receiving realm (Section 5.3). Once produced, it is a thing in its own

   right, and it can be related to other things in ways the producing context did not consider.

   A speech is manifested in a conversation and then becomes a xed element in an analysis

   of rhetoric; a building is manifested in a street and then becomes a xed element in the

   history of a city. The assumption is that this step never runs out. It is the framework's

   version of the familiar observation that every answer in inquiry opens further questions.




Proof: Non-terminating generation. (1) Let C0 be any cognitive context for an identity I , and
      let m0 be a manifestation of I in C0 .


 (2) By openness, there is a context C1 in which m0 is a xed element related by new uid

       elements and dynamics.    I has a manifestation m1 in C1 , and m1 diers from m0 since it
       is the product of I with a dierent context.


 (3) Apply the same step to m1 , and so on. The sequence C0 , C1 , C2 , . . . never repeats: each

       Cn+1 contains an element mn that is not a xed element of Cn or of any earlier context,
       since manifestations are context-bound products.


 (4) So no nite list of cognitive contexts is complete.

1.7 The Impossibility of Super-Identity
     Denition 1.6 (Super-Identity)

     A super-identity  claim is a subject's claim to have known, or to be able to know, all
     manifestations of an identity in all contexts, so that nothing about the identity's expression

     remains outside its knowledge.



     The claim must be distinguished from a dierent and acceptable one.          A subject may re-

construct an identity to some degree of adequacy, by nding what remains invariant across the

manifestations available to it; the result is a pseudo-identity (Section 4.4), and nothing in this

section denies that it can be good. What is denied is exhaustiveness.

     Three things are easily confused, and the book keeps them apart: the aggregation of data

or manifestations, the compatibility of the manifestations with one another, and the numerical

unity of the identity that they manifest.      Collecting manifestations (aggregation) and nding

them consistent (compatibility) do not restore the numerical unity of the source. This is argued

in Section 4.5 and in Chapter 17; it is only named here.


     Principle 1.9 (Impossibility of Super-Identity)

     (T) No nite subject can know all manifestations of an identity in all contexts.         The

     super-identity claim is therefore self-undermining.



Proof: Reductio. Premises:
P1. Finitude (Principle 1.1): every subject occupies nitely many contexts.

P2. Innity of contexts (Principle 1.8): the contexts in which an identity can be cognized are
       not nite in number.


P3. Manifestation is a product (A): the manifestation of I in a context is determined jointly
       by I and by the receiving realm, not by I alone. (This is argued in Section 5.3.)
1.7.   The Impossibility of Super-Identity                                                        13



P4. Knowing is situated : to know a manifestation is a cognition, and every cognition occurs in
       a context (the fourth column, Principle 1.3).



Reductio:



 (1) Suppose a nite subject knows all manifestations of I in all contexts.



 (2) By P3, a manifestation in C cannot be derived from I alone. Knowing I well, even perfectly,

       does not yield the manifestations in contexts that the subject has not entered.



 (3) By P4, knowing the manifestation in C is a cognition occurring in a context; the subject

       must have some standing in C for this to be possible.



 (4) By P2, there are non-nitely many such C , and by P1 the subject has standing in only

       nitely many.



 (5) Hence the subject cannot know all of the manifestations. This contradicts the supposition.




   Remark 1.23 (The generative-rule objection)

   It may be objected that a subject could know the invariant structure itself, and from it

   generate every manifestation by a rule, without entering each context. The framework's

   reply is P3: because manifestations are products of identity and receiving realm, no rule

   stated in terms of the identity alone yields them.     A generative rule would need the

   receiving contexts as inputs, and these are the very thing the nite subject lacks. This

   reply is the reason P3 is given the weight of a premise in this argument.



   Remark 1.24 (What the principle does not say)

   It does not say that identity is unknowable. It says that exhaustive knowledge is impossible

   and that claims to it are always mistaken. What remains is asymptotic knowledge: pseudo-

   identities of increasing adequacy (Section 4.4). The impossibility is epistemic. A further

   point about the kind of object an aggregate is (Section 4.5) and a fuller treatment of

   objections (Chapter 17) are given later; this chapter introduces and states the principle,

   Chapter 4 analyzes it in relation to identity, and Chapter 17 gives the detailed argument.



   Example 1.6 (A city)

   Someone who says I have known every manifestation of this city would have to have a

   standing in the economic, cultural, architectural, and legal contexts of the city, and in

   indenitely many more, since each manifestation that one frame produces can serve as a

   claim is not merely unproved but unavailable.

1.8 The Criteria of Context Validity
The plurality of contexts raises a danger: if any partition of entities into xed and uid counts

as a context, the framework would license arbitrary partitions. Three criteria discipline the use

of cognitive contexts. They are adopted, not derived from the four-column principle, and are

labelled accordingly.


     Principle 1.10 (Cross-Checkability)

     (A) A valid context is one whose xed and uid elements can be identied and distinguished
     from at least one other valid context.



     Principle 1.11 (Minimality)

     (A) A valid context is the smallest context that can still explain the phenomenon.



     Principle 1.12 (Consistency)

     (A) A valid context is consistent in all its overlaps with other valid contexts.



     Remark 1.25 (Why cross-checkability)

     The rst criterion cannot be stated as observable independently of the context, since in

     a framework in which elements are individuated by contexts that wording is not available.

     The cross-checkability form asks for something that the framework can supply: an element

     that is a xed element of a context must be recognizable from another valid context as

     the same element. This is what blocks purely private contexts.



     Remark 1.26 (The three-fold test)

     (1) Can the elements of the context be identied from some other valid context? (2) Is the

     context the smallest that explains the phenomenon? (3) Is the context consistent in all

     its overlaps? A context that fails one of these is invalid. These criteria apply to cognitive

     contexts.



     Principle 1.13 (Manifestational Adequacy (proposed))

     Status: proposed; not yet assigned (A) or (T). The context chosen for the study of a
     manifestation, and the realm to which a manifestation is attributed, must have the capacity

     and aptitude for the kind of appearance attributed to it.



     Remark 1.27 (What this criterion does and does not say)

     The criterion does not say that each context admits only one possible manifestation.

     Contexts dier in aptitude, and several manifestations of one identity may be suited to

     one realm. It says only that attributing to a realm an appearance of a kind that the realm
1.9.    The Four Columns and the Philosophy of Viability                                                 15




   cannot bear is an error of attribution. It is proposed because the existing three criteria

   govern the use of cognitive contexts and say nothing of the aptitude of the realm in which a

   manifestation is realized; it is retained only if Chapter 5 gives it an independent denition

   and support.




   Remark 1.28 (Status of the criteria)

   The three criteria are (A). A critic who rejects them is invited to propose alternatives; the

   framework's claim is that some such criteria are necessary, not that these particular ones

   are nal.




1.9 The Four Columns and the Philosophy of Viability
The four-column framework is not an external addition to the philosophy of viability. It is the

analytical structure within which that philosophy is developed. Since this book is named after

viability, the term is dened here, at the point where the framework meets it, rather than at the

end of the book.



   Denition 1.7 (Viability)

   (A) The viability of an identity in a context is the success of its expression there. Success
   has three components:


        (i)    Fidelity : the manifestations adequately reect the structure of the identity (Chap-
               ter 9, Chapter 21);


       (ii)    Sustainability : the cost of producing and maintaining the manifestations is one that
               can be borne (Chapter 26);


       (iii)   Recoverability : the identity has not lost its capacity to manifest in that realm, that
               is, the singularity at which restoring a manifestation costs more than creating a new

               one has not been crossed (Chapter 24).




   Remark 1.29 (Viability is not identity)

   Identity is rigid: it admits no degrees (Principle 4.4). Viability admits degrees: expression

   can succeed more or less, at a lower or a higher cost, and can be more or less recoverable.

   The two statements do not conict, because viability is a property of the relation between

   an identity and its manifestations in a realm, not of the identity itself. This is the structural

   reason why a philosophy of identity can also be a philosophy of ourishing: the identity is

   what is expressed; viability is how well. The normative upshot is developed in Chapters 26

   and 37.

2.1 Denition of a Fundamental Realm
Chapter 1 separated the architecture of cognition from the architecture of being. This chapter

and the next begin the second. They use two terms in two roles, and the usage is xed for the

   Denition 2.1 (Fundamental Realm)

   A fundamental realm is an independently real mode of existence within which a specic

   mode of manifestation, activity, and interaction is possible.



   Remark 2.1 (Realm and context)

   A realm is an ontological level: a mode of existence. A context is the setting in which a

   manifestation is realized or a cognition is carried out. The realm in which a manifestation is

   realized is its existential context ; a frame of inquiry is a cognitive context (Remark 1.12).

   The words are kept apart so that a level of being is not mistaken for a standpoint of

   knowing.



   Principle 2.1 (Independent Reality of the Realms)

   (A) Each realm is independently real: it has a mode of existence of its own, with rules of
   its own and possibilities of activity of its own, and it is not merely an aspect, a function,

   or a name for a relation among other realms.



   Remark 2.2 (What independence does and does not mean)

   Independence is not separability. The realms are ordered and are related (Section 2.6);

   a realm's independence means that what it is cannot be restated without remainder in

   terms of another realm's mode of being. Independence is a commitment of the framework

   ((A)). Its support is that, if it is denied, the rules and possibilities peculiar to each realm

   (Chapter 7) lose their subject.    It would be refuted by showing that the rules of some

   realm are in fact consequences of the rules of another.

2.2 The First Determination
   Denition 2.3 (First Determination)

   The First Determination is the fundamental realm of pure unity. Its mode of existence is

   that of an identity as such: there is no multiplicity, no change, and no succession in it.



   Remark 2.4 (The First Determination is not the ultimate source)

   The First Determination is not asserted to be the ultimate source of reality. It is the rst

   level at which the source's reality takes a determinate form. The source in its essence is

   beyond all determination; its relation to the First Determination is one of grounding, not

   of identity.



   Remark 2.5 (The Three Marks of Purity)

        (1)   Changelessness. Change requires multiplicity (a before and an after, a state and
              a transition). There is no multiplicity here, so there is no change.
22                                         Chapter 2.   The Fundamental Realms: The Four Categories




      (2)     Timelessness.        Succession requires a before and an after.   There is none here.

              (That the First Determination is without time does not make it merely unlasting;

              see Section 23.7.)


      (3)     Indivisibility. Division requires parts. There are none here.



     Remark 2.6 (What is possible here)

     The activity possible in the First Determination is not motion or becoming.            It is the

     being-what-it-is of an identity without remainder. This is not an absence of activity but
     its limit case: an activity that has no need to become anything else.




     Remark 2.7 (Ontological Distinctiveness)

     The First Determination diers from all others in that nothing in it is plural. In every other

2.6 The Relations Between the Fundamental Realms
The relations among realms are of three kinds, which do not coincide and do not need to follow

one path.



 (1)   Ordinal relation. The place of a realm in the overall order of expression: from unity,
       through general multiplicity and relational multiplicity, to material multiplicity. This re-

       lation is structural, not spatial: it says which realm is more general or more unied than

       which, not along what route anything must travel.


 (2)   Manifestation relation. The appearance of an identity in a realm other than its own,
       in a form suited to that realm (Chapter 5).


 (3)   Inuence relation. The eect of one realm, or of an identity in it, on another.
2.6.   The Relations Between the Fundamental Realms                                               25




   Remark 2.21 (Higher and lower)

   Throughout the book, higher and lower designate ordinal positions in the architecture

   of expression.   They do not designate existential routes, causal priorities, or degrees of

   reality. That one realm is higher than another says that it is more unied or more general;

   it does not say that anything must travel from it to the other, that it produces the other,

   or that the other is less real.



   Principle 2.3 (Non-Necessity of Downward Passage)

   (A) The presence or eect of an identity in a lower realm does not require that the identity
   pass existentially through every realm that lies between. Relations among the realms are

   not conned to a single sequential route.



   Remark 2.22 (Support and what would refute it)

   Two considerations support this. First, by the independence of the realms (Principle 2.1),

   each realm has its own capacity to receive; the form that an identity takes in a lower

   realm is xed by the identity and that realm's capacity (Chapter 5), not by what the

   identity became in the intervening realms. Second, the three relations above are distinct,

   and the ordinal relation does not by itself impose a route. The principle would be refuted

   by showing that the capacity of a lower realm to receive an identity is constituted by the

   identity's manifestations in each intermediate realm; the framework denies that.



   Remark 2.23 (What the principle does not say)

   It does not say that the intermediate realms are idle or dispensable. The barzakhic realms

   have functions of their own, and an identity's presence in a lower realm may still be

   regulated by the rules of the realms that bear on it.     Regulation is not passage.   The

   relation of this principle to the necessity of mediation is made explicit in Chapter 12.



   Remark 2.24 (Why four)

   The number of fundamental realms is not taken from any tradition. It is argued from the

   structure of expression: unity, general multiplicity, relational multiplicity, and material

   multiplicity are four qualitative grades of multiplicity. What that argument establishes,

   and what it does not, is stated in Chapter 10. It does not follow from the four-column

   framework (Principle 1.2).

3.1 Denition of a Barzakhic Realm

   includes mediating, transforming, or regulating relations between other realms.



   Remark 3.1 (Function is not dependence)

   That a barzakhic realm has a mediating function does not make it dependent in existence

   or reduce it to a gap between two other realms. The mediating function is a feature of

   its mode of existence. A realm may be independently real and still perform a function for

   others, just as a thing may exist in its own right and also serve as a means.

   (A) The Hub of Decree, the Hub of Signication, and the Hub of Realization are each

3.4 The Hub of Realization
   Denition 3.4 (Hub of Realization)

   The Hub of Realization is the barzakhic realm of actualization. Its mode of existence is

   that of the realizing of what is possible as something concrete.

   Realization is not merely a stage at which meanings are converted into matter.       It has

   on which they succeed or fail.    This is why the Hub of Realization has rules of justice,

   care, and sustainability that regulate how the conditions of manifestation are distributed,

   The Material Manifestation is the realm of what has been realized: things that are discrete,

   Denition 3.5 (Mixed Nature)

   A barzakhic realm has a mixed nature when its mode of existence shows features of both

   of its neighboring realms without being identical with either. The mixture is functional

   and qualitative ; it is not a deciency of existence.

   realm may combine features of the realm above (for example, the unity that an allotment

   preserves) and of the realm below (the plurality that it makes possible), and still be a

   complete level of existence. Mixture explains why the hubs can mediate; it does not make

   them less real.

4.1 Denition of Identity

  Denition 4.1 (Identity)

  An identity is that which remains invariant under a specied class of allowable transfor-

  mations.




  Remark 4.1

  Identity is not a substance, not a property, not a thing. It is a pattern of invariance across

  a space of possible transformations.    To say that a thing has an identity is to say that

  there is a class of transformations under which it remains the same. The framework asks

  what stays the same under the transformations a thing tolerates, with one dierence that

  matters in Section 4.7: the invariance is that of one source, not merely of a type.




  Remark 4.2 (The Determination of the Class of Allowable Transformations)

  The phrase specied class of allowable transformations raises a question: who species

  the class? If the observer species it, identity becomes subjective. If the structure species

  it, we risk circularity. The framework's answer is that the class of allowable transforma-

  tions is internal to the identity itself. A structure is not rst given and then subjected to

  transformations; rather, the structure is the pattern of invariance across a space of possible

  transformations. To grasp a structure is to grasp the transformations it tolerates. This

  is not a vicious circle but a hermeneutic circle: the identity and its transformations are

  co-constituted. The observer does not choose the class; the observer discovers it through

  the labor of reconstruction. The discovery is fallible, and the criteria by which it is checked

  are given in Section 4.4.




  Denition 4.2 (Dening Invariants)

  (A) The dening invariants of an identity are the features that constitute its determina-
  tion: those that distinguish it from its alternatives and are xed by what it is. They are

  taken as primitive in the framework.

4.1.1      The Distinction Between Invariance and Composition


The denition speaks of structure and of what remains invariant. The word invites a picture

of an arrangement of parts, and that picture does not t every realm. It would make the First

Determination, which is pure unity (Denition 2.3), either composite or no identity at all.


   Principle 4.1 (Invariance Is Not Composition)

   (T) That an identity is invariant does not entail that it is composed of several parts.
   Structure, in the denition of identity, means the principle that distinguishes the identity
38                                                                            Chapter 4.   Identities




     and holds it one, and not necessarily an arrangement of components.




Argument.      (1) To be invariant under a class of transformations is to be unchanged by each

       member of the class. That is a statement about the relation of the thing to the transfor-

       mations, and says nothing about whether the thing has parts.


 (2) A thing without parts can be unchanged under every transformation of a class (for the

       class may be empty or trivially satised).


 (3) Hence invariance does not entail composition.


 (4) In realms of multiplicity, the principle of distinction and unity may show itself as an

       arrangement and ratio of parts. In the realm of pure unity it cannot, and structure there

       names the principle itself.




     Example 4.1 (A melody)

     A melody transposed to another key has every one of its notes changed, and is still the

     same melody.   A melody in which a single interval is altered may not be.     A change of

     composition (which notes) is therefore neither sucient to show that the identity is lost

     nor sucient to show that it persists.   What decides is whether the dening invariants

     (here, the relations among the intervals) are kept.   Not every compositional change is

     allowable, and not every compositional change is a change of identity.



     Remark 4.5 (Consequence)

     The apparent conict between the pure unity of the First Determination and a structural

     denition of identity is removed: the denition uses structure in the wide sense, and the

     narrower sense of arrangement is available only where there is plurality.

4.3 Three Features of Identity
 (1)     Identity is relational. Identity is not a property that a thing has in isolation; it is a

         identity of a thing is not given; it is enacted.

   The two statements concern dierent things.              What is dynamic is the enactment, that

   invariant that is enacted. The dierence is an instance of category-relative predication,

4.4 Pseudo-Identity
     Denition 4.5 (Pseudo-Identity)

     A pseudo-identity is the observer's approximation of an identity, constructed from the

     available manifestations.



     Remark 4.10

     A pseudo-identity is not a falsehood; it is a working model: the best approximation the

     observer can achieve, given the available manifestations and the costs of reconstruction.

     It is a hypothesis about the identity, subject to revision in light of new manifestations. In

     the vocabulary of Remark 4.12, a pseudo-identity is an estimate of a signature.



     Remark 4.11 (The Three Criteria of Adequacy)

     A pseudo-identity can be more or less adequate. Its adequacy is measured by three criteria:


      (1)   Consistency. Observers working in dierent contexts, who carry out the labor of
            reconstruction, must arrive at the same pseudo-identity, up to isomorphism.


      (2)   Stability. The pseudo-identity must be stable under small perturbations of the
            observer's context or cognitive apparatus.


      (3)   Fruitfulness. A system that operates according to the pseudo-identity must be
            able to navigate its environment, predict future manifestations, and maintain its
4.5.   The Fallacy of Super-Identity (Detailed)                                                      41




             viability (Denition 1.7).


   These criteria do not prove that a pseudo-identity is adequate in an absolute sense. They

   provide a fallibilist account of adequacy, of the same kind that scientic theories have.

   They also have a second use in this chapter: they are the evidence base for the conservation

   principle (Section 4.6).



   Remark 4.12 (Signatures and pseudo-identities)

   The relation between the notions introduced so far is this: the identity is the invariance

   enacted by one source; its signature is the structural description of that invariance; a

   pseudo-identity is an observer's estimate of the signature. Observers deal only with the
   last two. This is why identity is never exhausted by any record of it (Section 1.7).



   Remark 4.13 (Four things that must not be run together)

   A representation that falls short of the identity is not a rival identity. Four cases dier:


       (1)   Cognitive error : a mistake in reconstruction, correctable by further manifestations.

       (2)   Incomplete representation : a pseudo-identity that is accurate as far as it goes but
             does not exhaust the signature. Every pseudo-identity is incomplete in this way.


       (3)   Misattribution : assigning a manifestation to an identity of which it is not a mani-
             festation.


       (4)   An independent identity : a dierent source with an identity of its own.

   A pseudo-identity may be a cognitive error or an incomplete representation; it is not, by

   being so, an independent identity that competes with the original. Calling a reconstruction

   pseudo states its epistemic standing, and gives it no ontological standing of its own.




4.5 The Fallacy of Super-Identity (Detailed)
The impossibility principle of Section 1.7 says that no nite subject can know all manifestations

of an identity.      The present section concerns a dierent and earlier mistake, one that arises

even when the subject sets out to know only some of them:            the belief that identity can be

reconstructed by aggregating manifestations taken from dierent contexts into a single record.


   Principle 4.2 (Fallacy of Super-Identity)

   (T) A record formed by aggregating manifestations drawn from contexts whose elements
   are not mutually translatable is not a reconstruction of the identity.         It is a context-

   confounded record: an object that corresponds to no identity at all.



Proof.       (1) To reconstruct an identity is to nd what remains invariant across its manifestations

       (Denition 4.1).
42                                                                               Chapter 4.   Identities



 (2) Each manifestation is the product of the identity and the context in which it appears

       (Section 5.3). It therefore contains two components, contributed by the identity and by

       the context, and these are not separated in the manifestation as given.


 (3) Invariants are isolated by comparing manifestations while the context varies and the identity

       is held constant : what survives the variation is attributed to the identity. Mere concate-
       nation does not compare; it only collects.


 (4) A concatenated record therefore mixes the identity's contribution with the contributions

       of every context it has absorbed, and gives no way of telling which is which.


 (5) Hence concatenation does not reconstruct the identity. A record that treats the mixture

       as an identity has no corresponding identity; call it a monstrosity in the sense of a thing

       that is put together from parts that do not belong to one whole.




     Remark 4.14

     The fallacy of super-identity is the counterpart, at the level of method, of the impossibility

     of super-identity.   The impossibility principle is epistemic and says that no subject can

     know everything. The fallacy principle is ontological in its reach: it says that the aggregate

     is the wrong kind of object. A subject can commit the fallacy with a small amount of data;

     and a subject can avoid it with a large one. The distinction between context and perspective

     (Section 5.5) shows where the fallacy lies: it is not in having a partial perspective, which

     is unavoidable, but in treating a concatenation across contexts as a single whole.



     Remark 4.15 (The Golden Record Illusion)

     The fallacy is closely related to what we may call the Golden Record Illusion: the belief

     that there is, or could be, a single record that contains all attributes of an entity across all

     contexts. The illusion is pervasive in contemporary data-driven culture, where the ideal

     is often to collect all the data and construct a complete model of the entity. The four-

     column framework treats this ideal as ill-posed. There is no Golden Record, not because

     we lack the resources to construct it, but because collecting attributes does not isolate an

     invariant, and an invariant is what an identity is. More data of the same kind makes the

     confounded record larger, not better.

The fallacy is not only a fault of gathering conicting information. Three things are separate:


      Aggregation : collecting manifestations;

      Compatibility : the absence of conict among them;

      Numerical unity : the oneness of the source identity.

Even an aggregate of manifestations that are wholly compatible with one another does not, by

being so, produce or restore the source. From aggregation and compatibility it does not follow

that the numerical unity of the source is reached. The source is not the sum or the consistent

sum of its appearances (Principle 4.3).


   Principle 4.3 (Non-Reduction of Identity to Manifestations)

   (T) An identity is not identical with the aggregate of its manifestations, nor with their
   compatible aggregate.



Argument.           (1) A manifestation is the product of the identity and a receiving realm, and is not

 (2) An aggregate of products is a plurality of products. A plurality of things is not, as such, a

4.6 Identity Rigidity and Conservation
   Principle 4.4 (Identity Rigidity)

   (A) Identity does not admit of degrees. A structure either is invariant under the specied
   transformations, or it is not.



Argument from the logic of identity. (1) Strict sameness is an equivalence relation: if a is the
     same as b and b is the same as c, then a is the same as c.


 (2) A relation that comes in degrees (more or less the same) is not transitive.           A chain of

         small dierences can connect two things that are very unlike. This is the structure of the

         sorites.


 (3) So a degree-based sameness is not strict sameness. It is resemblance, which is a matter

         of degree and has no tendency to settle whether something is the same one.


 (4) Hence identity, as strict sameness under the allowed class of transformations, admits no

         degrees. The apparent degrees of ordinary language (not himself today, no longer the

         same) are degrees of manifestation, or of resemblance between signatures, not of identity.
44                                                                               Chapter 4.   Identities




     Remark 4.16 (The Contrast with Properties)

     Rigidity must be distinguished from the degrees of properties. Properties admit of degrees:

     a room can be more or less warm, a person more or less tall. Identity is not a property;

     it is the condition of persistence itself, and as such it is not a matter of degree.       The

     framework's way of speaking of degrees of success belongs to viability (Denition 1.7),

     which is a property of the relation between identity and manifestation, not of identity.



     Remark 4.17 (Rigidity of identity is not rigidity of manifestation)

     Two propositions must be kept apart:


       (i)   Identity is invariant in itself. This belongs to the denition of identity.

      (ii)   Identity appears in the same way under all conditions and relations. This does not
             follow from (i).


     Hence the rigidity of identity does not mean the rigidity of its manifestations.         Mani-

     festations may dier widely while the identity does not change; this is the content of

     Chapter 18.



     Principle 4.5 (Identity Conservation)

     (A) Identity is invariant under all temporal transformations of its manifestations. Mani-
     festations may vary with time; identity itself is conserved.



     The principle is adopted, and two arguments support it. They are distinct, and they support

dierent things.


First argument: transcendental, to the presumption of sameness.           (1) We have experiences of

       temporal continuity: we remember the past, anticipate the future, and recognize objects

       and persons across time.


 (2) Such experiences are possible only if we presume that what is remembered, anticipated, or

       recognized is the same one across the interval.


 (3) Hence the presumption of sameness is a transcendental condition of temporal experience,

       and cannot be coherently denied by one who is describing such experience.




     Remark 4.18 (What the rst argument does not show)

     The conclusion is a statement about a presumption, and a presumption might be a useful

     construct rather than a fact. The rst argument therefore establishes less than the prin-

     ciple asserts. It does not, by itself, tell us that identity is really conserved. The gap is

     closed, to the extent that it can be closed, by the second argument.
4.7.   Non-Duplicability of Identity                                                              45



Second argument: abductive, to the reality of the invariant.    (1) Reconstructions of the same thing,

       carried out independently by observers in dierent contexts, converge on a single pseudo-

       identity up to isomorphism; the result is stable under perturbation; and it yields successful

       predictions (the three criteria of Section 4.4).


 (2) The best explanation of this convergence is that the observers are tracking something that

       is the same across the contexts and across time: an invariant.


 (3) The rival explanation, namely that the convergence reects shared convention or psycho-

       logical projection, predicts convergence only among observers who share the convention,

       and does not explain why predictions made from the pseudo-identity succeed in domains

       the convention never touched.


 (4) Hence it is reasonable to hold that there is an invariant that persists through time.




   Remark 4.19 (The Three Moments of Temporal Experience)

   The transcendental argument can be put in terms of three moments of temporal experience.

   In memory, I remember a past event as mine. In anticipation, I anticipate a future event

   as one that will happen to me. In recognition, I recognize an object or a person as the

   same one across time. The three are aspects of one structure, and all three presuppose

   the presumption of sameness. The abductive argument is what supports the further claim
   that the presumption is correct.




4.7 Non-Duplicability of Identity
   Principle 4.6 (Non-Duplicability)

   (A) Identity cannot be copied.       Signatures, pseudo-identities, and information can be

   duplicated; identity cannot.     A copy of a system reproduces its signature.    If the copy

   expresses at all, it expresses an identity of its own.



   Denition 4.6 (Duplication)

   Qualitative duplication produces a second thing that shares the rst thing's signature.
   Numerical duplication of an identity would produce two distinct things, each of which is
   that very identity.



Argument: the logic of numerical identity.      (1) Suppose I1 and I2 are numerically distinct, and

     each is identical to the identity I .


 (2) Identity is symmetric and transitive: from I1 = I and I2 = I it follows that I1 = I2 .


 (3) This contradicts the supposition that they are distinct. Hence numerical duplication of an

       identity is impossible.
46                                                                             Chapter 4.   Identities



 (4) What copying does produce is qualitative duplication. Since copies of a signature exist,

       and an identity cannot be copied, an identity is not the same as its signature. If it were,

       copying the signature would copy the identity.




     Remark 4.20 (Where the substance lies)

     Steps (1)(3) are a point of logic and carry little weight by themselves. The philosophical

     content is in step (4). It asserts that a thing's identity is more than its pattern, so that

     two things with exactly the same pattern are two, not one, and have two identities. This

     is the doctrine of   primitive thisness :   an individuating feature that is not qualitative,

     and that distinguishes one thing from an exact qualitative duplicate [Adams(1979)]. The

     framework adopts the doctrine as a commitment, (A), and states what it costs. It is a

     non-reductionist view of identity in Part's sense, because it holds that there is a further
     fact about identity over and above facts about continuity and similarity [Part(1984)].

     Consequently, in a case where the signature is perfectly copied, nothing observable settles

     which of the resulting systems, if any, carries the original identity.



     Remark 4.21 (Why the commitment is reasonable)

     The commitment is supported by three considerations. First, it explains why the rigidity

     of identity (Section 4.6) holds: strict sameness is not a matter of resemblance. Second, it

     is consistent with the best-known case against the identity of indiscernibles, Black's two

     exactly similar spheres [Black(1952)], in which two things share every qualitative feature

     and yet are two. Third, it explains why reconstruction can converge on a signature without

     ever delivering the identity: the signature is what can be read o, and the identity is what

     is enacted by a source. The cost is the one stated above: the identity-facts in duplication

     cases are not settled by observation.

Three claims are made in this section, and they have dierent statuses.


 (1)   Numerical duplication of an identity is impossible.       This is defended from the logic of

       identity (Section 4.7).


 (2)   Duplication of a signature is possible. This is accepted.

       and it is not derived from (1) or (2).

content, and the book says so openly instead of replacing it by an argument that would turn out

4.8 The Classical Puzzles of Personal Identity
The framework's account of rigidity, conservation, and non-duplicability bears on the classical

puzzles of personal identity in the following way.     It does not claim to resolve them from the

outside.   It claims to dissolve the metaphysical puzzle by relocating it: the question what is

identity?   receives a determinate answer, and the diculty returns as an epistemic question

about how identity can be known.



 (1)   Locke's memory criterion. Locke dened personal identity as continuity of conscious-
       ness and memory [Locke(1690)]. Butler objected that this is circular: memory presupposes

       personal identity, since to remember is to remember one's own past, and so memory cannot

       constitute it [Butler(1736)]. The framework agrees with the objection. Memory is a mani-

       festation, an expression of identity, and not what identity consists in. Reid pressed a further

       diculty, the case of the brave ocer: a boy is ogged; as an ocer he captures a standard

       and remembers the ogging; as a general he remembers capturing the standard but not the

       ogging [Reid(1785)]. By Locke's criterion the general is the ocer and the ocer is the

       boy, yet the general is not the boy, which violates transitivity. The framework's rigidity

       principle diagnoses the case: strict sameness is transitive, and memory-connectedness is a

       graded, non-transitive relation of manifestation, so the two cannot be the same relation.


 (2)   The gradual replacement puzzle. If the parts of a ship, or the cells of a body, are
       replaced over time, at what point does the object cease to be the same one? The framework

       answers that the identity is not the parts but the invariance under the allowed class of

       replacements. Dierent contexts x dierent classes. In a functional context the repaired

       ship is the same ship; in a material context the ship rebuilt from the original planks is.

       The puzzle arises from requiring one answer across contexts, which is a mild form of the

       very demand that the principle of super-identity rejects. The framework does not choose

       between the two answers; it says that each is correct in its own context and that none is

       correct in all.


 (3)   Fission and duplication. These are treated in Section 4.7. The framework's answer is
       stated there together with its price.
4.8.   The Classical Puzzles of Personal Identity                                                49




   Remark 4.26 (Four continuities that the puzzles confuse)

   The rival answers to the puzzles can be located by asking which of four things each treats

   as sucient for being the same one:


       (1)   continuity of structure (the invariant pattern persists);

       (2)   continuity of manifestation (the appearances follow one another smoothly);

       (3)   continuity of relation (the thing stands in the same relations to others);

       (4)   numerical unity of the identity (it is one and the same source).

   The memory criterion takes (2) for (4). The replacement puzzle contrasts (1) in dierent

   contexts. The brave-ocer case separates (2), which is not transitive, from (4), which is.

   A theory that treats one of the rst three as if it were the fourth has confused a level of

   identity with identity.



   Remark 4.27 (What is dissolved and what remains)

   What the framework removes is the metaphysical puzzle of what identity consists in. It

   does so by the doctrine of primitive thisness, which is a substantial commitment, and which

   is not shared by the reductionist views of Part and Lewis [Part(1984), Lewis(1976)] or

   by animalist views such as Olson's [Olson(1997)]. What remains is an epistemic puzzle:

   since observers deal only with signatures and pseudo-identities, there are cases in which

   no evidence can decide an identity-fact. The framework accepts this consequence openly.

   A reader who nds it too costly has located the place where the framework should be

   challenged.

     not.

     This criterion is internal: it uses only the dening invariants of the identity. The principle

     are bound by the nature of the source. What would refute it is a manifestation that is

     acknowledged to be of an identity and yet has a feature incompatible with an essential

     feature; the framework would then hold either that the thing is not a manifestation of

     that identity (misattribution, Remark 4.13) or that the principle fails.

     That manifestations dier in structure does not mean that the nature of the identity

   Remark 6.1 (The word light)

   The word is borrowed from the experience of seeing, in which something is made visible

   without being either the seer or the seen.    It is used in this book as a structural term.

   What is said of it is said by denition and argument, and nothing in the argument depends

   on what is said of light in any other domain of inquiry.




Argument: why the relation between identity and manifestation needs a medium.        (1) A mani-

     festation is not its identity, and it is not unrelated to it (Section 5.4).



 (2) A relation between two distinct things that is not mere coincidence needs an account of

     how one bears on the other. (This is the conveyor principle, Principle 1.5.)



 (3) The account cannot be given by the identity alone, since the identity is invariant and does

     not change by manifesting; nor by the manifestation alone, since the manifestation is the

     product, not the source.



 (4) So there must be something that conveys the eect of the one to the other, which is neither

     of the two.



 (5) That is what the book calls light.




   Remark 6.2 (Light is not another name for the relation)

   It might be said that light only names the relation between identity and manifestation,

   and so explains nothing. The reply is that the relation as such says that a manifestation

   is of an identity; light is what says how : that by which the eect passes and the relation

   holds as a carrying of the identity into the manifestation and back. A name for the relation

   answers whether; light answers how.

6.2 Light as the General Medium of Expression

   Principle 6.1 (Light as Conduit)

   In the network of cognition, light is the edge that connects nodes. Nodes are identities

   and manifestations; edges are the relations between them. Light is not a node. Without

   light the graph would be a set of disconnected points.




6.3 The Three Essential Properties of Light
Light has three essential properties: descending, ascending, and reective. They are not three

kinds or three modes of light. A kind or a mode would be a separate entity; an essential property

is an aspect of the same entity.



   Denition 6.1 (Descending Light)

   Descending light is the ow of eect from identity to manifestation: the generative light,
   in which expression has its occasion.




   Denition 6.2 (Ascending Light)

   Ascending light is the ow of eect from manifestation to identity: the light that returns
   a manifestation to its source.




   Denition 6.3 (Reective Light)

   Reective light is the ow within a realm, connecting manifestations to one another: the
   sustaining light.

     Remark 6.5 (Direction is not route)

     Two things are said when light is called descending: the direction of its eect (from the

6.4 Light as Process, Not Substance
Argument: light is a process, not a substance.      (1) Suppose light were a substance, a xed ele-

       ment among the elements it connects.


 (2) By the conveyor principle, how it bears on an identity and on a manifestation would need

       a further conveyor, and so on without end. This is a regress.


 (3) The regress is avoided only if light is not one more thing to be connected but the conveying

       itself.


 (4) A conveying is an occurring, a process.


 (5) Hence light is a process, not a substance.




     Remark 6.8 (A process is real)

     That light is a process does not mean that it lacks reality. A process is real as an occurring,

     with its own conditions and results.     What the argument denies is that light is a self-

     subsisting bearer of properties apart from the conveying that it is. This answers why light

     must not be pictured as an independent entity alongside what it conveys.

   Remark 6.9 (Three consequences)

       (1)   Light cannot be reied. It is not a thing alongside the things it connects.

   Denition 6.4 (Intra-Category Light)

   Intra-category light is light that has descended into a realm and has not ascended: eect

   It is not a fourth essential property; it is a state of descending light. Light that has reached

   a realm can ascend or remain. If it remains, it is intra-category light.




   Denition 6.5 (Sediment)

   Sediment is intra-category light that has become lodged : it persists beyond the activity
   that carried it, it is not integrated into the relations of the realm in which it remains, and

   it lays a lasting claim on that realm's capacity to receive.

   The two notions dier. If every unreturned light were sediment, the notion would be so

   of its return, or that has been integrated, is intra-category light but not sediment. The

   three marks of lodging (persistence, non-integration, lasting claim on capacity) separate

   sediment from the wider class. The management of sediment (Section 26.7) is then the

     Principle 6.2 (Reection)

   The rules of a context are not imposed from without; they are the structural constraints that

govern the mode of being of that context. Each context has its own rules, because each context

has its own mode of multiplicity. The rules of the Structural Determination are dierent from

the rules of the Relational Manifestation, and both are dierent from the rules of the Material

Manifestation. The First Determination has its own rules, but they are the rules of pure unity,

not of multiplicity.

   The chapter has four parts. First, it develops the rules of the First Determination. Second, it

develops the rules of the Structural Determination. Third, it develops the rules of the Relational

Manifestation. Fourth, it develops the rules of the Material Manifestation.




7.2 The Rules of the First Determination
The rules of the First Determination are the structural constraints that govern its mode of being.

They are four.


   Principle 7.1 (Invariance)

   (A) The First Determination is invariant under all transformations. There are no trans-
   formations within it, because there is no multiplicity; but even if we consider the transfor-

   mations of its manifestations in the lower contexts, the First Determination is not aected

   by them. It is invariant in the most radical sense.



   Principle 7.2 (Atemporality)

   (A) The First Determination is beyond time. There is no before or after, no past or future,
   no succession. The First Determination simply is.



   Principle 7.3 (Indivisibility)

   (A) The First Determination is beyond division. There are no parts within it, no compo-
   nents, no distinctions. The First Determination is one.




                                                66
7.3.   The Rules of the Structural Determination                                                   67




   Principle 7.4 (Unity)

   (A) The First Determination is pure unity. It is not a unity of parts (a composite unity),
   but a unity that precedes all division. It is the unity that makes division possible, without

   being divided itself.




   Remark 7.1 (The Articulation of the Four Rules)

   These four rules are not separate; they are four aspects of a single structural constraint.

   The First Determination is invariant because it is atemporal, atemporal because it is

   indivisible, indivisible because it is one. The four rules are the articulation of the First

   Determination's purity.




7.3 The Rules of the Structural Determination
The rules of the Structural Determination are the structural constraints that govern its mode of

being. They are three.



   Principle 7.5 (Universality)

   (A) The structural laws are universal. They apply to all manifestations of a given identity,
   regardless of context. A law that applies only in some contexts is not a structural law; it

   is a relational regularity.




   Principle 7.6 (Necessity)

   (A) The structural laws are necessary. They could not be otherwise, given the identity
   they express.   A law that could be otherwise is not a structural law; it is a contingent

   regularity.




   Principle 7.7 (Formality)

   (A) The structural laws are formal. They concern the form of the manifestations, not their
   content. The law of non-contradiction concerns the form of arguments, not their subject

   matter. The law of gravity concerns the form of trajectories, not the nature of the bodies.

   A law that concerns content is not a structural law; it is a material law.




   Remark 7.2 (The Articulation of the Three Rules)

   These three rules are not separate; they are three aspects of a single structural constraint.

   The Structural Determination is universal because it is necessary, necessary because it is

   formal. The three rules are the articulation of the context's nature.

The rules of the Relational Manifestation are the structural constraints that govern its mode of

     Principle 7.8 (Relationality)

     relation to one another; they have no independent existence. A manifestation that is not

     related to any other is not a manifestation of the Relational Manifestation; it is a mere

     depends on their context; it is not xed independently of the context. A manifestation

     (A) The manifestations of the Relational Manifestation are aware. They are not merely

     the Relational Manifestation from the Structural Determination: the laws of the Structural

     Remark 7.3 (The Articulation of the Three Rules)

     These three rules are not separate; they are three aspects of a single structural constraint.

       contextualization of the rules of the Structural Determination. When universal laws are
       contextualized into meanings, universality becomes relationality, necessity becomes con-

       textuality, formality becomes awareness.



 (4) The rules of the Material Manifestation (causality, temporality, discreteness) are the re-

       alization of the rules of the Relational Manifestation. When meanings are realized into
       matter, relationality becomes causality, contextuality becomes temporality, awareness be-

       comes discreteness.

   (A) The specication of unity into multiplicity must be ordered. The specication must
   follow the structure of the contextsthe higher before the lower, the general before the

   particular. A disorderly specication (confusing the higher and the lower) is a violation

   of the rule of order.



   Principle 8.3 (Harmony)

   (A) The specication of unity into multiplicity must be harmonious. The specication
   must produce a multiplicity that is coherent, not chaotic. A disharmonious specication

   (multiplication without coherence) is a violation of the rule of harmony.

The rules of the Hub of Signication are the structural constraints that govern its mode of being.

9.1 The Central Claim
   Principle 9.1 (Expression as Structural Necessity)

   (A) Every identity, by its very nature, tends toward expression. Expression is not a con-
   tingent choice but a structural necessity. Expression necessarily involves three moments:

   manifestational descent (the identity appears in another realm), plurality (the identity can

   appear in several manifestations), and ontological novelty (each manifestation is a reality

   of its own in the realm where it is realized).      These three moments are not temporally

   successive but structurally inseparable.



   The principle makes three claims:

 (1)   Expression is intrinsic. Identity is not something that can express itself or not; identity
       is its expression. To be an identity is to be in the act of expressing oneselfacross contexts,

       through light, in manifestation.


 (2)   Expression is necessary as a tendency.            Expression is not a possibility that some

       identities have and others lack; every identity tends toward expression. That the tendency is

       necessary does not make the success of any particular manifestation necessary (Section 9.6).

       The necessity is not a contingent fact about the world; it is a structural feature of identity

       itself.


 (3)   Expression is structural. Expression is neither imposed from without nor motivated
       from within; it is the structure of the relation between identity and its manifestations. It

       is not a choice that the identity makes; it is the form of the identity's being.




9.2 The Argument from Intelligibility
Proof: From Intelligibility.   (1) An identity is a structure that persists through transformation.


 (2) To persist through transformation, an identity must be expressed in its transformationsit

       must be manifested in some form.


 (3) An identity that did not express itself would not persist through transformation; it would

       have no transformations, and therefore no persistence.


 (4)   Conclusion: expression is a condition of the very concept of identity.

10.1 The Argument from Qualitative Grades of Multiplicity
   Denition 10.1 (Grade of Multiplicity)

   A grade of multiplicity is a qualitatively distinct way in which many things can stand to

   one another, as opposed to a merely quantitative dierence of how many there are.



Argument: four grades are the minimum. Premises:

P1. Expression is the appearance of unity in multiplicity (Principle 9.1).

P2. Multiplicity has qualitative grades, not only quantitative ones. (A)

P3. The qualitative grades that expression requires are: no multiplicity (pure unity); multiplic-
     ity of form (form without content); multiplicity of relation (relation with content, without

     extension); and multiplicity of matter (content with location and succession). (A)


Argument:


 (1) Suppose two adjacent grades were not distinguished.


 (2) Merging no-multiplicity and form would remove the distinction between there being nothing

     plural and there being at least a minimal plurality of laws.


 (3) Merging form and relation would remove the distinction between the general and the con-

     textual: form is general, relation is contextual.


 (4) Merging relation and matter would remove the distinction between what has meaning and

     what has location: relation is not extended, matter is.


 (5) Each merger removes a distinction that expression needs. Hence the four grades are needed.


The argument is as strong as P2 and P3. It is relative to them; they are commitments and are

The Non-Duplicability of Identity

Chapter 4 stated the non-duplicability principle (Principle 4.6) and gave its core argument. This

chapter does not repeat that argument. It does three things that Chapter 4 did not: it shows that

the principle holds on either side of the dispute over the identity of indiscernibles; it separates

the principle from qualitative similarity; and it answers the main objections. The vocabulary is

that of Denition 4.4: the signature of an identity is the structural description of its invariance,

and it can be copied; the identity is the invariance enacted by one source.




14.1 A Reminder of the Direct Argument
The direct argument is in Section 4.7. Its two parts, recalled in one sentence each: duplication

read numerically (two distinct things, each being the identity) is excluded by the logic of identity;

duplication read qualitatively (a second thing with the same signature) is possible, and is a

duplication of signature unless identity is nothing over and above signature. Everything below

presupposes only this.




14.2 The Argument from the Identity of Indiscernibles
The principle of the identity of indiscernibles (PII) says that two things sharing all their quali-

tative features are one thing. It is contested; Black argued against it with a world of two exactly

similar spheres [Black(1952)]. The principle of non-duplicability does not depend on how this

dispute comes out.



Proof: From the Identity of Indiscernibles.     (1)   First horn: PII holds. Then two things cannot
       share all qualitative features. A copy of a signature therefore diers from the original in

       some respect (at least in its relations or position), so it is a second thing with its own

       features and not a second instance of the same thing.


 (2)   Second horn: PII fails. Then there can be two things that share every qualitative feature,
       as in Black's spheres. They are two, and so have two identities (Section 14.1). An exact

       qualitative duplicate is still not a numerical duplicate.


 (3) In both horns, copying does not yield a second thing that is the original identity.


 (4)   Conclusion: identity cannot be duplicated, whether or not PII is true.

96                                                     Chapter 14.   The Non-Duplicability of Identity




     Remark 14.1 (What this does and does not show)

     The dilemma removes the dependence of the principle on PII. What it leaves is a com-

     is (A), and is defended in Chapter 4. A philosophical commitment such as PII is not used

     here as a premise from which the principle is derived.

     (T) The principle of non-duplicability is independent of the degree of qualitative similarity

 (2) Two manifestations may have similar or identical forms.

19.1 The Two Concepts
Unity : an identity is one. Multiplicity : its manifestations are many. The ancient problem of the

19.2 The Source of the Apparent Conict
The conict arises if the multiplicity of manifestations is taken to be a multiplicity of identities,

or if the unity of an identity is taken to be the sum of its manifestations. In either case the one

is turned into the many, or the many into the one.




19.3 Is the Conict Real?
No. Two multiplicities are run together: the plurality of manifestations of one identity, and the

plurality of identities. And two kinds of unity: the numerical unity of a source, and the unity of
an aggregate (Section 4.5.1).




19.4 The Resolving Principle: Recursive Manifestation
   Denition 19.1 (Recursive Manifestation)

   Recursive manifestation is the structure by which a manifestation that is realized in one
   realm can itself be the occasion for further manifestations of the same identity, in the same

   realm or another, without the identity being multiplied.




   Remark 19.1 (Realms are not manifestations of one another)

   An earlier formulation said that each level of the architecture is a manifestation of the level

   above. That formulation is withdrawn. Each realm is independently real (Principle 2.1);

   what recurs is the manifestation of an identity, not the realm itself.

114                                                           Chapter 19.   Unity and Multiplicity




   Principle 19.1 (Distinction of Plurality of Manifestations from Plurality of
                    Identities)

   (T) The plurality of the manifestations of one identity is not a plurality of identities, and
   recursive manifestation is not numerical multiplication of the identity.




19.5 The Argument
Resolution of the apparent conict.    (1) A manifestation is a product of a source identity and

      a receiving realm, and is not an identity (Section 5.3).



 (2) So many manifestations of one source are many products of one source; the number of

      products does not x the number of sources.



 (3) A manifestation may itself be the occasion of further manifestations; each further one is

      again a product of the same source and a receiving realm.



 (4) Hence the manifestations may be many, and recursively so, while the identity remains one.



 (5) The unity of the identity is therefore not the unity of an aggregate and not the unity of a

      thing underlying its manifestations; it is the numerical unity of a source.




   Remark 19.2 (An illustration)

   One author is expressed in many works, each of which is something in its own right, and

   some of which are occasions for others (a commentary on a work); and still the author is

   one. The illustration is not an argument.

It is valid given that manifestations are products and not identities ((A)). It does not say that

the unity is of a pattern rather than a thing for every reading of pattern; it says that the unity

of the source is not got by counting manifestations. The reality of the many is armed: the book

holds a stratied realism, in which both the one and the many are real in their own respects.

tions, as such, countable as identities. It would also fail if the commitment that identity is more

than signature were abandoned in a way that made the unity of a source identical with the unity

of a pattern.

    The intensity of a manifestation is the degree to which it conrms the source as a whole,

    in its unity and purity (the completeness of reection, Denition 6.6). Its breadth is the

    extent to which the implicit articulation of the source is made explicit in diverse features.



    Principle 20.1 (Structural Duality of Expression)

    (A) Every manifestation stands at once in a relation of intensity and of breadth to its
    source. These are two respects of one process. A manifestation may have less intensity

    and more breadth than the source in its own realm.




20.5 The Argument
Resolution of the apparent conict.      (1) In its own realm an identity is not articulated into

       diverse features, or is so only in the way that the realm allows.       In a realm of greater

       multiplicity its implicit articulation is made explicit.

 (2) The receiving realm limits what it can bear of the source's unity and purity (Denition 6.7).

       So the manifestation conrms less of the source as a whole.


 (3) The rst is a gain of breadth; the second is a loss of intensity. They are dierent measures.


 (4) Hence the apparent conict is resolved by distinguishing them.




   Remark 20.1 (What exactly is lost, and what added)

   Four questions, answered.


       (1)   What is lost? Intensity of conrmation: the fullness with which the manifestation

       (2)   What is added?     Articulation: diverse features through which the source is made

Tendency and Success


21.1 The Two Concepts
Tendency : every identity tends to express itself, necessarily (Denition 9.1). Success : expression
may succeed more or less (Denition 9.2).




21.2 The Source of the Apparent Conict
If expression is necessary it seems that it cannot fail; and if identity is rigid, it seems that

expression cannot come in degrees.




21.3 Is the Conict Real?
No. It comes from running together the necessity of a tendency with the necessity of an outcome,

and a rigid identity with a graded expression.




21.4 The Resolving Principle
   Principle 21.1 (Tendency Is Not Success)

   (T) The necessity of the tendency to express does not entail the necessity of the success
   of any manifestation, nor the necessity that a manifestation be realized in every realm.




21.5 The Argument
Resolution of the apparent conict.    (1) A tendency is a direction of what an identity is; a

      success is a result of what a tendency meets in a receiving realm. These are dierent kinds

      of thing.


 (2) A tendency that is necessary in the rst sense can meet a realm of low capacity, or be

      hindered; the result is then less than full success, or none.


 (3) Identity is rigid; the expression it tends to is graded in its delity. The grading belongs to

      the relation of identity to manifestation (Denition 1.7), not to the identity.


 (4) Hence the necessity of the tendency and the possibility of failure are compatible, and so

      are rigid identity and graded expression.

120                                                            Chapter 21.   Tendency and Success




   Remark 21.1 (Agreement with Chapter 9)

   This is the same distinction as in Section 9.6. Neither chapter says more than that. A

   tendency that is necessary is not thereby a success that is guaranteed.




21.6 The Scope of the Argument
It is valid for tendencies in the sense dened, and for success as delity. It does not say how

likely success is.




21.7 What Would Challenge the Resolution
It would be in question if the necessity of a tendency could be shown to carry with it the success

21.8 Consequences
 (1)   The possibility of failure. An identity can fail to express itself well; its tendency can

 (2)   The reality of success. Expression can succeed; success is a component of viability.

 (3)   Hindrance and poor success dier. A tendency may be hindered so that no mani-

Relation and Order


22.1 The Two Concepts
Relation : the barzakhic realms mediate, transform, and regulate relations between other realms.
Order : they are levels of being with rules and dynamics of their own.


22.2 The Source of the Apparent Conict
It seems that something cannot both be a relation between levels and be a level.




22.3 Is the Conict Real?
No. It comes from running together function and mode of existence. A thing may exist in its

own right (a level) and also perform a function for others (mediation).




22.4 The Resolving Principle
   Principle 22.1 (Function Is Not Dependence)

   (T) That a realm has the function of mediating between other realms does not entail
   that it lacks independent existence.    Given the independence of the barzakhic realms

   (Principle 3.1), the mediating function is a part of its mode of existence and not the whole

   of it.




22.5 The Argument
Resolution of the apparent conict.    (1) In the   structural analysis, a barzakhic realm is de-
      scribed by the relations it regulates among other realms: it is what it does for them.


 (2) In the existential analysis, it is described by what it is and what can occur in it: a level

      with its own rules and activities.


 (3) The two descriptions concern dierent questions about one realm, and neither excludes the

      other.


 (4) Hence the barzakhic realms are both mediating and independent.

122                                                              Chapter 22.   Relation and Order




   Remark 22.1 (The Two Analyses)

   The structural analysis describes the architecture: the realms and what relations they

   bear.    The existential analysis describes what inhabits the realms and what occurs in

   them. They are not independent of one another, and neither reduces to the other.




22.6 The Scope of the Argument
It is valid given the independence principle, which is a commitment ((A)) argued in Chapter 3

and Chapter 11. It resolves the conict stated; it does not itself prove independence.




22.7 What Would Challenge the Resolution
It would be in question if it were shown that whatever has a mediating function is by that fact

dependent for its existence on what it mediates between, or if the rules of a barzakhic realm were

derivable from those of its neighbors (Remark 11.3).

 (1)    The reality of the barzakhic realms. They are real as levels, and as mediators.

 (2)    The possibility of inhabitation and interaction. It follows from the independence of
        the barzakhic realms that they can be occupied and acted in as other realms can. This is

 (3)    The unity of the framework. The structural and the existential analysis are comple-

24.1 The Concept of Death
Chapters 23 and 26 have set out time and the cost of expression. This chapter asks what happens

when expression in a realm ceases. Is death the destruction of identity, or a cessation of a certain

kind?



   Principle 24.1 (Death as the Zeroing of Manifestation Functions)

   (A) Death, in a realm, is the zeroing of the output of an identity's manifestation functions
   in that realm. It is not the destruction of the identity.




   Remark 24.1 (Three questions answered)

     (1)   Is death the death of a manifestation or of the identity? It is the ceasing of mani-
           festation in a realm. The identity is conserved (Chapter 13).


     (2)   Does the end of manifestation in one realm end the possibility of manifestation in
           others? Not by itself. The possibility of manifestation in another realm is a separate
           matter, governed by that realm's capacity and the identity's tendency (Principle 2.3).


     (3)   Is death in one realm consistent with the identity's continuing in another? Yes. This
           is what conservation, together with the distinction of the persistence of identity from

           the continuity of manifestation, requires.


   The ascending light associated with the ceased manifestations ceases; reective light, which

   connects other manifestations, continues (Denition 24.2).




24.2 The Singularity of Ontological Death

   Denition 24.1 (The Singularity of Ontological Death)

   The singularity is the threshold at which the cost of restoring an existing manifestation in a

   realm exceeds the cost of bringing about a new one there (cost in the sense of Section 26.1).

   Remark 24.2 (Not a line at the level of identity)

   Identity admits no degrees. At the level of manifestation the approach to the singularity is

   a gradient: output declines and the cost of restoration rises. The singularity is a threshold

   of manifestation, not of identity.



   Remark 24.3 (Status)

   The singularity is (A) as a structural postulate that follows from the economy of expression

   and the denition of death. What the exact comparison of costs would be, for a given case,

   is a conceptual question the framework leaves open ((Q)). The singularity is a regulative

   notion.




24.3 Kinds of Death
Death is said of a realm, and so there are as many kinds as there are realms and manifestations

in them. Four are distinguished.


 (1)   Death in the Material Manifestation: the output of material manifestation functions
       has gone to zero.


 (2)   Death of the vital manifestation: the vital forms of an identity's material manifestation
       have ceased.


 (3)   Loss of the self-model: the pseudo-identity that the subject holds of itself has ceased to
       be produced. This is a death in the Relational Manifestation.


 (4)   Death of all manifestation: the manifestation functions of an identity in every realm
       have ceased. Even this is not the destruction of the identity.


   Remark 24.4 (Overlap)

   They can overlap in various ways. One may be dead in one realm and manifest in another.

   Whether a case is a case of the fourth kind is not usually decidable by observation.

          simply additive; how they trade against one another in a given case is not xed in

   delity: a thinner manifestation is cheaper and a poorer reection of the source. Viability

   (Denition 1.7) joins delity, sustainable cost, and recoverability.   Cost and delity are

   therefore taken together: what is sought is the least cost consistent with sucient delity.



   Remark 26.3 (No single best expression)

   The best expression depends on the receiving realm, its capacity, and the identity. The

   principle prescribes a family of optima, each indexed to a context.




26.4 Aspects of Cost
Three aspects of the demand on capacity may be named: its extent (how much of the realm's

capacity is taken), its reach (how much cognition and attention it takes to hold it), and its

duration (how long it must be kept up). They are constitutive aspects of one notion, a demand
on capacity, and not an arbitrary list. Their exact relation is an open question ((Q)).


   Remark 26.4 (The lived aspect)

   The demand on capacity is also lived, as fatigue, depletion of attention, and the sense of

   being overwhelmed. This is what makes the economy a matter of existential concern.




26.5 Sediment
   Denition 26.1 (Sediment)

   Sediment is intra-category light that has become lodged (Denition 6.5): light that has
   descended and not ascended, persists beyond the activity that carried it, is not integrated

   into the relations of its realm, and lays a lasting claim on that realm's capacity.



   Remark 26.5 (The criterion of sediment)

   What is unreturned is not for that reason sediment. Light on its way back, or integrated,

   is not.   The criterion is lodging:   persistence, non-integration, and a lasting claim on

   capacity. Sediment is thus a species of intra-category light. The denition unies the sorts

If sediment threatens viability, managing it is an existential task. Three activities are commonly

   The task is never nished: sediment accumulates. The viable subject works with it con-

36.1 Kashmir Shaivism

 (2)    The unity of the source. Kashmir Shaivism treats Shiva as the single source of all

 (3)    The dynamic nature of reality. Kashmir Shaivism treats the world as a play of Shiva's
        creative power. The framework treats reality as a continuous expression of identity through

        manifestation. Both accounts recognize that reality is dynamic.




36.3 Points of Departure
The framework departs from Kashmir Shaivism in one crucial respect: for Kashmir Shaivism, the

world is a play (lila ) of Shiva. For the framework, the world is a structural expression of identity.

The framework's expression is not a play; it is a structural necessity. The two accounts agree

that reality is dynamic; they disagree about the nature of the dynamism (play vs. structure).




36.4 The Framework's Assessment
The framework's relation to Kashmir Shaivism can be summarized as follows: it shares with

Kashmir Shaivism the reality of multiplicity, the unity of the source, and the dynamic nature of

play.

Conclusion: The Horizon of Flourishing


38.1 The Unnished Conversation
Philosophy has always been a conversation, often a fractured one. This book oers a language for

part of it. Its architecture proceeds from the four-column framework of cognition, to the seven

realms, to identities and manifestations, to light, to the rules of the realms, to the arguments,

to the resolution of tensions, to derivative concepts, to comparison with traditions, and to the

open horizons.




38.2 The Central Question
The central question is not What is identity?      but How does an identity express itself in

manifestations, in many realms, while remaining one?      The classical paradoxes of identity are

dissolved, not solved, by showing where levels have been run together.




38.3 Three Conclusions
 (1)   Identity and manifestation are two distinct levels. The manifestation is not the
       identity and not unrelated to it; it is a real appearance in its own realm, homogeneous in

       essence with its source.


 (2)   The plurality of manifestations is compatible with the unity of identity, and
       that unity is not the aggregate of appearances. Aggregating manifestations, even
       compatible ones, does not reach the numerical unity of the source.


 (3)   Viability is the quality of the realization and continuance of an identity's ex-
       pression in the realms, and not a criterion for counting identities or xing their
       nature. It joins delity, bearable cost, and recoverability, and admits of degrees, where
       identity does not.




38.4 The Horizon of Flourishing
The philosophy of viability is not a philosophy of mere survival. The viable identity is not merely

one whose manifestations persist; it is one that expresses itself faithfully, at a cost that can be

borne, and without losing the capacity to express again.
