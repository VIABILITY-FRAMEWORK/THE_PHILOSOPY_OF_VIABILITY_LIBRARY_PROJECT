      Observable Restriction, Identification, and the Two Sources of

                                             Abstract
         Every scientific model, simulation, digital twin, measurement, map, machine
     learning model, CAD model, and BIM model is fundamentally a representation of
     reality, and every such representation loses information relative to the system it
     represents. This paper develops a mathematical framework, grounded in the For-
     mal Theory of Manifestation (FTM), for describing exactly how that loss occurs.
     We give a formal definition of a deterministic representation and show that every
     such representation loses information through two distinct mechanisms: Observ-
     able Restriction (the choice of which observables to retain) and Identification
     (the merging of distinguishable states via a quotient), and that these two mech-
     anisms are not interchangeable in order: restriction is necessarily the first stage
     whenever the identification in question represents observational indistinguishabil-
     ity. This paper does not claim novelty for the underlying mathematical theorems:
     the minimality of a generated σ-algebra, the categorical first isomorphism theo-
     rem in exact categories, and the Shannon entropy chain rule are all classical. The
     novelty lies in recognizing that, read simultaneously as statements about represen-
     tation, these results jointly force a structural decomposition that, to our knowledge,
     has not previously been identified as such: every deterministic representation fac-
     tors canonically and uniquely as Observable Restriction followed by Identification
     (Theorems 8.2, 7.2, 9.1), and this decomposition admits a domain-independent
     quantitative language, the Restriction Index (RI) and Identification Index (II), ap-
     plicable uniformly across CAD, BIM, GIS, robotics, medical imaging, knowledge
     graphs, computer vision, and machine learning. We situate the framework against
     sufficient-statistics theory, rate-distortion theory, and the information bottleneck
     method, give explicit axioms for the effective-dimension functions underlying RI
     and II, and illustrate the framework with a single running example (a digital cam-
     era’s image pipeline) alongside eight domain-specific worked examples in uniform
     format.

     Keywords: Formal Theory of Manifestation, Representation Theory, Observable
     Restriction, Identification, Digital Twin, Sufficient Statistics, Rate-Distortion The-
     ory, Information Bottleneck, Quotient Spaces, Information Loss.

1     Introduction
Every scientific model, simulation, digital twin, measurement, map, machine learning
model, CAD model, BIM model, and knowledge graph is, at bottom, a representation
of reality: a finite, structured stand-in for a system too large, too continuous, or too
inaccessible to be manipulated directly. Every such representation is necessarily lossy.
Yet despite the ubiquity of representation as a scientific and engineering activity, there
has been no general mathematical language for describing how a representation loses
information, or for comparing the loss incurred by representations built by entirely differ-
ent disciplines. A simplified terrain mesh, a discretized structural model, and a trained
image classifier all discard information, but no shared vocabulary has existed for saying
precisely what kind of information each discards, or how much.
    One instance of this general problem, and the one that originally motivated this
work, is the digital twin. The concept has gained significant traction in engineering,
urban planning, and industrial systems. Despite its widespread use, it lacks a rigorous
formal definition of what constitutes a complete digital twin. The Formal Theory of
Manifestation (FTM) [1] supplies part of the needed foundation. It defines a digital twin
as a local section of a manifestation sheaf F over a context site (C, J):

                               Digital Twin Tk = sk ∈ F(Uk ),

where Uk ⊆ C is the context of the twin. FTM, however, is a theory of what reality and
its manifestations are. It is not a theory of how a specific finite representation of that
reality is built, nor of how it loses fidelity in the process. That is the gap this paper fills.
The resulting theory applies far beyond digital twins, to any representation of a physical
or informational system whatsoever.

Why this could not simply have been written twenty years ago. The individ-
ual mathematical ingredients used here — the minimality of a generated σ-algebra, the
first isomorphism theorem for exact categories, and the Shannon entropy chain rule —
have each been available for decades, in some cases nearly a century. The novelty of this
work does not lie in any individual theorem employed, but in identifying a structural
decomposition that becomes visible only when these classical results are interpreted si-
multaneously as statements about representation. To our knowledge, no previous work
has recognized Observable Restriction and Identification as the two universal and or-
dered mechanisms underlying deterministic representation across scientific domains, nor
derived their canonical ordering from the minimality property of Theorem 7.2. Sufficient
statistics theory addresses a closely related but distinct question — see Section 6 — and
rate-distortion theory and the information bottleneck optimize a restriction rather than
deriving one canonically from an already-given map. It is the combination, not any single
component, that is new.

What is new here, precisely. This work does not claim novelty for the underlying
mathematical theorems themselves. It introduces (i) a formal definition of representa-
tion (Definition 2.1) and a conceptual decomposition of representation into Observable
Restriction and Identification; (ii) the demonstration that classical results jointly imply
this decomposition and force a specific ordering between its two stages; and (iii) a uni-
fied, dimension-free, cross-domain language — the indices RI and II — for reasoning

about representation loss. Section 6 makes explicit, result by result, which parts of the
framework are classical, which are reinterpretations, and which are new.

1.1     Relation to the Formal Theory of Manifestation
FTM and the present theory operate at different layers and answer different questions.
    FTM asks: what is reality, and what does it mean for something to man-
ifest? It supplies the sheaf-theoretic machinery (F, contexts Uk , local sections sk ) by
which a digital twin is defined as a legitimate manifestation of a system within a context.
    This paper asks: given that a representation exists, how does it lose in-
formation relative to what it represents? It takes the existence of a representation
ϕ : Γ → D as given, whether or not that representation arises as an FTM manifestation,
and analyzes the internal structure of the loss ϕ incurs.
    Every FTM manifestation is, in particular, a representation in the sense of this paper,
and so is subject to the Observable Restriction / Identification decomposition. The
converse is not asserted: a representation need not be phrased in FTM’s sheaf-theoretic
vocabulary for the results here to apply. This is why the framework extends past digital
twins to CAD, GIS, robotics, medical imaging, and machine learning.


2     Representation, Formalized
Definition 2.1 (Deterministic Representation). Let Γ be an object of a concrete category
C, modeling a physical or informational system (“reality”). A deterministic repre-
sentation of Γ is a morphism
                                     ϕ:Γ→D
in C, where D is intended to retain only selected observable information about Γ. We
say ϕ is deterministic because it is a genuine morphism of C (a function, in the concrete
setting) rather than a stochastic kernel; the stochastic case, where ϕ is replaced by a
Markov kernel Γ ⇝ D, is discussed as a future direction in Section 14.
    All subsequent theorems in this paper are theorems about the object ϕ of Definition 2.1:
Theorem 8.2 concerns its factorization in an exact category, Theorem 7.2 concerns the
canonical σ-algebra it induces when C = Meas, and Theorem 9.1 concerns the entropy
loss it incurs when Γ additionally carries a probability measure.

Principle 2.1 (Two Sources of Information Loss). Every deterministic representation
ϕ : Γ → D of an open physical system loses information through two distinct mechanisms:

    1. Observable Restriction: the choice of which observables, degrees of freedom, or
       measurement contexts to retain from reality.

    2. Identification: the merging of distinguishable states within the retained observ-
       ables, i.e., passing to a quotient by an equivalence relation.

These mechanisms are separable in the following precise sense: for a fixed restricted space
Γ′ , the identification loss depends only on the quotient map Γ′ ↠ D, not on how Γ′ was
obtained from Γ; conversely, the restriction loss depends only on the map Γ → Γ′ , not
on any subsequent identification. We use separable rather than independent to avoid
suggesting a probabilistic-independence claim; see the remark following Proposition 9.2.

2.1    A Running Example: The Digital Camera Pipeline
To keep the abstract machinery grounded, we use a single running example throughout
the paper, revisited after each major definition and theorem.
Example 2.2 (Running Example: Digital Camera Pipeline). Let Γ = {0, . . . , 255}n×m×3
be the space of n × m RGB images, one byte per color channel per pixel, so dimeff (Γ) =
24nm bits. The camera’s on-device pipeline produces, in sequence:
                            grayscale conversion       thresholding
           RGB image Γ −−−−−−−−−−−→ ΓJ −−−−−−→ D (binary image).

Grayscale conversion retains, per pixel, a single fixed linear combination of the three
channels (an observable restriction: it discards two of the three retained bytes of infor-
mation per pixel, keeping only luminance). Thresholding at a fixed level then merges all
grayscale values above the threshold into one symbol and all values below into another (an
identification: it merges the 256 distinguishable gray levels per pixel into 2).

Remark 3.2 (What Counts as an Observable Restriction). Definition 3.1 is deliberately
abstract so that it captures, as special cases, activities that different disciplines treat as
unrelated:
    • Sensing: a physical sensor fi measuring one physical quantity (temperature, strain,
      position) is an observable in O; the set of installed sensors defines J.

    • Measurement: any experimental protocol reporting a value for some quantity,
      silent about all others, is a restriction to the reported quantities.

    • Abstraction: replacing a detailed object with a simplified description (bounding
      box, centroid, mass) is a restriction to those derived observables.

   • Dimensional reduction: PCA, autoencoding, or any projection to a lower-dimensional
     coordinate system is a restriction to the retained components.

   • Feature extraction: a neural network’s hidden layer, or a hand-designed feature
     vector, defines a family of learned or engineered observables.

   • Context selection: choosing which subsystem, time window, or spatial region Uk
     to model is itself a restriction, prior to any individual observable chosen within it.

Recognizing these as instances of the same construction is what lets RI be computed
identically whether the observable is a physical sensor or a learned neural feature.

3.1    Effective Dimension
Definition 3.3 (Axioms for Effective Dimension). A function dimeff assigning to each
object X of C a value in [0, ∞] is an effective dimension if it satisfies:

(D1) Invariance: if X ∼
                      = Y , then dimeff (X) = dimeff (Y ).
(D2) Monotonicity: if X is a subobject of Y (a regular monomorphism X ,→ Y ),
     then dimeff (X) ≤ dimeff (Y ); dually, if Z is a quotient of Y (a regular epimorphism
     Y ↠ Z), then dimeff (Z) ≤ dimeff (Y ).

(D3) Additivity: dimeff (X × Y ) = dimeff (X) + dimeff (Y ).

Definition 3.4 (Effective Dimension: Instances). For an object X in C, dimeff (X) is
defined contextually:

   • For a finite-dimensional vector space, dimeff (X) = dim(X).

   • For a finite set, dimeff (X) = log2 |X| (Hartley entropy).

   • For a manifold, dimeff (X) = dimmanifold (X).

   • For a measurable space with probability measure µ, dimeff (X) = H(X, µ) (Shannon
     entropy).

Remark 3.5. Each instance in Definition 3.4 satisfies (D1)–(D3). Vector-space dimen-
sion and manifold dimension satisfy all three by standard linear algebra and topology (a
submanifold has dimension ≤ that of the ambient manifold; products add dimensions).
Hartley entropy log2 |X| satisfies (D2) because an injection into Y forces |X| ≤ |Y | and
a surjection from Y forces |Z| ≤ |Y |, and (D3) because |X × Y | = |X||Y |. Shannon
entropy satisfies (D2) because conditioning (restricting to a sub-σ-algebra or coarsening
a partition) cannot increase entropy, and (D3) is the standard entropy-of-independent-
product identity. This is why the same symbol dimeff and the same formulas for RI, II
can be reused unchanged across Definitions 3.1–3.4’s four settings.

(D1)–(D3) can be assigned, measuring exactly one of the two orthogonal sources of loss

Remark 4.4 (Scope of the Non-Uniqueness of Observable Families). Different observable
families may generate the same σ-algebra, and Theorem 7.2 shows that only the gener-
ated σ-algebra σ(ϕ), not the particular generating family {fi }, is canonical. The theory
therefore identifies observable structures only up to their generated σ-algebra. This mir-
rors standard practice in probability theory, where a random variable’s distribution and
conditional structure depend only on the σ-algebra it generates, not on the specific func-
tions used to generate it, and it avoids making RI or II depend on an arbitrary choice of
generating family.

5.1    Why Restriction Must Come First
The correct pipeline is

Γ −→ choose what can be observed −→ Γ′ −→ merge distinguishable states −→ D,

not the reverse. To “merge states” before any observable is chosen presupposes an equiv-
alence relation on Γ itself, and such a relation can only be specified in terms of some
observable structure: two states are declared equivalent because they agree on some set
of retained observables, or because some retained measurement cannot distinguish them.
Definitions 3.1 and 4.1 reflect this: the equivalence relation ∼ is defined on the already-
restricted space Γ′ , never directly on Γ.

Remark 5.1 (Scope of the Claim). This claim is not that one cannot mathematically
write down an equivalence relation directly on Γ — one always can, since any equiva-
lence relation is by definition a subset of Γ × Γ. The claim is narrower, and concerns
observational indistinguishability: whenever ∼ is meant to encode which states cannot be
told apart by some retained measurement or representation, ∼ is necessarily induced by
an antecedent choice of observable structure, in the sense that it refines the kernel rela-
tion of some restriction RJ . An equivalence relation imposed on Γ without reference to
any observable does not correspond to identification in the sense of Definition 4.1, which
requires ∼ to live on an already-restricted space Γ′ .

   Theorem 7.2 makes this precise: any deterministic representation ϕ, however it was
actually constructed, is provably equivalent to one that first restricts to the canonical
minimal observable algebra σ(ϕ) and only then quotients. This is why Theorems 8.2
and 7.2 together should be read as a single claim: restriction-then-identification is the
canonical, and up to the freedom in Remark 8.3, essentially forced factorization of any
deterministic representation.


6     Related Work
Sufficient statistics and generated σ-algebras. The minimality property underlying
Theorem 7.2 is the classical fact that the σ-algebra generated by a measurable map is
the coarsest one making the map measurable. This is foundational to the theory of
sufficient statistics developed by Halmos and Savage [7], and appears as a standard lemma
in Kallenberg [9]. The two theories nonetheless address different problems. Sufficient
statistics theory solves an inference problem: given a family of probability distributions
indexed by a parameter, it identifies a statistic that retains all information relevant to
estimating that parameter, discarding the rest as noise. The present framework instead
characterizes the internal structure of an arbitrary given representation, independent of
any inference target or parametric family: σ(ϕ) is canonical relative to ϕ alone, not
relative to a statistical model ϕ is meant to help estimate. A sufficient statistic can be
viewed as one particular identification that happens to lose no task-relevant information;
the present theory applies whether or not any such task is specified. Blackwell’s theory of
comparison of experiments [8] formalizes when one restriction is informationally coarser
than another, close in spirit to comparing RI across choices of J.
    Rate-distortion theory and the information bottleneck. The additive decom-
position of Theorem 9.1 is an instance of the Shannon entropy chain rule [5, 10]. Splitting

                                            10
a lossy encoding into a restriction stage and a quotient stage parallels rate-distortion the-
ory [10], where an encoder is chosen under a distortion budget, and the information
bottleneck method [11], where a representation is optimized to preserve task-relevant
information. The distinction is that both of these treat restriction as a free design vari-
able to optimize, whereas Theorem 7.2 shows that, for an already fixed deterministic
representation, a canonical minimal restriction is induced by the map itself, not chosen.
The framework does not yet supply a tradeoff bound between RI and II analogous to a
rate-distortion function R(D); see Appendix B.
    Exact categories and the first isomorphism theorem. Theorem 8.2 applies the
axioms of an exact category [6]. The requirement that every equivalence relation be a
kernel pair with a coequalizer is what makes the categorical first isomorphism theorem
available in this generality; see also Mac Lane [4].
    Digital twins and representation in engineering. The applied motivation is
widely discussed in the digital twin literature, typically without a shared formal vocabu-
lary for comparing loss across domains.

Definition 7.1 (Observable Algebra). Let (Γ, A) be a measurable space. An observable
algebra on Γ is a sub-σ-algebra B ⊆ A.

standard Borel space, and let ϕ : Γ → D be a deterministic representation (Definition 2.1)

8      Factorization in Exact Categories
Theorem 7.2 is stated in Meas. To obtain the same factorization result in categories
without a σ-algebra structure — such as Set, or categories of sheaves relevant to FTM

                                            12
— we need a categorical setting in which quotients by equivalence relations are guar-
anteed to exist and to behave as ordinary quotients do in Set or Meas. Exactness, in
the sense of Barr [6], is precisely the structure that guarantees this: it ensures every
equivalence relation on an object is the kernel pair of some morphism, so that “quotient
by observational indistinguishability” is always a well-defined operation, not merely a
coincidence of Set or Meas.

Definition 8.1 (Exact Category). A category C is exact if:

    • It has finite limits.

    • Every morphism factors as a regular epimorphism followed by a monomorphism.

    • Regular epimorphisms are stable under pullback.

    • Every equivalence relation is a kernel pair of some morphism (equivalently, has a
      coequalizer).

Examples include Set, Meas (surjective measurable maps as regular epimorphisms), and
any topos.

Theorem 8.2 (Factorization Relative to an Observable Restriction). Let C be an exact
category, O = {fi : Γ → Xi }i∈I an observable structure on Γ, and ϕ : Γ → D a
deterministic representation. Suppose J ⊆ I is such that ϕ factors as ϕ = ψ ◦ RJ for
some ψ : ΓJ → D. Then ϕ admits an essentially unique factorization

                                       ϕ = Q ◦ RJ ,

where Q : ΓJ ↠ D is the coequalizer of the kernel pair of ψ.

Proof. RJ : Γ → ΓJ is a regular epimorphism onto its image (exactness). The kernel
pair K ⇒ ΓJ of ψ has a coequalizer Q : ΓJ ↠ ΓJ /K (exactness), and ψ factors uniquely
through Q; since K is exactly the kernel pair of ψ, the induced map ΓJ /K → D is both
mono and epi, hence an isomorphism, so D ∼= ΓJ /K. Uniqueness follows from uniqueness
of coequalizers up to isomorphism.

Remark 8.3. J is not uniquely determined by ϕ. However, Theorem 7.2 shows that in
measurable settings, there is a canonical minimal choice: J corresponding to the genera-
tors of σ(ϕ). Any other factorization factors through this minimal one.


9     Separability and Additivity of Information Loss
We work in Meas with a probability measure on Γ. Additivity below is the Shannon
entropy chain rule [5, 10].

Theorem 9.1 (Additivity of Information Loss). Let Γ be a probability space, and let
ϕ = Q ◦ RJ : Γ → D be a deterministic representation. Then

              ℓϕ = ℓRJ + ℓQ ,   ℓRJ = H(Γ) − H(ΓJ ),    ℓQ = H(ΓJ ) − H(D),

and ℓϕ = H(Γ) − H(D) = H(Γ | D), all entropies Shannon entropy under the induced
measures.

                                            13
Proof. Since ϕ is deterministic, H(D | Γ) = 0, so by the chain rule H(Γ) = I(Γ; D)+H(Γ |
D) = H(D) + H(Γ | D), giving H(Γ | D) = H(Γ) − H(D). Writing H(Γ) − H(D) =
[H(Γ) − H(ΓJ )] + [H(ΓJ ) − H(D)] gives the decomposition, exactly, with the first bracket
depending only on RJ and the second only on Q.

Proposition 9.2 (Separability of Loss Components). For fixed ΓJ : (1) ℓRJ depends only
on RJ , not on Q; (2) ℓQ depends only on the partition of ΓJ defining D, not on how ΓJ
was obtained from Γ.

Proof. (1) The quantity involves only Γ, ΓJ . (2) Once ΓJ is fixed, H(ΓJ ) − H(D) depends
only on the partition defining D.

Remark 9.3. We say separable, not independent: the proposition states the two loss
terms are computed from disjoint data, not that they are probabilistically independent
random quantities.

10     Computational Considerations
Remark 10.1 (Computing RI and II in Practice). For finite Γ, D given explicitly, dimeff
reduces to a cardinality count or an entropy computable in time linear in |Γ| or |D|
given the induced measure. For continuous or high-dimensional Γ (natural images, neural
feature spaces), dimeff (Γ) = H(Γ, µ) generally cannot be computed exactly and must be
estimated, e.g., via k-nearest-neighbor entropy estimators or variational bounds. The
cost and accuracy of such estimators, not any obstruction in the framework itself, are the
practical bottleneck to computing RI, II for realistic machine learning pipelines.

11     Worked Examples
Each example below follows the same template: Reality, Observable Restriction, Identi-
fication, RI, II, Loss Decomposition.

Example 11.1 (Map Projection with Discretized Grid). Reality: Γ = {1, . . . , N }2 ×
{1, . . . , H} (longitude, latitude, altitude), |Γ| = N 2 H.
Observable Restriction: retain longitude/latitude, discard altitude; Γ′ = {1, . . . , N }2 .
Identification: group into M × M blocks, D = set of M 2 blocks.
RI: log(N 2 )/ log(N 2 H). II: log(M 2 )/ log(N 2 ).
Loss: ℓR = log H; ℓQ = 2 log(N/M ); ℓϕ = log H + 2 log(N/M ).

Example 11.2 (Digital Twin of a Building). Reality: BIM state space, dimeff (Γ) =
ns log K + nt log L (structural/thermal observables at K, L levels).
Observable Restriction: retain the ns structural observables.
Identification: FEM discretization reduces structural detail by factor c < 1.
RI: ns log K/(ns log K + nt log L). II: c.
Loss: ℓR = nt log L; ℓQ = (1 − c)ns log K.

Example 11.3 (Machine Learning Feature Extractor). Reality: images Γ = {0, . . . , 255}n×m×3 .
Observable Restriction: feature extractor f : Γ → Rd ; Γf = f (Γ), dimension ≤ db (b
= bit precision).
Identification: classifier g partitions Γf into K regions, D = {1, . . . , K}.
RI: ≤ db/(8nm · 3). II: log K/H(Γf ).
Loss: restriction loss = pixel-to-feature information discarded; identification loss =
feature-to-class information discarded. (Parallels the information bottleneck’s encoder/predictor
split [11], though f, g are given, not jointly optimized.)

Example 11.4 (Geographic Information Systems). Reality: land-cover raster, N × N
cells, C fine categories, dimeff (Γ) = N 2 log C.
Observable Restriction: retain only an N ′ × N ′ study boundary.
Identification: reclassify C subtypes into K < C coarse classes.
RI: N ′2 /N 2 . II: log K/ log C.
Loss: ℓR = (N 2 − N ′2 ) log C; ℓQ = N ′2 (log C − log K).

Example 11.5 (Robotics: Occupancy Grid Mapping). Reality: continuous configura-
tion space of obstacle positions.
Observable Restriction: lidar observes only a horizontal slice within sensor range/field
of view.
Identification: continuous occupancy probability per cell thresholded into {free, occu-
pied, unknown}.
RI: fraction of the environment’s effective dimension covered by the slice. II: log 3/H(cell occupancy).
Loss: restriction loss = unobserved regions; identification loss = collapse of graded oc-
cupancy to a ternary symbol (symbolic abstraction, Remark 4.2).

Example 11.6 (Medical Imaging: Tumor Segmentation). Reality: MRI intensity field
over a 3D voxel grid Γ = {1, . . . , N }3 .
Observable Restriction: field of view and chosen contrast sequence (e.g., T1-weighted
only).
Identification: segmentation into {tumor, healthy tissue, background}.
RI: fraction of physiological state accessible under the chosen modality. II: log 3/H(Γ′ ).
Loss: restriction loss = physiological information no modality in the protocol can ac-
cess; identification loss = intensity detail discarded per voxel (image segmentation, Re-
mark 4.2).

Observable Restriction: extraction restricted to a fixed ontology’s predicate vocabulary
J.
Identification: coreference resolution merges mentions into one graph node.
RI: fraction of semantic content within the ontology’s vocabulary.     II: node-count /
mention-count ratio.
Loss: restriction loss = semantic content outside the ontology (sentiment, hedging);

13     Limitations
   • Determinism. Theorems 7.2 and 9.1 require H(D | Γ) = 0. Stochastic represen-
     tations, where ϕ is a Markov kernel, require a non-vanishing conditional entropy
     term, and the minimal-σ-algebra construction does not directly carry over.

   • Standard Borel / finite settings. Theorem 7.2 assumes standard Borel spaces;
     genuinely infinite-dimensional systems (function-space-valued states) are outside
     the present scope.

   • No tradeoff bound. No inequality relating RI and II is established; see Ap-
     pendix B.

   • Static factorization. The dynamic/higher-order treatment in Section 9 is schematic
     and has not been extended to trajectory spaces in full generality.

   • Choice of dimeff . dimeff is defined case-by-case rather than from one unifying
     construction; cross-category comparisons require a further compatibility argument.



                                             16
14     Future Directions
   • Optimal observable selection: characterizing, for a fixed budget on RI, the
     choice of J minimizing total loss.

   • Dynamic representations: extending Theorems 7.2 and 9.1 to trajectory spaces
     and stochastic processes.

   • Stochastic representations: replacing deterministic ϕ with a Markov kernel Γ ⇝
     D and re-deriving additivity via conditional mutual information; this is the natural
     setting for probabilistic encoders and variational autoencoders (VAEs), where the
     encoder q(z | x) plays the role of a stochastic observable restriction and the decoder
     plays the role of identification, and for which H(D | Γ) > 0 in general.

   • Representation geometry: a metric or divergence on the space of representations
     of a fixed Γ based on (RI, II).

   • A representation category: formalizing Rep(Γ) with morphisms as refinements
     of restriction/identification pairs.

   • AI representations: connecting RI, II to existing probing and interpretability
     metrics for intermediate neural network layers.

   • Viability-aware representations: connecting back to FTM’s notion of system
     viability.

   • Digital twin completeness metrics: (RI, II) as a standardized, cross-vendor
     completeness metric.

   • An RI–II tradeoff bound: a rate-distortion-style inequality relating RI, II, ℓϕ
     (Appendix B).


15     Conclusion
This paper has organized a family of classical results — the minimality of a generated
σ-algebra, the categorical first isomorphism theorem in exact categories, and the Shannon
entropy chain rule — around a single decomposition of representation loss, and shown
that together they force a specific structural claim not automatic from any one result
alone: observable restriction is the necessary, non-arbitrary first stage of any determin-
istic representation, and identification the necessarily subsequent stage. Rather than
proposing yet another representation model, this work proposes a structural language for
analyzing every deterministic representation through the universal notions of Observable
Restriction and Identification. It is this common language, spanning digital twins, scien-
tific modeling, machine learning, simulation, and beyond, rather than any single theorem
in isolation, that constitutes the contribution of this work.

B      Open Direction: An RI–II Tradeoff Bound
The framework as developed reports RI and II side by side but does not prove any
relationship between them. A natural next result would be a bound

                                        ℓϕ ≥ g(RI, II)

for some function g, analogous to a rate-distortion function, characterizing the minimum
achievable total loss for given RI, II. Establishing such a bound, rather than only the
additive accounting of Theorem 9.1, would be a genuinely new quantitative contribution
beyond the classical results this paper otherwise recasts.
