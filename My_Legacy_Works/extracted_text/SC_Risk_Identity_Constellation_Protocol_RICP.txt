1     Core Concepts and Definitions
This section introduces and formally defines the core concepts of the Risk
Identity & Constellation Protocol (RICP). The purpose of this section is con-
ceptual clarity; no control logic, algorithms, or implementation assumptions
are introduced here.

1.1    Physical Entity
A Physical Entity is any uniquely identifiable real-world object, system,
organization, location, infrastructure, or natural phenomenon that can par-
ticipate in the transmission, reception, or transformation of hazards.
    Physical entities constitute the foundational building blocks of RICP and
may assume different roles depending on context.

1.2    Source of Imposition (SoI)
A Source of Imposition (SoI) is a physical entity from which a hazard
originates for the first time within a given constellation.
    An SoI:

    • Is uniquely identifiable

    • Acts as the initial origin point of a hazard

    • May be intentional or unintentional

    • Does not require awareness of the hazard it emits

   A source does not constitute risk by itself; it only becomes relevant
within a constellation.

1.3    Hazard
A Hazard is any factor capable of disrupting, degrading, or preventing the
expected functionality of a target of impact.
   Hazards may be:

    • Concrete: physical forces, materials, energy, natural events

    • Abstract: legal, economic, informational, or symbolic constructs

    Hazards may further be classified as:

    • Hard Hazards: directly causing physical or structural disruption

    • Soft Hazards: causing functional, operational, or systemic disruption

    A hazard alone does not constitute risk.

1.4   Hazard Carrier
A Hazard Carrier is a medium that encapsulates or contains a hazard,
allowing it to be transported without necessarily activating its effects.
    Examples include documents, digital packets, materials, signals, or phys-
ical matter.

1.5   Hazard Channel
A Hazard Channel is the transmission path through which a hazard carrier
moves from one physical entity to another.
   Channels may be:
   • Physical (transport routes, infrastructure)

   • Informational (networks, communication systems)

   • Institutional (procedural or legal pathways)

1.6   Hazard Hub
A Hazard Hub is a physical entity that receives a hazard via one or more
hazard channels and may:
   • Transform the hazard

   • Amplify or attenuate the hazard

   • Redirect the hazard to other entities
   A hazard hub may subsequently act as a new Source of Imposition.

1.7   Target of Impact (ToI)
A Target of Impact (ToI) is a physical entity whose expected functionality
may be disrupted by a hazard.
   A ToI:
   • Is uniquely identifiable

   • Possesses one or more expected functionalities

   • May be affected intentionally or unintentionally

1.8   Expected Functionality (EF)
Expected Functionality (EF) represents the set of functions or outcomes
a target of impact is expected to deliver under normal conditions.
    EF is not a single value, but a structured concept that may be evaluated
qualitatively or quantitatively.

                                     2
1.8.1    Qualitative Expected Functionality
Qualitative Expected Functionality refers to non-numeric or subjective
dimensions of performance such as trust, legitimacy, reputation, or perceived
stability.

1.8.2    Quantitative Expected Functionality
Quantitative Expected Functionality is defined as a set of measurable
and comparable parameters representing the functional performance of a
target of impact.
    Each parameter:
   • Is individually measurable
   • Is comparable to an expected or reference value
   • May have its own unit of measure
   Quantitative EF is inherently multi-dimensional.

1.9     Risk Velocity
Risk Velocity is the rate of change of expected functionality over time,
under the assumption that the change is caused by a single identifiable risk.
   Risk velocity may be defined for:
   • Individual quantitative EF parameters
   • Aggregated qualitative EF scores

1.10     Singularity of Expected Functionality
The Singularity of Expected Functionality (SEF) is the point at which
the cost of restoring a target of impact to its prior expected functionality
equals or exceeds the cost of recreating that functionality anew.
    Beyond this point, recovery is no longer economically or structurally
rational.

1.11     Risk Constellation
A Risk Constellation is the joint coexistence of:
   • A source of imposition
   • A contextual hazard
   • A target of impact
within a defined time and space boundary.
   Risk does not exist outside a constellation.

                                     3
1.12   Risk
Risk is the uncertainty of failing to meet a defined objective, emerging
exclusively from a risk constellation and evaluated relative to the expected
functionality of the target of impact.
   A source, hazard, or target in isolation does not constitute risk.
