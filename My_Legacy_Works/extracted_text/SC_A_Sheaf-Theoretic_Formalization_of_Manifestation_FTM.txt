    Computational modeling of complex systems traditionally conflates identity with state.
    This paper introduces the Formal Theory of Manifestation (FTM), a mathematical
    framework that separates these notions by treating identity as an invariant equivalence
    class over a reality substrate (I ∈ Ω/G) and treating state as a local section of a sheaf over
    a context site. We define Manifestation Sheaves, equipped with policy-lattice structure, and
    prove the Observer Incompleteness Theorem, the Minimum Observability Cover Theorem,
    a sharpened Fallacy of Super Identity, and a Viability Monotonicity result with an explicit
    set-inclusion lemma. Beyond re-instantiating standard sheaf-theoretic facts, we establish
    one new result with independent mathematical content: for the broad class of function
    sheaves on a finite point set, the Minimum Observability Cover problem is exactly the
    classical Minimum Set Cover problem, and is therefore NP-hard and, under standard
    complexity assumptions, hard to approximate below a logarithmic factor (Section 6.6).
    We give a fully explicit worked example (a three-zone bridge sensor network, Section 7) in
    which every axiom and theorem in the paper is verified against concrete data, and we report
    a computational experiment (Section 8) that brute-force verifies locality, gluing, minimal
    observability, and the failure of the Cartesian-product reconstruction, cross-checked against
    the new theorem’s closed-form prediction. We are explicit throughout about which results
    are genuinely new, which are standard facts instantiated in a new setting, and which are
    definitional (Section 11). We close by relating FTM to established literature on system
    viability and hazard propagation, and by defining Digital Twins as local sections of a
    manifestation sheaf.

1     Introduction: The Ontological Hierarchy and the Necessity of
      Sheaves
In classical information systems, an entity is modeled as a “Golden Record”—a hypothetical global
object containing all attributes across all contexts. FTM proposes an inversion of this default:
state is the local observation of identity through context.
1.1    The Ontological Hierarchy
FTM organizes manifestation into a directional hierarchy:
1. Reality (Ω): the raw, unstructured substrate.
2. Identity (I): the invariant structure extracted from Reality.
3. Context Universe (C): the topological space of structural constraints.
4. Context / Observer (U ⊆ C): an information vantage point (open set) inhabiting the
   Context Universe.
5. Local Section (sU ): the manifestation of Identity within the Observer’s vantage point.
6. Measurement: the final extraction of data from the Local Section.
FTM – Revised Edition                                                                          2



Observers do not create Contexts; they inhabit them. Contexts exist independently as structural
constraint spaces. This hierarchy is a modeling choice, not a theorem; its value is judged by
whether it clarifies real systems, which Section 7 tests directly against a concrete example.
1.2    Why Sheaf Theory?
Table 1 compares candidate structures against three requirements a manifestation model needs:
locality of observation, partial observability, and consistent gluing of compatible local data.

          Table 1: Comparison of mathematical structures for modeling manifestation
          Structure       Locality    Partial Obs.      Gluing   Suitable for FTM?

          Graph / Set         ×             ×             ×              No
          Category         Partial       Partial          ×            Partial
          Fiber Bundle       Yes          Weak          Weak           Partial
          Sheaf              Yes           Yes           Yes            Yes

• Graphs/Sets lack a topology of “overlaps” and cannot express partial observability.
• Ordinary categories give morphisms but no built-in notion of an open cover, which we need
  to model how observers partition the context universe.
• Fiber bundles assume local triviality, which is too rigid once governance rules impose non-
  trivial, asymmetric constraints across contexts.
• Sheaves provide exactly the two ingredients FTM needs: restriction maps ρU V , and a gluing
  condition that decides when locally observed manifestations assemble into a globally valid
  identity.
This is a design justification, not a uniqueness proof: we are not claiming sheaves are the
only adequate structure, only that they are the minimal standard structure satisfying all three
requirements simultaneously.

2.2    Categorical Interpretation
1. Identity lives in a quotient category: the category Id has as objects the orbits of G on Ω.
2. Context induces a site: the Context Universe C, equipped with a Grothendieck topology J,
   forms a site (C, J).
3. Manifestation is a sheaf on that site: state assignments form a sheaf F ∈ Sh(C, J).
4. Reconstruction is a right adjoint: the global-section functor Γ is right adjoint to the
   constant-sheaf functor. Concretely, Γ(F) is the terminal object among cones over the diagram of
   local sections and restriction maps — the largest space of global data consistent with everything
   observed locally. This is a standard categorical fact about sheafification/global sections, applied
   here to the manifestation setting rather than a new adjunction being constructed.

3     The Topology of Manifestation: Context Universe and Observers

    a context, modeled as a specific open U ⊆ C. The collection of all observer vantage points
    forms an open cover U = {Ui }i∈I of C.

4     The Manifestation Sheaf

    A sheaf F ∈ Sh(C, Top), together with a distinguished identity I ∈ Ω/G, is a manifestation
    sheaf for I, written FI , if it satisfies:
    1. Policy lattice structure: for each open U , the space F(U ) carries a lattice ΠU of valid
       subspaces, representing contextual constraints on which local states are admissible.
    2. Identity marking: the global sections Γ(FI ) are equipped with a distinguished point
       ιI ∈ Γ(FI ), and the assignment I 7→ ιI is required to be well defined and injective
       across identities: I1 ̸= I2 ⇒ ιI1 ̸= ιI2 under any common embedding of the Γ(FI )’s.



    We have revised the identity-compatibility clause from an earlier version of this framework.
    Requiring Γ(FI ) itself to be homeomorphic to the entire quotient Ω/G cannot be right
    for a sheaf attached to a single identity I: it would force the global sections of every
    identity’s sheaf to be the same space, regardless of I, since Ω/G does not depend on I.
    The corrected clause above instead asks only that identities be recoverable as distinguished
    points of their sheaves, and that this marking be injective.



    For each open U , F(U ) = ΣU is the topological space of valid local states. For V ⊆ U , the
    restriction map ρU V : F(U ) → F(V ) is continuous.



    F satisfies the standard sheaf axioms:
    1. Locality: if s, t ∈ F(U ) and ρU,Ui (s) = ρU,Ui (t) for every Ui in an open cover of U , then
       s = t.
    2. Gluing: if si ∈ F(Ui ) agree on overlaps, there is a unique s ∈ F(U ) with ρU,Ui (s) = si
       for all i.

6.4   Viability Monotonicity Theorem


   For a policy-lattice element Π, let Σ(Π) ⊆ F(U ) denote the set of states satisfying the
   constraints encoded by Π.



   If Π1 ≤ Π2 in the policy lattice, then Σ(Π1 ) ⊆ Σ(Π2 ).


Proof. Π1 ≤ Π2 means the constraints of Π2 are implied by those of Π1 : every state satisfying
Π1 ’s constraints also satisfies Π2 ’s, since Π2 forbids no state that Π1 permits. Hence s ∈ Σ(Π1 ) ⇒
s ∈ Σ(Π2 ).



   If a context morphism f : C1 → C2 satisfies Π1 ≤ Π2 , then the survival measure SCi (t) :=
   µ(Σ(Πi ) ∩ Reach(t)), for µ a Radon measure on C and Reach(t) the set of states reachable
   by time t, satisfies SC1 (t) ≤ SC2 (t) for all t.


Proof. By Section 6.4, Σ(Π1 ) ⊆ Σ(Π2 ), hence Σ(Π1 ) ∩ Reach(t) ⊆ Σ(Π2 ) ∩ Reach(t) for every t.
Monotonicity of the Radon measure µ under inclusion gives SC1 (t) ≤ SC2 (t).

    This result is deliberately modest in scope: it applies to the function-sheaf class, not to
    arbitrary manifestation sheaves with non-discrete context universes or non-trivial policy
    lattices. We regard it as a first genuinely new theorem in this line of work — one that
    connects an invariant introduced by FTM to an independently well-studied problem, with
    consequences (hardness, an approximation guarantee) that could not have been read off
    from an existing sheaf theory textbook, precisely because Nobs is not a standard sheaf-
    theoretic invariant. Extending the characterization beyond the function-sheaf class — e.g.
    relating Nobs to the nerve of the cover for sheaves valued in more general categories — is
    open and is, in our view, the most promising direction for a deeper structural theorem.


7     A Worked Example: Three-Zone Bridge Sensor Network
We now instantiate every construction above on an explicit, numerically concrete example, so that
the sheaf axioms, the Fallacy of Super Identity, and the Minimum Observability Cover Theorem
can each be checked by hand rather than taken on faith. This is a fully specified synthetic
instantiation, not measured data from a deployed sensor network; Section 11 is explicit about
that distinction.
7.1    Setup
Consider a bridge deck instrumented with vibration sensors at five points,

                                        X = {p1 , p2 , p3 , p4 , p5 },

where p1 lies on span A, p5 lies on span C, p3 lies on span B, and p2 , p4 sit at the two expansion
joints A–B and B–C respectively (hence are physically shared between adjacent spans’ monitoring
zones). Three overlapping sensor zones cover the deck:

                     U1 = {p1 , p2 },      U2 = {p2 , p3 , p4 },         U3 = {p4 , p5 }.

Each sensor reports a Boolean reading, V = {0, 1}: 0 for “vibration within normal range,” 1 for
“vibration threshold exceeded.” This is exactly the function-sheaf construction of Section 4 with
|X| = 5, |V | = 2, so |Γ(F )| = 25 = 32 possible full readings of the deck.

7.2    Verifying the Sheaf Axioms
Locality. If two candidate global readings s, t ∈ Γ(F) agree on U1 , U2 , U3 , they agree at p1 , . . . , p5
(since U1 ∪ U2 ∪ U3 = X), hence s = t: locality holds for the full cover.
Gluing. Given local readings s1 = (1, 0) on U1 (meaning p1 =1, p2 =0), s2 = (0, 1, 0) on U2
(p2 =0, p3 =1, p4 =0), s3 = (0, 1) on U3 (p4 =0, p5 =1): these agree on the overlaps (p2 =0 from
both s1 , s2 ; p4 =0 from both s2 , s3 ), so gluing predicts a unique global section s = (1, 0, 1, 0, 1).
Section 8 verifies this by brute-force search over all 32 candidates.
7.3    The Fallacy of Super Identity, Concretely
Now choose local readings that do not agree on overlaps:

                 s1 = (1, 0) on U1 ,       s2 = (1, 0, 1) on U2 ,     s3 = (0, 1) on U3 .

Here s1 reports p2 =0 but s2 reports p2 =1 (conflict on U1 ∩ U2 ); s2 reports p4 =1 but s3 reports
p4 =0 (conflict on U2 ∩ U3 ). The tuple (s1 , s2 , s3 ) ∈ F(U1 ) × F(U2 ) × F(U3 ) = P is a perfectly
good element of the Cartesian product — exactly the kind of object a naive “collect all sensor
readings into one record” design would accept — but by Section 5 it does not correspond to
any actual state of the bridge, since no single deck-wide reading vector restricts to all three
simultaneously. Section 8 confirms this by checking all 32 candidates.
7.4    Minimum Observability Cover
By Section 6.6, since p1 appears only in U1 , p3 only in U2 , and p5 only in U3 , no subcover of size
≤ 2 can cover X (dropping any zone leaves its exclusive point unmonitored), so Nobs (F) = 3: all
three sensor zones are necessary to uniquely reconstruct the deck’s state. Section 8 verifies this
exhaustively over all 23 = 8 subcovers.

9     Integration with Established Systems-Viability and Hazard-Propagation
      Literature
Rather than resting FTM’s application layer on unpublished companion work, we ground it here
in existing, peer-reviewed literature on system viability and hazard propagation, and indicate
FTM – Revised Edition                                                                              14



where FTM’s sheaf-theoretic vocabulary offers a translation of established ideas rather than a
replacement for them. Where we draw an analogy rather than a proof, we say so explicitly.
9.1   System Viability: Cybernetic and Ecological Foundations
• Ashby’s Law of Requisite Variety (Ashby, 1956) states that a regulator can counter the
  variety of a system only if the regulator has at least as much variety available. Read against
  FTM, the policy lattice ΠU bounds the disturbance variety the system can absorb at U ;
  Section 6.4 is a sheaf-local restatement of the requisite-variety intuition. This is an interpretive
  analogy, not a new derivation of Ashby’s law.
• Beer’s Viable System Model (VSM) (Beer, 1972, 1979) decomposes an organization into
  recursively nested viable subsystems, structurally close to FTM’s context/observer hierarchy
  (§3), and gives an independently studied account of “recursive viability” against which FTM’s
  hierarchy can be checked.
• Holling’s ecological resilience (Holling, 1973) distinguishes engineering resilience (return to
  a single equilibrium) from ecological resilience (persistence of identity across regimes) — the
  distinction FTM’s identity/manifestation split is designed to formalize.
9.2   Hazard Propagation as Network Topology
• Fault tree and bow-tie analysis (Ericson, 1999) is the standard formalism for cataloguing
  minimal cut-sets of hazard-propagation paths — the role played by Σ(c) below.
• Cascading failure in networked infrastructure (Albert, Jeong, & Barabási, 2000; Motter
  & Lai, 2002) formalizes how local failure propagates through network topology; the cut-set
  equivalence classes below are a sheaf-theoretic redescription of the grouping this literature
  already uses.
9.3   A Sheaf-Theoretic Reading of Hazard Contexts
Let Chaz be a context universe whose opens correspond to operating regimes of a networked
system, and Fhaz a manifestation sheaf whose local sections are fault-tree/bow-tie minimal
cut-sets (Ericson, 1999). Then Σ(c) = Fhaz (U ), and Σ(c) / ∼risk (paths sharing a cut-set) is
an instance of the identity quotient Ω/G applied to hazard trajectories. This is a translation
exercise, not a claim that FTM improves on the underlying engineering analysis. We note in
passing that this construction is also, formally, a function sheaf in the sense of Section 4 when
cut-sets are represented as Boolean vectors over component failure states, so Section 6.6 applies
directly: determining the minimum instrumentation needed to observe all distinguishable hazard
scenarios in such a system is itself an NP-hard set-cover problem, for which the greedy heuristic
of Section 6.6 gives a practical logarithmic approximation guarantee.
9.4   Capacity and Survival
The policy lattice ΠU models regulatory capacity in Ashby’s sense; the survival measure S(t) of
Section 6.4 tracks admissible reachable states under that capacity — resilience in Holling’s sense,
not stability around a single equilibrium.

    A digital twin Tk is a local section sk ∈ F(Uk ).



    A single digital twin cannot capture the full identity whenever Observational Non-
    degeneracy holds for its context. Synchronizing multiple twins requires verifying pairwise
    compatibility on overlapping contexts; twins on disjoint contexts are parallel manifestations,
    not replicas, and cannot be checked against one another at all. Determining the minimum
    set of twin contexts needed for full observability is, by Section 6.6, generally NP-hard, and
    the greedy heuristic of Section 6.6 is the recommended practical approach for large twin
    networks.


11     Scope and Limitations
We distinguish four kinds of claim in this paper:
• A genuinely new theorem, tested computationally: Section 6.6 (Observability as Set
  Cover) and its corollaries (Section 6.6) connect an invariant introduced in this line of work (Nobs )
  to an independently studied NP-hard problem, with consequences not visible from standard
  sheaf theory. Section 8 confirms the theorem’s prediction against brute-force computation on a
  concrete instance, rather than only presenting the proof. This result is deliberately narrow in
  scope (the function-sheaf class); extending it to sheaves with non-discrete context universes or
  non-trivial policy lattices is open.
• Other genuine derivations specific to this framework: the Fallacy of Super Identity
  (Section 5) and the Identity Separation Criterion (Section 6.3) and State-Space Monotonicity
  Lemma (Section 6.4) connect FTM-specific structure to conclusions via arguments that could,
  in principle, fail.
• Standard mathematical facts instantiated in this setting: Manifestation Stability
  (universal property of the product topology), Viability Monotonicity (monotonicity of measure
  under inclusion), and the adjunction remark in §2.2 are correct and useful confirmations that
  the framework behaves sensibly, but are not new mathematics.
• Definitional restatements: Section 6.3 follows immediately from its hypothesis and is
  retained only as motivation for Section 6.3.
Section 9 draws analogies to established literature (Ashby, Beer, Holling, Ericson, cascading-
failure network theory); these are interpretive correspondences, not proofs that FTM derives
or improves upon those results. An earlier version of this paper grounded that section in
unpublished companion preprints by the same author; that dependency has been removed in
favor of peer-reviewed sources.
The worked example of Section 7 is a fully specified, numerically explicit synthetic instantiation —
constructed to be small enough to verify by hand and by brute-force computation (Section 8)
— not measured data from a deployed sensor network. It demonstrates that the axioms and
FTM – Revised Edition                                                                            16



theorems are mutually consistent and computable on a concrete case, not that FTM has been
empirically validated against real infrastructure data. Building and verifying a manifestation
sheaf from an actual measured dataset (e.g. a public structural-health-monitoring or SCADA
dataset), and checking whether the sheaf axioms hold against real sensor noise and missing data,
remains the most valuable next empirical step. Likewise, Section 6.6’s greedy heuristic has not
yet been benchmarked against real large-scale instrumentation-placement problems; doing so is a
natural extension of the computational work reported here.

12     Conclusion
By separating identity (I ∈ Ω/G) from manifestation (local sections of a sheaf F over a context
site), FTM gives a framework in which claims like “a single observer is insufficient” or “the union
of all observations over-generates” can be stated and proved precisely. This edition addresses the
three most consequential gaps in the earlier presentation: it gives one fully worked, numerically
explicit example against which every axiom and theorem can be checked (Section 7); it reports
an actual computational experiment, with real output, that verifies locality, gluing, minimal
observability, and the failure of the Cartesian-product reconstruction on that example (Section 8);
and it proves one theorem with genuine, non-instantiated mathematical content — that Minimum
Observability Cover for function sheaves is exactly Minimum Set Cover, hence NP-hard and
logarithmically hard to approximate (Section 6.6) — which could not have been anticipated from
general sheaf theory, since the underlying invariant is specific to this framework. Section 11 makes
explicit which of the paper’s remaining results are standard facts instantiated in a new setting
versus framework-specific derivations, so that the paper’s actual mathematical contribution is not
overstated.
