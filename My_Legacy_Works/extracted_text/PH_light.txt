The Status of This Module
This module is Part IV of the fifth edition of The Philosophy of Viability. Its purpose is
to develop, organize, and formally articulate the topic of light—not as a scientific topic,
but as the second column of the four-column framework of cognition. A reader already
familiar with Parts I–III can read this module independently.


The Independence of Philosophy from Science
This module is written under the following principle:

Principle 0.1 (Autonomy of Philosophical Justification). (A)The validity of no
philosophical principle in this module depends on confirmation from outside philosophy—
including from science. Every principle is derived from within the four-column framework
and from philosophical arguments.

Methodological consequence. References to science in this module can serve only
three roles:

 (a) Coherence test: showing that science can develop itself within the philosophical
     framework without contradiction. This role is permitted—provided it is explicitly
     marked as “test” and not “evidence.”

 (b) Historical motivation: showing that the problem is a real problem, arising from
     the history of thought. This role is permitted—provided it is treated as “motivation”
     and not “reason.”

 (c) Legitimation: deriving the authority of philosophy from the confirmation of sci-
     ence. This role is forbidden.

Throughout this module, every reference to science appears inside a “Coherence Test”
box, with its role explicitly stated.


On Formalization
Formalization in this module is balanced and philosophical : brief notation, formulation of
principles, dependency tables, and epistemic labels. Not full formal proof, not mathemat-
ical formulation. The aim is precision of presentation, not justification. Every principle
appears first in full philosophical prose, and only then in symbolic form.

Why Light? Problem and Method

1.1       The Problem
In the four-column framework (Part I), every explanatory cognition requires four roles:
fixed elements, fluid elements, governing dynamics, and context.1 The question is: what
is the fluid element? What is it that conveys effect between identities and manifestations,
and makes connection possible?
    The answer of this module: light. But “light” here means neither electromagnetic
waves nor a mystical metaphor. “Light” is a structural concept that must be defined with
philosophical precision.


1.2       The Method
The method is threefold:

   1. Description: gathering and classifying the multiple human answers to the question
      “What is light?”—not as historiography, but as a structural map.

   2. Proposal: offering a philosophical definition of light as a trans-categorical sub-
      stance.

   3. Establishment: developing this definition within the four-column framework, and
      drawing its consequences for cognition, experience, and explanation.


1.3       Why Is Light a Philosophical Problem?
Light was a topic of philosophy before it was a topic of science. Plato called it the image
of the Good;2 Plotinus, emanation from the One;3 Suhrawardi, the very substance of
reality;4 Ibn Arabi, self-disclosure of the Real;5 Mulla Sadra, a mode of existence.6 This

2.2       Beginning: An Experience
Close your eyes. In the dark, objects are—you reach out, you touch a table. But they are
not seen. Then you open your eyes. The same table, the same distance, the same hand.
But something has been added : the table is seen. What is that added thing?
    The question seems simple. But across history it has received multiple answers—and
this multiplicity is not accidental.

2.5       Four Structural Observations
From this table, four observations follow:

     1. Multiplicity is patterned. The four patterns recur across history—not randomly,
        but structurally.

     2. No pattern eliminates another. Each pattern captures an aspect of light—and
        leaves another aside.

     3. No pattern is complete. All four are incomplete—and this very incompleteness
        motivates the proposal of the next chapter.

     4. All patterns share a common presupposition. All take light to be something
        that is in space-time—a wave that propagates, a particle that moves, a field that
        oscillates. And this common presupposition is challenged in Chapter 4.


2.6       The Common Presupposition
The common presupposition is this: light is a thing in space-time, whether wave, particle,
or field. This presupposition is so deeply embedded that it is rarely noticed. Yet it is the
source of the tension among the patterns. If light is a thing, it must be either substance
or accident, either process or relation. And each of these categories fails to capture the
whole.

Chapter 3

Light as Trans-Categorical Substance

3.1      The Aim of This Chapter
This chapter is a proposal. After describing the four patterns in the previous chapter, it
now offers a philosophical definition that goes beyond the tension among the patterns—not
by eliminating one, but by changing the level.


3.2      Deadlock
The previous chapter showed that four structural patterns exist for understanding light—
and that none is complete. If light is substance, its being a relation is unexplained. If
it is accident, its independent existence is unexplained. If it is process, its stability is
unexplained. If it is relation, its physical reality is unexplained.
    Deadlock: every existing category takes something away from light.


3.3      The Need for a New Category
Principle 3.1 (The Trans-Categorical Character of Light). (A)Light goes beyond the
existing philosophical categories—substance, accident, process, relation. A new category
is needed: the trans-categorical substance.


3.4      The Definition
Definition 3.1 (Trans-Categorical Substance). (A)A trans-categorical substance is a
substance that comes before the distinction substance/accident/process/relation—not af-
ter it. That is, it is the condition of possibility of that distinction, not its result.

Remark 3.1 (How to Speak of the Trans-Categorical). This definition appears paradox-
ical. How can something come before the categories while itself being understood through
categories? The answer requires a shift in how we use language. The trans-categorical
substance is not understood through a categorical definition (which would place it within a
category); it is understood through analogical indication and ostension. Just as one can-
not define the nature of sight by a proposition, but only by pointing to the act of seeing, so
too light can only be indicated by the structural role it plays in making relation possible.

When we call light a “substance,” we use the term analogically: it is not a substance
among other substances, but the precondition for any substance to be related to another.


3.5         Light as Condition of Possibility of Relation
Principle 3.2 (Light as Condition of Possibility of Relation). (T)Light is not a relation—
it is the condition of possibility of relation. Without light, no relation is legible. But light
itself is not legible—it is legible-making.
Argument.
     1. Every relation is between two terms.
     2. For a relation to be legible, there must be something that connects the two terms.
     3. This “something” cannot itself be a relation—for then it would need another relation
        (regress).
     4. So there must be something that comes before relation—its condition of possibility.
     5. This “something” is what we call “light.”

Light: Active Structure, Not
Traveler

4.1        The Aim of This Chapter
This chapter sharpens the definition of the previous chapter. If light is a trans-categorical
substance, it cannot “move”—because motion requires time, and time is a category of
material manifestation.1 So light does not travel —it is an active connection structure.


4.2        The Problem: Fluidity vs. Motion
In the four-column framework, light is the fluid element. But “fluid” is usually understood
as “flowing” and “in motion.” If light moves, it travels—and “travel” contains time within
itself. And this is incompatible with the stability of light.


4.3        The Solution: Active Connection Structure
Definition 4.1 (Active Connection Structure (Scaffold)). (A)An active connection struc-
ture (metaphorically, a “scaffold”) is a structure that:
 (a) always is—not that it is built and then moves;
 (b) is a structure of connection—not a path of connection;
 (c) has edges that are activated or deactivated—according to rules;
 (d) itself does not move—its connections change.
Principle 4.1 (Light Is an Active Structure). (A)Light is an active structure—not a
traveler. Light does not move; the edges of the structure, according to the rules of the
realms, connect different nodes to one another.
Remark 4.1 (The Metaphor of the Scaffold). The term “scaffold” is used here as a
structural metaphor, not a spatial diagram. It is meant to evoke the idea of a pre-existing
framework of connection, not a physical object located in space. To avoid spatial baggage,
the formal term adopted in this module is “Active Connection Structure.” The metaphor
of the scaffold is retained only for illustrative purposes.

Remark 4.2 (Activation vs. Temporal Change). One might object: if an edge is “acti-
vated,” does this not imply a temporal event? The answer is no. Activation is a structural
determinacy, not a temporal event. At any given stage of the material realm (which is
temporal), an edge is either structurally active or inactive. The transition between states
is temporal, but the state itself (the active edge) is a structural fact. The activation is
not a change in light; it is a change in the configuration of the nodes that the structure
connects.

Remark 4.3. Motion is our language—not the reality of light. We say “light goes”—
because time and motion are categories of material manifestation, and we can speak only
within material manifestation. But light itself is beyond these categories.


4.5        Why Does Light Not Travel?
Principle 4.2 (Non-Travel of Light). (T)Light does not travel, because:

     1. Travel requires time.

     2. Time is the order of succession of the stages by which changes are realized in a
        realm.

     3. Light is beyond time—because it is the condition of possibility of any relation.

     4. Therefore light does not travel.

Chapter 6

The Pairs of the Four Columns

6.1      The Aim of This Chapter
This chapter presents a structural insight that organizes the four columns into two pairs.
This insight is directly relevant to light, because light belongs to the second pair.


6.2      The Two Pairs
The four columns are not four isolated roles. They form two structural pairs:

Pair     Columns                                  Role
Pair 1   Fixed elements + Context                 The terms of the relation
Pair 2   Dynamics + Fluid element                 The medium of the relation


6.3      Pair 1: Fixed Elements and Context
Pair 1 determines the terms of the relation: what is related and where it is related.
Without fixed elements, there is nothing to relate. Without context, the fixed elements
have no frame of reference. The two are mutually dependent: fixed elements without
context are undefined; context without fixed elements is empty.


6.4      Pair 2: Dynamics and Fluid Element
Pair 2 determines the medium of the relation: how the relation works and by what it
is conveyed. Without dynamics, the fluid element is unordered. Without fluid element,
dynamics is meaningless—because there is nothing for the rule to govern.

Principle 6.1 (Pair 2 as the Medium of Relation). (A)Dynamics and fluid element form
the medium of the relation. Without fluid element, dynamics is meaningless. Without
dynamics, fluid element is unordered. The two are mutually dependent.


6.5      Why This Pairing?
The pairing follows from the structure of relation itself. Every relation has two aspects:

                                            25
26                               CHAPTER 6. THE PAIRS OF THE FOUR COLUMNS

     1. Terms: what is related. These are the fixed elements, situated in a context.

     2. Medium: how the relation is established and governed. This is the fluid element,
        governed by dynamics.

     The four columns are the analytical decomposition of these two aspects.


6.6       Implications for Light
Light belongs to Pair 2. This has three implications:

     1. Light is not a term of the relation—it is the medium. It is not what is related, but
        what conveys the relation.

     2. Light is inseparable from dynamics. Without the rules of the realms, light is un-
        ordered. The two must be understood together.

     3. Light is the condition of possibility of the relation between fixed elements and con-
        text. Without light, the two terms are disconnected islands—without any relation.

connection, without dynamics, without explanation. This is offered as a coherence test, not as
evidence: it shows that the principle “light is the condition of possibility of any explanation” is
coherent with the way explanatory discourses actually work.

10.3       Open Questions
Q1. Descent without travel. If light does not travel, what exactly is “descent”? First
    answer: appearance of light in a lower realm in the mode of that realm—not motion
    toward it. (Q)

Q2. Succession in higher realms. If time is phenomenal, what is “succession” in
    higher realms? First answer: structural succession—not temporal. Like logical
    succession (premise before conclusion)—not temporal succession. (Q)

Q3. Estimate from structure. If light is the only accessible source, how do we esti-
    mate from it? First answer: through structure—not content. (Q)

Q4. Sediment as active edge. If light is an active structure, what is “sediment”?
    First answer: an active structural edge that has remained active—and has not been
10.4. A CLOSING REMARK                                                                35

     integrated. Not light that has “settled”—but a structural edge that has remained
     in an active state. (Q)

Q5. Relation to light. If light is the condition of possibility of relation, can we speak
    of a “relation to light”—or is that a category mistake? (Q)

Q6. Experience of light. If we are encompassed in light, can we speak of an “expe-
    rience of light”—or is experience always experience of something other than light?
    (Q)

Q7. Circularity of explanation. If light is the condition of possibility of any expla-
    nation, can any explanation explain light—or is every explanation of light circular?
    (Q)

Q8. Energy and light in higher realms. If energy is a relational feature in material
    manifestation, what is its counterpart in higher realms? First answer: a structural
    analogue, not energy as quantity of matter. (Q)


10.4      A Closing Remark
This module has developed, organized, and formally articulated the topic of light within
the four-column framework. It has proposed that light is a trans-categorical substance,
an active structure rather than a traveler, the medium of relation, the condition of pos-
sibility of relation and of any explanation, and the foundational fluid element of every
cognitive apparatus. It has done so without deriving its authority from science, and
without reducing itself to science. The horizon is open. The inquiry continues.
