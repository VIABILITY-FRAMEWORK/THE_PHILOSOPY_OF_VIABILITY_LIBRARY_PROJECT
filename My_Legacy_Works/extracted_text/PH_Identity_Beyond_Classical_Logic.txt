      Sheaf-Theoretic and Topos-Theoretic
    Framework for Non-Classical Metaphysics

                     Prepared for: Philosophical Studies Special Issue
                      Non-Classical Metaphysics: New Perspectives

                                           Abstract
         Recent developments in non-classical metaphysics have challenged classical as-
     sumptions about identity, existence, and unity. This paper introduces a rigorous
     mathematical framework for non-classical metaphysics grounded in sheaf theory, cat-
     egory theory, and topos theory. I define identity as an invariant equivalence class in a
     quotient space Ω/G rather than as a classical logical predicate, and manifestations as
     local sections of a sheaf over a context site. The framework rests on a set of axioms
     formalized in the Formal Theory of Manifestation (FTM) and its categorical founda-
     tions. I prove the Identity Rigidity Theorem, demonstrating that fibred equivalences
     between manifestation fibers induce isomorphisms of the underlying identities, and
     the Characterization Theorem, establishing that every contextual manifestation fibra-
     tion satisfying six axioms is equivalent to a unique sheaf over a context site. Three
     case studies demonstrate the framework’s non-classical applications: (1) the Ship of
     Theseus paradox, resolved by distinguishing identity from material manifestations; (2)
     the Buddhist Anattman doctrine, reinterpreted through the Observer Incompleteness
     Theorem; and (3) Ibn Arabi’s Unity of Existence, formalized via the Classifying
     Topos for FTM. By providing a unified formal language, this framework aims to
     bring isolated non-classical approaches into a systematic dialogue. I conclude by
     discussing implications for dialetheism, fragmentalism, and non-standard mereology,
     and by proposing future directions for paraconsistent and many-valued extensions.

     Keywords: Non-classical metaphysics, Identity, Sheaf theory, Category theory,
     Topos theory, Dialetheism, Fragmentalism

1    Introduction: The Crisis of Identity in Classical Meta-
     physics
Classical metaphysics, from Aristotle to contemporary analytic philosophy, has been
structured around two foundational logical principles: the principle of identity (x = x)
and the principle of non-contradiction (¬(P ∧ ¬P )). These principles have shaped how
philosophers understand existence, identity, time, and modality. Yet three persistent
paradoxes suggest that classical logic may be insufficient for capturing the full complexity
of identity:
    The Ship of Theseus. If all planks of a ship are gradually replaced, is it still the
same ship? If the old planks are reassembled, which one is the original? Classical logic
forces a binary choice, yet both answers seem intuitively defensible.
    The Anattman Doctrine. Buddhism denies a permanent self, arguing that the five
aggregates (skandhas) are all impermanent. Yet if there is no self, what reincarnates?
What attains nirvana? Classical logic cannot accommodate both the denial of self and the
continuity of personal identity.
    Unity of Existence. Ibn Arabi’s doctrine that "all is the manifestation of the Real"
asserts both unity (all is one) and diversity (observers perceive many things). Classical
logic treats unity and diversity as contradictories, yet mystical traditions maintain their
coexistence.
    These paradoxes share a common structure: they arise from conflating identity with
manifestations. A ship is not its planks; a person is not their memories or aggregates; the
Real is not its manifestations. Yet classical logic, with its binary categories and principle
of non-contradiction, cannot model this distinction without generating paradoxes.
    Recent developments in non-classical metaphysics—dialetheism [Priest(2006)], frag-
mentalism [Fine(2005)], and non-standard mereologies [Varzi(2016)]—have opened new
avenues for rethinking these foundational notions. However, these approaches have largely
evolved in isolation, lacking a unified mathematical language that could systematically
model identity across contexts and transformations.
    By providing a unified formal language, this framework aims to bring these isolated
non-classical approaches into a systematic dialogue. It clarifies their shared metaphysical
commitments regarding identity and context, showing how apparent contradictions often
arise not from logical failure, but from the conflation of the invariant identity level with
the contingent manifestation level.
    This paper introduces a rigorous mathematical framework for non-classical metaphysics
grounded in sheaf theory, category theory, and topos theory. The framework,
developed in the Formal Theory of Manifestation (FTM) [Hasibi(2026a)] and its categorical
foundations [Hasibi(2026b)], offers a unified language for modeling identity, manifestation,
and context. My central thesis is:

      Identity is not a logical predicate but an invariant equivalence class
      under transformations; manifestations are local sections of a sheaf
      over a context site.

    This inversion—from "identity as substance" to "identity as equivalence class"—
constitutes a fundamental departure from classical metaphysics. It enables the modeling
of identities that persist through change, that are inaccessible to any single observer, and
that simultaneously exhibit unity and diversity.

2     Mathematical Preliminaries
2.1     Category Theory as a Language for Metaphysics
Category theory provides a natural language for non-classical metaphysics because it
focuses on relationships rather than substances. A category C consists of objects and
morphisms (arrows) between them, with composition and identity laws. In this framework:

    • Identities are objects in a base category B.

    • Contexts are objects in a context category C.

    • Manifestations are elements of a sheaf F : Cop → Set.

2.2     Sheaf Theory: Locality, Gluing, and Context
A sheaf F on a topological space C assigns to each open set U ⊆ C a set F(U ) of local
sections, with restriction maps ρU V : F(U ) → F(V ) for V ⊆ U . Two axioms characterize
sheaves:

    1. Locality: If two sections agree on every open set in a cover of U , they are identical.

    2. Gluing: If local sections agree on overlaps, they can be uniquely glued into a global
       section.

    Sheaves are the mathematical structures that model how local observations (mani-
festations) compose into global entities (identities) while preserving consistency across
overlapping contexts.

2.3     Topos Theory: Internal Logic and Universality
A topos E is a category that behaves like the category of sets, but with a richer internal
logic. Topos theory enables reasoning about identities, contexts, and manifestations within
the mathematical structure itself, without relying on external set theory. Key results
include:

    1. The Internal Logic of a topos allows quantification over identities and manifesta-
       tions.

    2. Geometric Morphisms preserve manifestation structure across toposes.

    3. The Classifying Topos for FTM provides a universal structure for all models of
       the theory.

5     Non-Classical Applications
5.1     The Ship of Theseus: Identity and Material Manifestation
The paradox arises from identifying the ship with its material components. In my
framework:

    1. The ship’s identity Iship is an object in B, independent of its planks.

    2. Planks are manifestations of the ship in the material context site Cmaterial .

    3. When planks are replaced, the manifest set M(Iship ) changes, but Iship remains
       invariant.

    4. By the Identity Rigidity Theorem, Iship ∼
                                               = Iship under all transformations, ensuring
       persistence.

   The classical paradox is resolved by distinguishing identity (invariant equivalence class)
from manifestation (context-dependent local section). The reassembled old planks are not
the original ship—they are a different spatial configuration, a different manifestation of
the same identity only if the structural constraints so determine.

5.2     The Anattman Doctrine: Observer Incompleteness and Per-
        sonal Identity
Buddhism’s doctrine of non-self (anattman) asserts that the five aggregates (skandhas) are
all impermanent, and therefore that there is no permanent self. Yet this raises a question:
if there is no self, what reincarnates? What attains nirvana?
    My framework offers a non-classical resolution:

    1. The five aggregates are manifestations of identity in the psychological context site
       Cpsychological .

    2. The Observer Incompleteness Theorem establishes that a single observer (even
       oneself) cannot reconstruct the full identity from these manifestations.

    3. Identity Iperson ∈ B exists independently of any single context.

    4. The Anattman doctrine correctly identifies the impermanence of manifestations but
       incorrectly concludes that identity does not exist.

                                              7
    This reinterpretation is non-classical in two ways. First, it denies that the self is
a substance while affirming that identity exists as an invariant structure. Second, it
introduces observer-dependence into the concept of self: the self is not directly accessible
from any single context.

5.3     Ibn Arabi’s Unity of Existence: The Classifying Topos
Ibn Arabi’s doctrine of wahdat al-wujud (unity of existence) asserts that all things are
manifestations of the Real (al-Haqq). In my framework:

    1. The Real corresponds to the Classifying Topos EFTM for FTM.

    2. All identities I ∈ B are objects in this topos.

    3. Diversity arises at the level of local sections F(U ) in different contexts.

    4. Unity is at the base level; diversity is at the manifestation level.

   The Classifying Topos Theorem provides a mathematical model for this non-classical
metaphysics: unity and diversity coexist because they exist at different levels of the
same structure. Unity is the universal structure (the topos), while diversity is the local
manifestations.


6     Implications for Non-Classical Metaphysics
6.1     Dialetheism and Identity
Dialetheism accepts true contradictions. My framework can accommodate dialetheism by
allowing identities that are both same and different across contexts. For example, the Ship
of Theseus can be both the same ship (as identity in B) and different (as manifestation in
Cmaterial ). The contradiction is not in the logic but in the conflation of levels.

6.2     Fragmentalism and Time
Fragmentalism holds that reality is composed of fragments that are internally coherent but
mutually inconsistent. My framework models this through context sites: each context U
defines a fragment. The sheaf condition ensures that fragments are consistent on overlaps,
but different contexts may yield different manifestations of the same identity.

6.3     Non-Standard Mereology
Non-standard mereologies challenge the classical part-whole relation. My framework offers
a context-dependent mereology: parts and wholes are manifestations in contexts. A part
in context U may be a whole in context V , depending on the restriction maps.

7     Conclusion
This paper has introduced a rigorous mathematical framework for non-classical metaphysics
grounded in sheaf theory, category theory, and topos theory. The framework establishes:

    1. Identity is an equivalence class in a quotient space Ω/G, not a logical predicate.

    2. Manifestations are local sections of a sheaf over a context site.

    3. The Observer Incompleteness Theorem demonstrates that no single observer can
       reconstruct full identity.

    4. The Identity Rigidity Theorem shows that fibred equivalences of manifestations
       imply isomorphic identities.

    5. The Characterization Theorem proves that every contextual manifestation fibration
       arises uniquely from a sheaf.

    6. The Classifying Topos for FTM provides a universal structure for all models.

    Three case studies demonstrate the framework’s non-classical applications:

    1. The Ship of Theseus is resolved by distinguishing identity from material manifesta-
       tions.

    2. The Anattman doctrine is reinterpreted through observer incompleteness.

    3. Ibn Arabi’s Unity of Existence is formalized via the classifying topos.

   The framework offers a relational metaphysics in which identity is not a substance
but an invariant structure under transformations, while diversity emerges at the level of
contextual manifestations. This provides a unified mathematical language for non-classical
approaches to identity, existence, time, and modality.


Future Directions
Several directions for future research emerge:

    1. Paraconsistent Logics: Extending the framework to paraconsistent logics, allowing
       identity to be both true and false in different contexts.

    2. Many-Valued Logics: Modeling identities with degrees of membership across
       contexts using many-valued logics.

    3. Quantum Metaphysics: Applying the framework to quantum identity, where
       particles are indistinguishable and identity is context-dependent.

    4. Computational Implementation: Developing software tools for reasoning about
       non-classical identity in digital twin systems.
