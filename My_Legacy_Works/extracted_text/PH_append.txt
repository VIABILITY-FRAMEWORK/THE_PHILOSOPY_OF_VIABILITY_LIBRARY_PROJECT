Preface to the Extension

This document is an extension of The Philosophy of Viability (Hasibi, 2026). It develops, in a
systematic and formal form, seven structural claims that were either absent from the main volume
or present only implicitly. The claims are the following:

  (I) Containment Topology. The seven realms are not merely ordered; each is a topological
      subspace of the next, and the whole chain is contained in the Ground.

 (II) Hubs as Categories. The three barzakhic realms are not functions but categories of
      transition—modes of existence whose internal structure is passage.

(III) The Trans-Categorical Triad. Light, identity, and self-awareness are the three trans-
      categorical conditions of possibility: of relation, of succession, and of choice respectively.

(IV) The Ontological Status of the Modal Space. The modal space of possibilities is onto-
     logically real. It is posterior to the Ground, prior to actualization, and prior to the structural
     order of the realms. This yields a structural resolution of the classical tension between divine
     omniscience and creaturely freedom.

 (V) The Thread and the Beads. Identity is the invisible thread that holds a sequence of
     manifestations together; it is not the sequence, and it is not reducible to it.

(VI) Action as Change of Possibility Space. An action is any effect that expands or contracts
     the space of possible realization of an identity. The ethical evaluation distinguishes three
     categories of ethical action—blameworthy (contraction), duty (preservation of harmony), and
     virtue (expansion)—and is grounded in the structural parallel with the trans-categorical triad.

(VII) Time and Fundamental Self-Awareness. Time is not a non-phenomenal feature of real-
      ity; it is the structure of succession, and it presupposes a fundamental self-awareness as its
      scaffold.

   The document is written under the same methodological commitment as the main volume:
no principle is justified by appeal to any outside domain; every principle is formalized where
formalization aids precision, and the prose carries the principle. Epistemic status labels (A) (axiom),
(T) (argued), (E) (estimate), (Q) (open question), and (S) (speculation) follow the conventions of
the main volume.
   The chapters of this extension are numbered E1–E6 to distinguish them from the chapters of
the main volume. Cross-references to the main volume appear in the form Philosophy, Ch. N.

The Topology of Containment

E1.1      The Question
The main volume defines seven realms (Philosophy, Chs. 7–8) and states that they are indepen-
dently real (Philosophy, Principle 7.1). It also states that the ordinal relation among realms does
not impose a route (Philosophy, Principle 7.3), and that descent is structural rather than kinetic
(Philosophy, Principle 13.4). These three claims, taken together, leave a question unanswered: in
what precise sense are the realms related?
   The present chapter answers this question by introducing a single structural notion—containment—
and by showing that the three claims of the main volume are consequences of it.


E1.2      Containment as Topological Subspace
Definition E1.1 (Containment). (A) A realm Ri is contained in a realm Ri+1 , written Ri ⊏ Ri+1 ,
if and only if there is a topological embedding ιi : Ri ,→ Ri+1 such that the topology of Ri is the
subspace topology induced by ιi .

Remark E1.1. The embedding ιi is not merely a set-theoretic injection. It preserves the structural
relations internal to Ri : every relation that holds among manifestations of Ri is the restriction of
a relation of Ri+1 . In this sense, the higher realm contains the lower without thereby exhausting
it—the higher realm has structural possibilities that the lower realm cannot express.

Principle E1.1 (The Containment Chain). (A) The seven realms form a containment chain

                              R1 ⊏ R2 ⊏ R3 ⊏ R4 ⊏ R5 ⊏ R6 ⊏ R7 ,

where

                                  R1 = First Determination,
                                  R2 = Hub of Decree,
                                  R3 = Structural Determination,
                                  R4 = Hub of Signification,
                                  R5 = Relational Manifestation,
                                  R6 = Hub of Realization,
                                  R7 = Material Manifestation.

                                                 9
10                                      CHAPTER E1. THE TOPOLOGY OF CONTAINMENT

Principle E1.2 (The Ground as Environment). (A) The Ground (the structural name for what
the traditions call al-‘Arsh) is not a realm within the chain. It is the environment of the chain:
R1 ⊏ R2 ⊏ · · · ⊏ R7 ⊏ Ground, where the final containment is not a containment in a realm but
a containment in the condition of possibility of the chain.
Remark E1.2 (Why the Ground Is Not an Eighth Realm). If the Ground were an eighth realm, the
argument for the number of realms (Philosophy, Chs. 19–20) would be called into question, and the
distinction between the created order and its uncreated ground (Philosophy, Remark 7.7) would
collapse. The Ground is therefore not a realm; it is the environment in which the chain of realms
is contained.


E1.3      Descent as Dimensional Restriction
Principle E1.3 (Descent as Restriction). (T) Descent from Ri+1 to Ri is not a motion. It is the
restriction of the structure of Ri+1 to the subspace Ri . What appears in Ri is the maximum that
Ri can bear of the structure of Ri+1 .
Proof. By Definition E1.1, Ri is a topological subspace of Ri+1 . A structure on Ri+1 (a function, a
relation, a rule) restricts to a structure on Ri by composition with the embedding ιi . The restricted
structure is the maximum that Ri can express of the structure of Ri+1 , because any structure on
Ri that is compatible with the embedding is a restriction. If a richer structure were expressed in
Ri , the embedding would not be an embedding—it would be an isomorphism, contradicting the
strictness of the containment.

Example E1.1 (Sphere and Circle). Consider a sphere S 2 contained in R3 , and a plane P ⊂ R3 .
The intersection S 2 ∩ P is a circle (or empty, or a point). The circle is not the sphere; it is the
maximum of the sphere that the plane can bear. The sphere does not “travel” to the plane; it
appears in the plane as a circle, because the plane’s two-dimensional capacity cannot express the
sphere’s three-dimensional structure. The circle is a faithful—but partial—manifestation of the
sphere in the plane.
Remark E1.3. The sphere–circle image is an illustration, not an argument. It illustrates the struc-
ture of restriction, but it must not be taken literally: the realms are not spatial dimensions, and the
containment is not spatial containment. The image is useful because it makes visible the structure
of maximum expressible in a subspace, which is the structure of descent.


E1.4      Compatibility with Independence and Non-Passage
Proposition E1.1 (Containment Entails Independence of Mode). (T) If Ri ⊏ Ri+1 , then Ri has
a mode of existence that is not reducible to the mode of existence of Ri+1 .
Proof. Suppose, for contradiction, that the mode of existence of Ri is reducible to that of Ri+1 .
Then every structural possibility of Ri is a structural possibility of Ri+1 restricted. But by Def-
inition E1.1, the embedding ιi is strict: Ri+1 has structural possibilities that Ri cannot express
(otherwise the containment would be an isomorphism). Hence the mode of existence of Ri has
features—namely, the structural possibilities it excludes—that are not features of Ri+1 ’s mode as
restricted to Ri . Therefore the modes are distinct.

Remark E1.4. This proposition shows that containment strengthens the independence principle of
the main volume (Philosophy, Principle 7.1). Independence is not separability; it is independence
E1.5. THE SELF AS CONTAINED IN ALL REALMS                                                         11

of mode. The realms are contained, one in the next, but each has a mode that the others cannot
exhaust.

Proposition E1.2 (Containment Entails Non-Passage). (T) If Ri ⊏ Ri+1 , then an identity man-
ifested in Ri need not be manifested in any intermediate realm Rj with i < j < k for any k > i.

Proof. By Definition E1.1, Ri is already a subspace of Ri+1 , hence of every Rk with k > i. The
identity, being manifested in Ri , is therefore present in every higher realm as an element of the
subspace, without needing to appear in any intermediate realm as such. Passage would require the
identity to be manifested in each intermediate realm, which is a strictly stronger condition than
containment. Containment does not require passage.

Remark E1.5 (Containment Is Not Pantheism). Containment must not be confused with identity.
That Ri ⊏ Ri+1 does not entail that Ri = Ri+1 , nor that the Ground is identical with the chain.
The subspace is a proper part of the space, not the whole of it. The principle containment is
not identity is stated explicitly in Philosophy, Remark 14.6, and is here extended to the relation
between realms.


E1.5      The Self as Contained in All Realms
Principle E1.4 (The Self as Contained). (A) The self is not located in a single realm. It is a
subspace of every realm in the chain, from R1 to R7 . In each realm, the self appears according to
that realm’s capacity, but the self is not exhausted by any of its appearances.

Remark E1.6. This principle is the structural foundation of the anthropology developed in Ap-
pendix B of the main volume. There, the self is defined as the meeting-point of nafs (the exigency
of the material category) and ruh (the light of the higher category). With the containment prin-
ciple, the meeting-point is not a point in a single realm; it is a subspace of the whole chain. The
nafs is the aspect of the self that is expressed in R6 and R7 ; the ruh is the aspect expressed in R1
through R4 ; the self is the whole of the subspace.

Hubs as Categories of Transition

E2.1      The Question
The main volume defines the three barzakhic realms by their function: the Hub of Decree appor-
tions measure, the Hub of Signification carries structure into meaning, and the Hub of Realization
actualizes what is possible (Philosophy, Definitions 8.4–8.6). It argues for their independence (Phi-
losophy, Principle 8.1) and for their irreducibility (Philosophy, Principle 20.1).
     But the main volume does not say what kind of thing a hub is. If a hub is defined by its function,
it risks collapsing into a mere operator—something that does something but is not anything in itself.
The present chapter argues that a hub is a category of transition: a mode of existence whose internal
structure is passage.


E2.2      Hubs as Categories
Principle E2.1 (Hubs as Categories). (A) Each hub is a category of transition: an independently
real mode of existence whose internal structure is passage from one kind of multiplicity to another.
The hub is not a function that could be performed by another realm; it is a category whose being
is the passage.

Definition E2.1 (Category of Transition). A category of transition from Ri to Ri+1 is a realm
R such that:

 (a) R is a topological subspace of the environment that contains both Ri and Ri+1 ;

 (b) the internal structure of R is a passage: every element of R is a passage from an element of
     Ri to an element of Ri+1 ;

 (c) the passage is regulated by structural constraints (the rules of the hub, Philosophy, Ch. 17).

Remark E2.1. The definition locates the hub by its mode of existence, not by its effects. A hub
does not first exist and then mediate; it exists as mediation. This is the structural meaning of the
claim that the hubs are not reducible to functions (Philosophy, Principle 8.1).

Proposition E2.1 (The Three Hubs Are Categories of Transition). (T) The Hub of Decree is the
category of transition from R1 (pure unity) to R3 (multiplicity of form); the Hub of Signification
is the category of transition from R3 to R5 (multiplicity of relation); the Hub of Realization is the
category of transition from R5 to R7 (multiplicity of matter).

                                                  13
14                                   CHAPTER E2. HUBS AS CATEGORIES OF TRANSITION

Proof. By Definition E2.1, each hub must satisfy conditions (a)–(c). The Hub of Decree satisfies
them because (a) it is contained in the environment, (b) its internal structure is the apportioning
of unity into forms, and (c) its rules are proportion, order, and harmony (Philosophy, Principles
17.2–17.4). The same argument applies, mutatis mutandis, to the Hub of Signification and the Hub
of Realization.


E2.3       Hubs as Cognitive Operators
Principle E2.2 (Hubs as Conditions of Cognition). (T) The hubs are the conditions of possibility
of cognition of identities. An identity is veiled ( Philosophy, Ch. 25); what makes it legible is the
passage through the hubs. Each hub makes legible a different aspect of the identity.

Proof. By Philosophy, Ch. 25, an identity is intrinsically unknown to a finite subject. Cognition of
the identity requires manifestation. By Definition E2.1, manifestation is the passage through the
hubs. Each hub makes legible a specific aspect of the passage:

     • The Hub of Decree makes legible the measure of the identity (how much of it can appear);

     • The Hub of Signification makes legible the meaning of the identity (what it signifies in con-
       text);

     • The Hub of Realization makes legible the actuality of the identity (that it is realized here and
       now).

Without the hubs, the identity would remain veiled—not because the subject lacks information,
but because the structure of manifestation would not be in place.

Remark E2.2 (The Three Operators of Cognition). The three hubs correspond to three operators
on the veiled identity:

                   Measure : I → P1 ,       Signify : I → P2 ,    Realize : I → P3 ,

where P1 , P2 , P3 are the possibility spaces of the identity at the levels of measure, meaning, and
actuality. Cognition is the composition of these operators.

Light, Identity, and Self-Awareness

E3.1      The Question
The main volume introduces light as a trans-categorical substance (Philosophy, Ch. 12). The
present chapter argues that light is not the only trans-categorical: there are three, and they are
structurally parallel.


E3.2      The Principle of Totality of Possibility
Before the argument against self-awareness as a category can be stated, a principle on which it
depends must be introduced.

Principle E3.1 (Totality of Possibility). (A) Every possible is already in existence. What an
action does is not to create a possibility but to select a branch of the possible. Existence contains
all possibilities; actuality is the selection among them.

Remark E3.1. The principle is modal, not temporal. It does not say that all possibilities are
realized; it says that all possibilities are available for realization. Realization is the act of selection,
not the act of creation. The distinction between existence and actuality is the structural foundation
of the account of action in Chapter E4.


E3.3      The Argument Against Self-Awareness as a Category
Theorem E3.1 (Self-Awareness Is Not a Category). (T) Self-awareness cannot be a manifestation
in any realm. If it were, a regress would follow.

Proof. Suppose, for contradiction, that self-awareness is a manifestation in some realm Ri . By the
Principle of Totality of Possibility (Principle E3.1), every manifestation is the product of a choice.
Hence self-awareness is the product of a choice. But a choice requires a chooser, and the chooser
must be self-aware (otherwise it could not choose). Hence a further self-awareness is required, and
this regress is vicious. Therefore self-awareness is not a manifestation.

Remark E3.2. The theorem does not deny that self-awareness appears in a realm; it denies that
self-awareness is a manifestation. Self-awareness appears as a structural feature of the passage, not
as a term in the passage.

E3.4       The Trans-Categorical Triad
Principle E3.2 (The Trans-Categorical Triad). (A) Light, identity, and self-awareness are the
three trans-categorical conditions of possibility:

     • Light is the condition of possibility of relation;

     • Identity is the condition of possibility of succession;

     • Self-awareness is the condition of possibility of choice.

Each is trans-categorical: it comes before the distinction among substance, accident, process, and
relation, not after it.

Remark E3.3 (Parallelism). The triad is structurally parallel: each trans-categorical is the condition
of a specific structural feature of the realms. Relation (light) makes the network of manifestations
possible; succession (identity) makes the persistence of an identity through its manifestations pos-
sible; choice (self-awareness) makes the actualization of one possible manifestation over another
possible.

Proposition E3.2 (Irreducibility of the Triad). (T) None of the three trans-categoricals is reducible
to another.

Proof. Suppose light were reducible to identity. Then relation would be reducible to succession. But
relation and succession are distinct structural features: a network can be simultaneous (relation
without succession) or sequential (succession without relation). Hence the reduction fails. The
same argument applies, mutatis mutandis, to the other pairs.


E3.5       The Ontological Status of the Modal Space
The Principle of Totality of Possibility (Principle E3.1) states that every possible is already in
existence. The present section clarifies the ontological status of this principle, and draws four
consequences that bear on the rest of the framework.

E3.5.1      The Three Ontological Layers
Definition E3.1 (The Three Ontological Layers). (A) The ontological architecture has three layers,
in order of fundamentality:

  (i) The Modal Space Ω: the totality of all possibilities. These are grounded in the Ground and
      known by the Ground. They are ontologically real, not merely logical abstractions.

 (ii) The Actualization Chain X ⊂ Ω: the subset of possibilities that are actualized, in succes-
      sion. The chain is selected by self-awareness S.

(iii) The Structural Order R: the seven realms, the categories, and the four columns. These are
      posterior to both Ω and X : they describe the form that actualization takes, not its condition.

     The relation among the three layers is:

                            Ω
                           |{z}       −−→          X
                                                  |{z}            −−−→         R
                                                                              |{z}          .
                        Modal Space         Actualization Chain          Structural Order

Remark E3.4 (The Ground and the Theological Vocabulary). The structural term Ground is used
throughout this extension in preference to the theological term God. The Ground is the structural
condition of possibility of the chain of realms; it is not a being within the chain, and it is not
described by the framework. The choice of the structural term is not a denial of the theological
term: the traditions speak of God as the creator of all possibilities, and the structural Ground is
what the framework can say about that creator without exceeding its method. The relation between
the structural Ground and the theological God is a question for another inquiry—the inquiry that
revelation authorizes—and the framework neither undertakes it nor forecloses it. The pattern is
the same as the pattern of light in the main volume (Philosophy, Ch. 12): a structural term that
points beyond itself to what the traditions call divine.

E3.5.2    The Ontological Principle Restated
Principle E3.3 (Totality of Possibility (Ontological)). (A) The modal space Ω is ontologically real.
Every possibility is grounded in the Ground and known by the Ground. What an action does is not
to create a possibility but to select one for actualization. The actualization chain X is the selection
of self-awareness from Ω.

Remark E3.5 (Epistemic Inaccessibility). (A) We have no direct epistemic access to Ω. We access
only X (the actualized). Every formalization of possibility is therefore formalized from the stand-
point of actualization, not from the standpoint of Ω. The space Ω is posited as the ontological
ground of selection, not as an object of knowledge.

Principle E3.4 (The Modal Space Is Contained in the Ground). (A) The modal space Ω is con-
tained in the Ground: Ω ⊏ Ground. The containment is not spatial or temporal; it is the contain-
ment of a condition in its condition of possibility. The Ground is not a realm within Ω, and Ω is
not identical with the Ground. The relation is one of grounding, not of identity.

Remark E3.6 (Creation ex Nihilo, Structural Priority). The containment of Ω in the Ground is the
structural expression of the traditional doctrine of creation ex nihilo. The Ground creates Ω; Ω is
not made from anything prior. But the creation is not a temporal event: time is posterior to Ω
(Chapter E5). The creation is a relation of structural priority, not of temporal succession. The
Ground is prior to Ω in the order of grounding, not in the order of time.

E3.5.3    The Theological Resolution
Theorem E3.3 (Omniscience and Freedom Are Compatible). (T) Given the modal ontology of
Principle E3.3, divine omniscience and creaturely freedom are compatible. The Ground knows all
possibilities (Ω); the self selects among them (S). The Ground’s knowledge of Ω does not constrain
S, because the selection is constituted by the act of self-awareness, not determined by the Ground’s
knowledge.

Proof. By Principle E3.3, Ω is ontologically real and known by the Ground. By Definition E3.1, X
is the selection of S from Ω. Suppose the Ground’s knowledge of Ω determined X . Then S would
be redundant: the selection would be fixed by the Ground’s knowledge alone. But S is the trans-
categorical condition of choice (Principle E3.2); it cannot be redundant without contradicting the
triad. Hence the Ground’s knowledge of Ω does not determine X . The selection is the creature’s
act, and the creature is therefore free and responsible.

Remark E3.7 (What This Resolves). The resolution has three consequences:
20                               CHAPTER E3. LIGHT, IDENTITY, AND SELF-AWARENESS

  (i) The Ground’s omniscience is knowledge of Ω (all possibilities), not of X (the actualized
      trajectory). The Ground knows what can happen; the Ground does not determine what does
      happen.
 (ii) Human freedom is the selection of S from Ω. The selection is real, not illusory, because X
      is genuinely indeterminate until S acts.
(iii) Moral responsibility is grounded in the selection: since the selection is the creature’s act,
      the creature is responsible for the actualized chain.
Remark E3.8 (Why This Is Not Open Theism). Open theism says that the Ground does not
know the future because the future is not yet determinate. The present view is different: the
Ground knows all possibilities (including the future possibilities), but the selection among them
is not determined by the Ground’s knowledge. The future is not unknown to the Ground; it is
undetermined by the Ground’s knowledge. The distinction is between ignorance (open theism’s
problem) and non-determination (the present view’s solution).

E3.5.4    Middle Knowledge and the Molinist Refinement
The theorem of the previous subsection states that the Ground knows Ω but does not determine
X . A reader may ask: does the Ground know what the creature will freely do, or only what the
creature can do? The present subsection refines the theorem to answer this question, and adopts
the Molinist position.
Definition E3.2 (Conditional Possibilities). (A) The conditional possibilities of Ω are the coun-
terfactuals of freedom: for each creature c, each circumstance σ, and each action a, the proposition
“c would freely do a in σ” is either true or false, and is part of Ω.
Principle E3.5 (Molinist Omniscience). (A) The Ground knows (i) all bare possibilities (natural
knowledge), (ii) all conditional possibilities (middle knowledge), and (iii) the actualized chain (free
knowledge). The three kinds of knowledge are exhaustive of what there is to know, and none of
them determines the creature’s selection.
Theorem E3.4 (Omniscience and Freedom Are Compatible (Molinist Version)). (T) Given the
Molinist refinement of Principle E3.5, divine omniscience and creaturely freedom are compatible.
The Ground knows what the creature would freely do in every circumstance, but this knowledge does
not determine what the creature does. The determination is the creature’s act of selection (S), not
the Ground’s knowledge.
Proof. By Principle E3.5, the Ground knows the conditional possibilities. Suppose this knowledge
determined the creature’s selection. Then the selection would be fixed by the Ground’s knowledge
alone, and S would be redundant—contradicting Principle E3.2. Hence the Ground’s knowledge
of conditional possibilities does not determine the selection. The selection remains the creature’s
act.

Remark E3.9 (Why Molinism and Not Open Theism). Open theism says that the Ground does
not know the future because the future is not yet determinate. The present view is different: the
Ground knows all conditional possibilities (including what the creature would freely do), and knows
the actualized chain (because it is the selection of S). The future is not unknown to the Ground; it
is undetermined by the Ground’s knowledge. The distinction is between ignorance (open theism’s
problem) and non-determination (the present view’s solution). The Molinist position preserves
both omniscience and freedom; open theism preserves freedom at the cost of omniscience.

Remark E3.10 (The Problem of Evil Resolved). (T) If all possibilities, including evil possibilities,

    The Ptest. In quantum mechanics, a system in superposition is described by a wave function

Principle E3.6 (The Structural Order Is Posterior to the Modal Space). (A) The seven realms,

Remark E3.11 (What This Changes). This principle reverses the methodological priority of the

are derived from it. In the present extension, the modal space is the first principle, and both the
four-column framework and the realms are posterior to it. The reversal is not a contradiction: the
four-column framework remains the analytical structure of explanation, and the realms remain the
ontological structure of actualization. What changes is their ground : they are grounded in the
modal space, not in themselves.

Remark E3.12 (Posteriority in Fundamentality, Not in Reality). The posteriority of the realms to Ω
(Principle E3.6) is a posteriority in fundamentality, not a posteriority in reality. The realms are not
the ground of Ω; but they are not less real than Ω. The distinction is the same as the distinction
in Philosophy, Remark 7.4: independence is not self-sufficiency. The realms are independent of
each other in mode (Philosophy, Principle 7.1); they are not independent of Ω in grounding. The
two claims are compatible because they concern different respects: the first concerns the mode of
existence, the second concerns the order of grounding.

E3.6      The Thread and the Beads
Definition E3.3 (Identity as Thread). (A) An identity is a thread: a structural invariant that
holds a sequence of manifestations together. It is not the sequence, and it is not any of its members.

Principle E3.7 (The Invisibility of the Thread). (T) The thread is not visible in the field of
manifestations. What is visible are the beads (the manifestations); the thread is not visible because
the field does not have the capacity to display it.

Proof. By Definition E3.3, the thread is the invariant, not the varying. What is displayed in a
realm is the varying (the manifestations). The invariant is the structural condition of the display,
not an item in it. Hence the thread is not visible in the field of manifestations.

Remark E3.13. The invisibility of the thread is structurally parallel to the invisibility of light
(Philosophy, Principle 14.5). In both cases, a condition of possibility is not one of the things made
possible.


E3.7      Reduction of Members and Conservation of Identity
Theorem E3.5 (Conservation Under Reduction). (T) If the number of manifestations of an iden-
tity is reduced, the identity is not thereby destroyed. The thread is conserved; only the beads are
lost.

Proof. By Definition E3.3, the identity is the thread, not the beads. The thread is the condition
of possibility of the sequence, not the sequence itself. Hence the loss of some beads does not entail
the loss of the thread. The identity is conserved.

Example E3.1 (Amputation and Hydrocephalus). A person who loses a limb, or whose brain is
significantly compressed by fluid (hydrocephalus), loses some manifestations but not her identity.
The thread is conserved; the beads are reduced. This is consistent with the empirical findings cited
in the literature, and it illustrates the structural principle: identity is not reducible to its material
substrate.
Remark E3.14. The empirical examples are coherence tests, not justifications. They show that the
structural principle is compatible with the empirical facts; they do not establish it. The structural
argument (Theorem E3.5) is what establishes the principle.

E4.1      The Question
The main volume’s Appendix A states that viability metaethics describes the architecture of moral
systems (Philosophy, Appendix A.1). But it does not say what an action is, nor what makes an
action ethical. The present chapter proposes a structural definition of action, refines it by the
distinction between action, contextual act, and ethical action, and derives from it a structural
taxonomy of ethical evaluation.


E4.2      Action Defined
Definition E4.1 (Action). (A) An action is any effect that changes the space of possible realization
of an identity.
Definition E4.2 (Space of Possible Realization). (A) The space of possible realization of an
identity I in a realm R is the set P(I, R) of manifestations of I that are structurally compatible
with I and with R.
Remark E4.1. The space P(I, R) has two dimensions: its cardinality (how many possible mani-
festations it contains) and its structure (how rich the relations among them are). An action may
change either or both.
Proposition E4.1 (Three Types of Action). (T) Every action is of one of three types:
  (i) Contractive: reduces P(I, R);
 (ii) Expansive: increases P(I, R);
(iii) Rearranging: changes the structure of P(I, R) without changing its cardinality.
Proof. By Definition E4.1, an action changes P(I, R). The change is either a decrease (contractive),
an increase (expansive), or a change of structure without a change of cardinality (rearranging).
These are exhaustive.


E4.3      Action, Contextual Act, and Ethical Action
The definition of action in the previous section is deliberately broad. It includes every effect that
changes a space of possible realization, whether or not the effect has an agent. The present section
refines the definition by distinguishing three kinds of effect.

                                                 25
26                            CHAPTER E4. ACTION AS CHANGE OF POSSIBILITY SPACE

Definition E4.3 (Action, Contextual Act, Ethical Action). (A) Let P(I, R) be the space of possible
realization of identity I in realm R, and let e be an effect that changes P.
  (i) e is an action iff e changes P(I, R).
 (ii) e is a contextual act iff e is an action and no will or choice precedes it.
(iii) e is an ethical action iff e is an action and a will or choice precedes it.
Remark E4.2 (The Falling Stone). The falling of a stone is an action (it changes the space of
possible realization of the things beneath it), but it is a contextual act (no will precedes it). It
is therefore not subject to ethical judgment—not because it has no effect, but because it has no
agent. This is consistent with the distinction between ethical and contextual evil developed in
Section E4.6.
Remark E4.3 (Why the Distinction Matters). Without the distinction, the expansion criterion
(Principle E4.1) would have to evaluate the falling of a stone, the flow of a river, and the growth
of a plant—which is absurd. The distinction locates the domain of ethical evaluation: only actions
that are preceded by a will or choice. Actions that are not so preceded are contextual acts; they
are structurally real, but they are not candidates for ethical evaluation.


E4.4      The Expansion Criterion
Principle E4.1 (The Expansion Criterion). (A) An ethical action is ethical if it expands (or at
least preserves) the space of possible realization of some identity, without contracting the space of
possible realization of any other identity.
Remark E4.4 (The Criterion Is Structural). The expansion criterion is not a utilitarian criterion
(which would maximize the sum of spaces) nor a deontological criterion (which would be a categor-
ical rule). It is structural: it states a condition on the relation between an action and the space of
possible realization. A utilitarian or deontological criterion may be derived from it, but only under
additional premises.
Proposition E4.2 (Non-Ethical Actions). (T) If an ethical action contracts the space of possible
realization of an identity I2 without a structural justification, the action is non-ethical.
Proof. By the expansion criterion, an ethical action must not contract any identity’s space without
justification. If the contraction is unjustified, the action fails the criterion. Hence the action is
non-ethical.


E4.5      The Bridge Principle
Principle E4.2 (Bridge Principle). (A) Viability (in the sense of Philosophy, Def. 6.2) is a
structural good in the following sense: it is the condition of possibility of any good that the relation
between an identity and its manifestations can bear. It is not itself the good, but its reduction
reduces the condition of possibility of the good.
Remark E4.5 (The Bridge Does Not Violate the Is/Ought Gap). The bridge principle does not
derive an ought from an is. It derives a conditional ought from an is: if the good is to be possible,
then viability must be maintained. The conditional is a structural claim, not a normative one. The
normative premise (that the good is to be possible) is supplied by the inquiry into the good, not
by the structural framework.

E4.6      Contextual Evil and Ethical Evil
Definition E4.4 (Ethical and Contextual Evil). A contextual evil is a contraction of a space of
possible realization that is not caused by an agent (e.g., a flood). An ethical evil is a contraction
caused by an agent.
Proposition E4.3 (Responses to Evil). (T) Responding to a contextual evil by expanding the space
of possible realization is an ethical action. The response is not a response to an ethical evil (the
flood is not an agent), but it is an ethical action, because it expands the space.
Proof. By Definition E4.4, the flood is a contextual evil. By the expansion criterion (Principle E4.1),
an action that expands the space is ethical. Hence the response to the flood is ethical.


E4.7      The Threefold Ethical Taxonomy
The expansion criterion (Principle E4.1) states a binary condition: an ethical action either satisfies
it or does not. The present section sharpens the criterion into a threefold taxonomy, which locates
every ethical action in exactly one of three categories.
Definition E4.5 (Threefold Ethical Taxonomy). (A) Let A be an ethical action, and let ∆P be
its effect on the space of possible realization. A belongs to exactly one of three categories:
 (1) Blameworthy ( mazmūm): ∆P < 0—the action contracts the space.

 (2) Duty ( wazı̄fa): ∆P = 0 and the harmony between P and the self-awareness S is preserved.

 (3) Virtue ( fad.ı̄la): ∆P > 0—the action expands the space.
Remark E4.6 (On the Traditional Labels). The Arabic-Persian labels mazmūm, wazı̄fa, and fad.ı̄la
are the traditional names for the three categories. They are used here as structural labels, not as
ethical prescriptions in the traditional sense. The structural sense of each is fixed by Definition E4.5,
and no traditional use of the terms determines their sense here.
Definition E4.6 (Harmony of Possibility Space and Self-Awareness). (T) The possibility space
P(I, R) and the self-awareness S are in harmony iff:
 (a) every element of P that is selected by S is compatible with the structural constraints of S;

 (b) every constraint of S is reflected in P.
Remark E4.7 (Why Duty Is Not Mere Preservation). “Duty” in this taxonomy is not mere preser-
vation of the space. If an action preserves the space but breaks the harmony between the space and
the self-awareness, it is blameworthy—because the self-awareness can no longer select properly from
a disharmonious space, and the space is therefore effectively contracted. Duty is the preservation
of harmony, not merely of cardinality.
Theorem E4.4 (Completeness of the Threefold Taxonomy). (T) Every ethical action belongs to
exactly one of the three categories: blameworthy, duty, or virtue.
Proof. Let A be an ethical action. ∆P is either negative, zero, or positive. If negative, A is
blameworthy. If positive, A is virtue. If zero, either harmony is preserved (then A is duty) or
harmony is broken (then A is effectively contractive, and therefore blameworthy). In every case, A
belongs to exactly one category. The taxonomy is complete.

     Blame ∪ Duty ∪ Virtue = Aeth ,       Blame ∩ Duty = Duty ∩ Virtue = Virtue ∩ Blame = ∅.

Proof. By Principle E3.2, each trans-categorical has a structural role. By Definition E4.5, each

E4.10       The Three Levels and the Threefold Taxonomy

 (ii) Mean: compensate for a contextual contraction;

Proof. The three levels correspond to the three possible relations between an action and the space

E4.11       Soft Manifestation and the Action Chain
The previous sections defined action and ethical action. The present section refines the account by
distinguishing hard from soft manifestations, and by showing that an action is not complete until
its effect is received by a hard manifestation.

E4.11.1     Hard and Soft Manifestation
Definition E4.8 (Hard and Soft Manifestation). (A) Following the main volume ( Philosophy,
Ch. 35), a hard manifestation of an identity I in a realm R is a manifestation with structural
inertia: it resists change, and its persistence is a condition of unity. A soft manifestation of I in
R is a manifestation without structural inertia: it exists only as the act by which self-awareness
activates a hard manifestation.
Definition E4.9 (Soft Manifestation as Product). (A) A soft manifestation of an identity I in a
realm R is the product of a hard manifestation H(I, R) and the self-awareness S of I: it is the
action by which S activates H to emit an effect into R.
Remark E4.10 (Soft Manifestation Is Not Independent). The soft manifestation is not an indepen-
dent entity that exists alongside the hard manifestation. It is the product of the hard manifestation’s
activation by self-awareness. Without the hard manifestation, there is nothing to activate; with-
out the self-awareness, there is no activation. The soft manifestation is the act of the pair (hard
manifestation, self-awareness), not a third thing.

E4.11.2     The Action Chain
Definition E4.10 (Action Chain). (A) An action chain is a sequence of four stages:

  (i) Emission: S activates H to emit a signal σ into R;

 (ii) Transmission: σ travels through R via the fluid element (light);

(iii) Reception: σ meets a hard manifestation H′ in R;

(iv) Effect: H′ is modified by σ.

The action is actual only when all four stages occur. Before the fourth stage, the action is potential:
an emitted signal that has not yet landed.

Example E4.1 (The Radio Station). A radio employee, out of mischief, announces that extrater-
restrials are attacking. The announcement is an emission (stage i): the employee’s voice, a hard
manifestation, is activated by her self-awareness. The radio waves travel through the air (stage
ii): the transmission. As long as the waves are in the air and have not been received, the action
is potential, not actual. When a listener hears the announcement and panics, the action becomes
actual (stages iii–iv): the listener’s body and psychology, hard manifestations, are modified by the
signal. The panic, the rumor, and the social disruption are the effects of the action.
Remark E4.11 (The Radio Metaphor Is Structural). The radio metaphor is not decorative. It
makes visible the structure of the action chain: the action is not complete until the signal lands
on a hard manifestation. The metaphor distinguishes three things that are easily run together:
the emission (the signal), the transmission (the medium), and the effect (the modification of the
receiver). The emission is the soft manifestation; the transmission is the fluid element (light); the
effect is the modification of the receiving hard manifestation.


E4.11.3     Crystallization of Effect
Definition E4.11 (Crystallization). (A) The crystallization of an action is the process by which
the effect of the action, initially a modification of a receiving hard manifestation, becomes itself a
hard manifestation in the realm: a sediment, a habit, a structure, an institution.

Example E4.2 (The Panic as Sediment). The panic that follows the radio announcement is initially
a soft effect (a modification of the listeners’ bodies and psychologies). Over time, it crystallizes:
it becomes a habit of fear (a hard manifestation in the listeners), a rumor (a hard manifestation
in the relational realm), and a social disruption (a hard manifestation in the material realm). The
action has become sediment (Philosophy, Definition 36.1).

Principle E4.4 (The Crystallization Principle). (T) Every actual action tends toward crystalliza-
tion: its effect, once received, becomes a hard manifestation in the realm, and the hard manifestation
becomes the condition of further soft manifestations.

Proof. By Definition E4.10, an actual action modifies a receiving hard manifestation. By Defini-
tion E4.8, a hard manifestation exhibits structural inertia: it resists change, and its persistence is
a condition of unity. Hence the modification, once received, tends to persist and to become itself a
hard manifestation. The principle follows.

E5.1      The Question
The main volume defines time as realm-relative succession (Philosophy, Def. 33.1) and argues for
the non-completion of expression (Philosophy, Principle 33.2). The present chapter refines this
account: time is not merely a structural feature of the realms; it is the product of a fundamental
self-awareness.


E5.2      The Argument from Causation
Principle E5.1 (Time Is Not Non-Phenomenal). (A) Time is not a non-phenomenal feature of
reality. It is the structure of succession that appears in the relation between cause and effect.

Proof. Causation is the relation of a cause to its effect. The cause is prior to the effect; the effect
is posterior to the cause. The priority and posteriority are the structure of succession. If time were
not the structure of succession, causation would have no temporal structure, and the priority of
the cause would be unintelligible. Hence time is the structure of succession, and the structure of
succession is a feature of the relation between cause and effect—that is, a phenomenal feature.

Principle E5.2 (Self-Awareness as the Scaffold). (A) Self-awareness is the fundamental scaffold
of existence. The chain of succession (time) presupposes a chooser (self-awareness); the chooser
presupposes a choice; the choice presupposes a space of possibilities. Hence self-awareness is prior
to time, and time is prior to the realized world.

Remark E5.1 (The Order of Priorities). The order is not temporal priority (which would presuppose
time). It is structural priority: self-awareness is the condition of possibility of time, and time is the
condition of possibility of the realized world. The priority is a priority of conditions, not a priority
in time.


E5.3      Fundamental and Human Self-Awareness
Principle E5.3 (Two Senses of Self-Awareness). (T) Fundamental self-awareness is the trans-
categorical condition of possibility of choice (Principle E3.2). Human self-awareness is the appear-
ance of the fundamental self-awareness in the Relational Manifestation.

Proof. The fundamental self-awareness is trans-categorical (Theorem E3.1). It cannot be a man-
ifestation. Human self-awareness is a manifestation (it appears in a realm). Hence the two are
distinct: one is the condition, the other is the appearance.

Remark E5.2 (The Distinction Resolves the Tension). Without the distinction, the claim that self-
awareness is fundamental would collapse into the claim that human self-awareness is fundamental,
which is clearly false (human self-awareness is a manifestation, dependent on the realm). The
distinction preserves the fundamentality of the fundamental self-awareness while acknowledging
the manifest character of human self-awareness.


E5.4      The Unconscious as Weak Self-Awareness
Principle E5.4 (The Unconscious). (T) The unconscious is not the absence of self-awareness; it
is a weak self-awareness—a self-awareness that does not fully regulate its relation to its source.

Proof. By Principle E5.2, self-awareness is the condition of possibility of choice. Every choice, even
an unconscious one, presupposes a chooser. Hence the unconscious cannot be the total absence of
self-awareness; it must be a mode of self-awareness in which the regulation is incomplete.


E5.5      Phenomenal and Structural Time
Principle E5.5 (Two Senses of Time). (A) Phenomenal time is the time we experience: the
succession of conscious states. Structural time is the order of succession of the realms ( Philosophy,
Def. 33.1). The two are distinct: phenomenal time is the product of fundamental self-awareness;
structural time is a feature of the realms.

Remark E5.3. The distinction is needed because the realms are not all temporal (Philosophy,
Ch. 33). The First Determination is timeless; the Structural Determination is eternal without
being temporal; the Material Manifestation is temporal. Phenomenal time, by contrast, is the time
of conscious experience, which is a manifestation in the Relational Manifestation. The two must
be kept apart.

Arguments for the New Axioms

E6.1      The Question
The present extension introduces three new axioms: the Totality of Possibility (Principle E3.3,
E3), the Fundamental Self-Awareness as Scaffold (Principle E5.2, E5), and the Expansion Criterion
(Principle E4.1, E4). The main volume’s methodology requires that every axiom be accompanied
by an argument, and that the argument state what would refute it. The present chapter supplies
the arguments.


E6.2      The Argument for the Totality of Possibility
Argument E6.1 (Transcendental Argument for Totality). (T) The concept of action presupposes
a space of possibilities from which to select. If there were no prior possibilities, action would
be creation ex nihilo, which would make the creature a creator—contradicting the framework’s
structural asymmetry between the Ground and the creature. Hence the space of possibilities must be
prior to action. The space must be exhaustive, because a possibility outside it would be uncreated—
contradicting the Ground’s exhaustive grounding of the modal space (Principle E3.4). Therefore
the modal space is total.

Remark E6.1 (What Would Refute the Argument). The argument would be refuted by showing
that action can be creation ex nihilo without a prior space of possibilities, or that there can be
a possibility outside the modal space. The framework does not claim that no such case can be
described; it claims that none has been described, and that the structural asymmetry between the
Ground and the creature rules it out.


E6.3      The Argument for the Scaffold
Argument E6.2 (Transcendental Argument for Scaffold). (T) Time presupposes succession; suc-
cession presupposes a chooser; a chooser presupposes self-awareness. If the chooser were a mani-
festation, it would be in time, and would presuppose another chooser—a regress. Hence the chooser
must be trans-categorical. This is the same regress as in Theorem E3.1. Therefore fundamental
self-awareness is the scaffold of time.

Remark E6.2 (What Would Refute the Argument). The argument would be refuted by showing
that time can exist without a chooser (e.g., a purely physical time), or that the chooser can be a

manifestation without regress. The framework does not claim that no such case can be described;
it claims that none has been described, and that the regress is vicious.


E6.4      The Argument for the Expansion Criterion
Argument E6.3 (Structural Argument for Expansion). (T) Viability is the condition of possibility
of the good (Principle E4.2). Expansion of the possibility space is expansion of viability. Hence
expansion is structurally good. The argument is conditional: if the good is to be possible, then
expansion is required. The normative premise (that the good is to be possible) is supplied by the
inquiry into the good, not by the structural framework.

Remark E6.3 (What Would Refute the Argument). The argument would be refuted by showing
that expansion of the possibility space is not correlated with viability, or that viability is not the
condition of possibility of the good. The framework does not claim that no such case can be
described; it claims that none has been described, and that the Bridge Principle (Principle E4.2)
is the structural link.
Conclusion

This document has developed seven structural extensions to The Philosophy of Viability:

  (I) The topology of containment (§E1), which gives a precise structural meaning to the relation
      among the seven realms and to the descent from higher to lower.

 (II) The categorization of the hubs (§E2), which interprets the three barzakhic realms as categories
      of transition and as conditions of cognition.

(III) The trans-categorical triad (§E3): light, identity, and self-awareness as the three conditions
      of possibility of relation, succession, and choice.

(IV) The ontological status of the modal space (§E3.5): the three ontological layers (Ω, X , R),
     the epistemic inaccessibility of Ω, the Molinist refinement of omniscience, and the structural
     resolution of the tension between divine omniscience and creaturely freedom.

 (V) The thread-and-beads interpretation of identity (§E3), with the conservation theorem that
     follows from it.

(VI) The definition of action as change of possibility space (§E4), the distinction between action,
     contextual act, and ethical action (§E4.3), and the threefold ethical taxonomy (§E4.7) that
     refines the expansion criterion into blameworthy, duty, and virtue.

(VII) The refinement of time and self-awareness (§E5), distinguishing fundamental from human
      self-awareness and phenomenal from structural time.

    Each extension is a structural claim, not a normative one. Each is offered at the strength of
its argument. Each is integrated into the architecture of the main volume by being placed under
the same epistemic discipline: the axioms are declared, the arguments are given, the estimates are
marked, and the open questions are indicated.
    The horizon is open. The inquiry continues.

Appendix C

Open Questions

Open Question C.1 (Trade-off). How is the sum of possibility spaces to be evaluated when cardi-
nality and structure do not vary in the same direction?
Open Question C.2 (Negative Responsibility). Is the failure to act (when action was possible and
would have expanded the space) itself a contractive action?
Open Question C.3 (Infinite Network). How is an action to be evaluated in an infinite network of
affected identities, given that complete evaluation is impossible (Philosophy, Principle 6.1)?
Open Question C.4 (The Ground). What is the precise structure of the containment of both Ω
and the realms in the Ground—whether it is a single containment relation or a family of analogous
relations, and whether the Ground’s mode of containment is itself a mode of existence or a condition
of possibility?
Open Question C.5 (Fundamental Self-Awareness). Is fundamental self-awareness identical with
the divine self-awareness, or is it a structural condition that the divine self-awareness grounds?
Open Question C.6 (Time). Is structural time a feature of the realms independent of any subject,
or is it a feature of the relation between the realms and fundamental self-awareness?
Open Question C.7 (The Boundary of Duty). If an action preserves the space of possible realization
but improves the harmony between the space and self-awareness (without increasing cardinality),
is it duty or virtue? Definition E4.5 classifies it as duty (since ∆P = 0), but the improvement of
harmony is a kind of structural expansion. Should it be classified as virtue?
Open Question C.8 (The Agent of Contextual Act). Is a contextual act always without an agent?
Or can it have an agent whose will is not deliberate (e.g., an unintentional action)? In the latter
case, is a reduced ethical judgment (e.g., limited responsibility) applicable?
Open Question C.9 (The Unity of the Three Categories). Are the three categories of the threefold
taxonomy independent or continuous? Can an action be simultaneously blameworthy and virtuous
(e.g., contracting the space of one identity while expanding another)? If so, how is the structural
trade-off principle to be applied?
Open Question C.10 (The Boundary of Potential Action). If an action is potential until it lands,
what is the ethical status of the attempt? Does the attempt count as an action on the emitter
(which is actual) or as a failed action (which is potential)? And how is the ethical evaluation of
the attempt related to the ethical evaluation of the completed action?
