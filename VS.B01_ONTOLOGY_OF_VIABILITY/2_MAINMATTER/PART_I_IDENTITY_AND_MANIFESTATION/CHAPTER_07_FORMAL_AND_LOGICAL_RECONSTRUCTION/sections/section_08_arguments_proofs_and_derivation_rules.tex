\section{Arguments, Proofs, and Derivation Rules}
\label{sec:arguments-proofs-derivation-rules}

\subsection{The Problem of Justified Inference}

A formal reconstruction requires more than a vocabulary, a set of
definitions, and a collection of propositions. It also requires an
account of how claims are supported by other claims. Without such an
account, the relation between foundational commitments, axiomatic
formulations, and derived propositions remains insufficiently
specified. A formal system may contain individually intelligible
statements while leaving unclear whether one statement genuinely
follows from another, whether an inference depends on an unstated
assumption, or whether a conclusion exceeds the scope of its premises.

The central task of this section is therefore to clarify the
conditions under which an argument supports a conclusion and a
formal derivation establishes that a conclusion follows within a
specified system.

Three questions must be kept distinct:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Inferential question:} Does the conclusion follow
    from the stated premises according to the adopted rules of
    inference?

    \item \textbf{Interpretive question:} Do the premises and
    conclusion retain the intended meaning when interpreted in the
    relevant domain?

    \item \textbf{Ontological question:} Are the premises and the
    conclusion adequately justified as claims about being and
    identity?
\end{enumerate}

These questions are connected, but none can simply replace the
others. A formally valid derivation may begin from an inadequately
justified premise. An ontologically plausible commitment may be
expressed through an invalid argument. An interpretation may assign
meanings to formal expressions that do not preserve the distinctions
required by the ontological inquiry.

The governing thesis of this section is that formal derivation
establishes inferential dependence under specified assumptions and
rules. It does not, by itself, establish the ontological truth of
those assumptions or authorize conclusions beyond their conceptual
and ontological scope.

\subsection{Argument, Proof, and Derivation}

An argument is an ordered presentation of premises and a conclusion
in which the premises are offered as support for the conclusion.
Arguments may be expressed in ordinary language, symbolic notation,
or a combination of both. Their adequacy depends on the clarity of
their premises, the legitimacy of their inferential transitions,
and the relevance of their conclusion to the question under
examination.

A derivation is a formally regulated sequence of expressions in
which each step is licensed by an inference rule, a premise, or an
explicitly permitted assumption. A proof is a derivation that
establishes a specified conclusion relative to a formal system and
its assumptions.

The distinction is important. An argument may contain informal
explanatory steps that have not yet been formalized. A derivation
makes its inferential structure explicit. A proof is successful
only relative to the rules and assumptions that govern that
derivation.

Let \(\Gamma\) denote a set of premises and let \(\varphi\) denote
a conclusion. The expression

\[
    \Gamma \vdash_{\mathcal{L}} \varphi
\]

indicates that \(\varphi\) is derivable from \(\Gamma\) within a
specified formal system \(\mathcal{L}\). The symbol \(\vdash\)
expresses a relation of formal derivability. It does not, by itself,
assert that the premises are true in reality.

A semantic consequence relation may be represented as

\[
    \Gamma \models_{\mathcal{M}} \varphi,
\]

where \(\mathcal{M}\) specifies the relevant class of interpretations
or models. This notation indicates that every interpretation
satisfying the premises also satisfies the conclusion, subject to
the semantic conditions of the system.

The two relations answer different questions. Derivability concerns
what can be established through the rules of a calculus; semantic
consequence concerns what holds across the interpretations admitted
by a semantics. Their relationship depends on the formal system.
Soundness and completeness, where established, characterize that
relationship. Neither property independently establishes that the
chosen interpretation captures the ontological subject matter
adequately.

\subsection{Premises and Their Justificatory Roles}

Not every premise enters an argument with the same justificatory
standing. A formal derivation may use several kinds of premises, but
their roles must remain identifiable.

For the purposes of this reconstruction, the following distinctions
are necessary.

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Foundational commitments} express adopted
    ontological commitments that orient the inquiry. They are not
    automatically formal axioms, and their adoption does not mean
    that every possible formal representation of them is already
    settled.

    \item \textbf{Definitions and stipulations} establish the
    meanings or permitted uses of expressions within a specified
    framework. They constrain interpretation and substitution, but
    they do not establish the existence or truth of what their terms
    describe.

    \item \textbf{Formal axioms} are expressions accepted as starting
    points within a formal calculus. Their role in derivation must
    not be confused with an independent demonstration of their
    ontological adequacy.

    \item \textbf{Previously established propositions} may be used
    as premises in subsequent derivations, provided that their
    derivations, assumptions, and scope remain available for
    examination.

    \item \textbf{Conditional assumptions} are introduced for a
    restricted argument. Their consequences remain conditional
    unless the assumptions receive independent justification.

    \item \textbf{Hypotheses} provide provisional starting points
    for examination. A conclusion derived from a hypothesis does
    not acquire a stronger justificatory status merely because the
    derivation is valid.
\end{enumerate}

A derivation must therefore disclose not only which premises it
uses, but also the role and status assigned to them. Otherwise, a
provisional assumption may silently acquire the appearance of an
established ontological commitment.

This requirement is especially important when a formal system
contains both adopted ontological principles and representational
conventions. A convention may be indispensable to the calculus
without being an ontological claim. Conversely, an ontological
commitment may guide the construction of a calculus without being
reducible to any single formal expression.

\subsection{Basic Rules of Inference}

A formal derivation requires explicit rules governing the transition
from premises to conclusions. The following rules illustrate
elementary inferential operations that may be used in a suitable
classical propositional or first-order framework. Their inclusion
does not establish that every subsequent formal reconstruction must
adopt one complete logical system without qualification.

\paragraph{Modus ponens.}

From a conditional statement and its antecedent, the consequent
may be inferred:

\[
\frac{\varphi \rightarrow \psi \qquad \varphi}
     {\psi}
\]

This rule permits the derivation of \(\psi\) when both
\(\varphi \rightarrow \psi\) and \(\varphi\) are available as
premises or previously established expressions.

The rule does not establish the truth of the conditional statement
or its antecedent. It specifies only the inferential legitimacy of
the transition.

\paragraph{Conjunction introduction.}

From two established expressions, their conjunction may be
introduced:

\[
\frac{\varphi \qquad \psi}
     {\varphi \land \psi}
\]

The resulting conjunction records the joint availability of the
two expressions. It does not establish that they are identical,
inseparable in an ontological sense, or related by any additional
structure not expressed in the premises.

\paragraph{Conjunction elimination.}

Either conjunct may be inferred from a conjunction:

\[
\frac{\varphi \land \psi}
     {\varphi}
\qquad
\frac{\varphi \land \psi}
     {\psi}
\]

The rule permits the recovery of a component of a conjunction.
It does not establish that the recovered expressions exhaust the
meaning of the original statement.

\paragraph{Universal instantiation.}

Within an appropriate first-order system, a universally quantified
statement permits substitution of an admissible individual term:

\[
\frac{\forall x\,\varphi(x)}
     {\varphi(a)}
\]

The substitution must respect the domain, variable-binding
conditions, and interpretation of the formal language. It cannot
be used to introduce an individual that the domain or interpretation
does not admit.

\paragraph{Conditional derivation.}

A conditional may be established by temporarily assuming its
antecedent and deriving its consequent:

\[
\begin{array}{c}
    [\varphi] \\
    \vdots \\
    \psi
\end{array}
\quad
\Longrightarrow
\quad
\varphi \rightarrow \psi
\]

The assumption is discharged at the conclusion of the derivation.
The resulting conditional states what follows if the antecedent
holds; it does not assert the antecedent independently.

These rules are elementary examples rather than an exhaustive
specification of the logic required by the entire book. The choice
of a formal calculus, its semantics, and its admissible extensions
must be made in accordance with the distinctions the ontological
analysis is intended to preserve.

\subsection{Derivation from the Three Foundational Principles}

The three foundational principles introduced in the preceding
sections provide an important test of inferential discipline. They
are analytically distinct commitments, and their formal treatment
must not allow one principle to acquire the content of another
through an unexamined inference.

Let the principles be designated as follows:

\[
    AD = \text{Analytical Distinction},
\]

\[
    OI = \text{Ontological Inseparability},
\]

\[
    AP = \text{Absence of Ontological Priority}.
\]

Their joint adoption may be represented schematically as

\[
    F_{BI} := AD \land OI \land AP.
\]

This expression records their conjunction as adopted commitments.
It does not establish that any one of them is derivable from the
other two. Nor does it establish that their conjunction exhausts
every commitment required for a complete account of being and
identity.

The formal expressions introduced for these principles in the
preceding section must be interpreted according to their stated
limits.

\paragraph{Analytical Distinction.}

The expression

\[
    q_B \neq q_I
\]

records a difference between the analytical questions or roles
associated with being and identity. It licenses the conclusion
that the inquiry must preserve two distinguishable analytical
orientations.

It does not license the conclusion that being and identity are
two independently existing entities. Such a conclusion would
require an additional premise concerning ontological separation,
and it would conflict with the intended distinction between
analytical differentiation and ontological independence.

Nor does the expression establish that every property, predicate,
or explanatory treatment associated with being must differ from
every one associated with identity. The distinction concerns
analytical roles and must not be indiscriminately extended to
all possible predicates.

\paragraph{Ontological Inseparability.}

The co-applicability schema introduced previously may be written
as

\[
    \forall x \in D_E
    \bigl(E(x) \rightarrow (B(x) \land I(x))\bigr).
\]

Within its intended domain, this schema states that an existent
to which the relevant existential predicate applies is represented
as involving both being and identity.

A valid application of universal instantiation and modus ponens
may yield:

\[
    E(a) \rightarrow (B(a) \land I(a)),
\]

and, given \(E(a)\), the conditional consequence

\[
    B(a) \land I(a).
\]

This derivation establishes co-applicability under the specified
formalization. It does not, by itself, capture the full ontological
meaning of inseparability. In particular, the conjunction does
not establish that being and identity are identical, that their
conceptual roles are interchangeable, or that every possible
relation between them has been formally exhausted.

The stronger commitment represented by ontological inseparability
therefore remains dependent on its ontological interpretation and
cannot be replaced by the co-applicability schema alone.

\paragraph{Absence of Ontological Priority.}

The formal expression

\[
    \neg(B \prec_O I)
    \land
    \neg(I \prec_O B)
\]

denies the two specified directions of ontological priority.
It can be decomposed, by conjunction elimination, into:

\[
    \neg(B \prec_O I)
\]

and

\[
    \neg(I \prec_O B).
\]

These consequences are limited to the priority relation
\(\prec_O\) as defined for the relevant analysis. They do not
establish the absence of every conceivable relation between
being and identity.

In particular, the denial of ontological priority does not
automatically deny conceptual dependence, explanatory order,
temporal succession, causal relations, epistemic dependence, or
other relations that may be relevant under different descriptions.
Each such relation requires its own definition and examination.

\subsection{The Difference Between Derivation and Ontological
Commitment}

The preceding examples demonstrate a general constraint: a
formal expression can support only those conclusions licensed by
its logical form, its premises, and its interpretation.

This constraint prevents several recurrent inferential errors.

First, a conjunction of principles does not demonstrate their
mutual equivalence. The expression

\[
    AD \land OI \land AP
\]

does not entail, without additional premises, that

\[
    AD \leftrightarrow OI,
\]

or that either principle is reducible to another.

Second, a difference between analytical questions does not entail
the existence of separate ontological entities. The inference from

\[
    q_B \neq q_I
\]

to a claim of ontological separation would require an independent
bridge premise. Without such a premise, the inference is invalid.

Third, co-applicability does not establish conceptual identity.
From

\[
    B(a) \land I(a)
\]

one cannot infer

\[
    B(a) \leftrightarrow I(a)
\]

without additional premises. The conjunction asserts the
co-occurrence of two formal predicates in the specified
representation; the biconditional asserts a stronger relation
between their conditions of application.

Fourth, the denial of priority in two directions does not establish
the absence of all possible relations. The absence of a particular
ordering relation cannot be generalized into the absence of every
form of dependence or connection.

Finally, a conclusion does not inherit a stronger epistemic status
than its premises merely through valid inference. If a conclusion
depends on a provisional hypothesis, it remains conditional upon
that hypothesis unless further justification is supplied.

\subsection{Soundness, Validity, and the Status of Premises}

Validity concerns the relation between premises and conclusion.
An argument is deductively valid when it is impossible, under the
relevant logical interpretation, for all its premises to be true
and its conclusion false.

Soundness requires more. A sound argument is valid and has premises
that are true under the relevant interpretation.

These distinctions are indispensable for ontological reconstruction.
A formally valid argument may preserve inferential correctness
while relying on a premise that has not been established. In that
case, the derivation is valid, but the conclusion is not thereby
established as an unconditional ontological truth.

The distinction can be represented schematically:

\[
\text{Validity}
=
\text{preservation of truth from premises to conclusion},
\]

\[
\text{Soundness}
=
\text{Validity}
+
\text{truth of the premises}.
\]

The second expression is schematic and presupposes a sufficiently
specified semantics. In ontological inquiry, the truth and
adequacy of premises also require examination of whether their
interpretation properly addresses the intended subject matter.

Consequently, three assessments should be recorded separately:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item whether the inferential transition is formally valid;
    \item whether the premises are justified at the level claimed;
    \item whether the interpretation of the formal expressions
    preserves the intended ontological distinctions.
\end{enumerate}

A positive answer to the first question cannot substitute for
positive answers to the other two.

\subsection{Conditional Derivation and the Scope of Conclusions}

A conditional derivation is particularly important when the
reconstruction examines alternative formal interpretations,
provisional assumptions, or hypotheses whose status remains
unresolved.

Let \(\Delta\) be a set of assumptions introduced for a restricted
argument. If

\[
    \Delta \cup \Gamma
    \vdash_{\mathcal{L}}
    \varphi,
\]

then the conclusion \(\varphi\) is derivable relative to both
\(\Delta\) and \(\Gamma\).

If the assumptions in \(\Delta\) are later withdrawn, the conclusion
cannot be retained as an unconditional result merely because its
derivation was valid. Its status must remain conditional unless
the relevant assumptions are independently established or the
conclusion can be derived without them.

This requirement also applies to formal alternatives. Suppose a
proposed account of identity introduces a particular relation,
structure, or condition. A consequence derived from that proposal
may be formally correct while remaining dependent on the proposal's
acceptance. It must not be presented as a necessary consequence
of the foundational commitments if those commitments do not
themselves entail it.

A useful distinction therefore exists between:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Unconditional derivation:} the conclusion follows
    from the adopted premises and accepted rules without relying
    on unresolved assumptions.

    \item \textbf{Conditional derivation:} the conclusion follows
    only if one or more additional assumptions are granted.

    \item \textbf{Interpretive consequence:} the conclusion follows
    under a specified interpretation of the formal expressions,
    but that interpretation itself requires justification.

    \item \textbf{Hypothetical consequence:} the conclusion follows
    from a hypothesis whose justificatory status remains
    provisional.
\end{itemize}

These categories concern the dependence and scope of conclusions.
They do not replace the separate classification of their
functional or logical form and their epistemic or justificatory
status.

\subsection{Hidden Premises and Inferential Completeness}

Arguments often appear stronger than they are because a necessary
premise remains unstated. This problem is especially serious when
ordinary-language expressions are translated into formal notation.

For example, a statement that two aspects are inseparable may be
interpreted in several ways: as co-applicability, mutual
implication, necessary co-presence, constitutive dependence, or
the denial of independent ontological separation. These readings
are not automatically equivalent.

A derivation that moves from one reading to another without an
explicit premise introduces an unacknowledged inferential step.
The apparent proof then depends on a change in meaning rather than
on a valid rule of inference.

To prevent this, every substantial argument should satisfy the
following requirements:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Premise disclosure:} all premises essential to the
    conclusion must be stated or explicitly identified.

    \item \textbf{Rule disclosure:} each inferential transition
    must be licensed by an identifiable rule or justified
    explanatory step.

    \item \textbf{Scope preservation:} the conclusion must not
    extend beyond the domain, conditions, or qualifications of
    its premises.

    \item \textbf{Meaning preservation:} the same expression must
    not silently change its meaning during the argument.

    \item \textbf{Assumption tracking:} provisional and conditional
    assumptions must remain identifiable throughout the derivation.

    \item \textbf{Status preservation:} the justificatory status
    of a conclusion must reflect the status of the premises on
    which it depends.

    \item \textbf{Counterexample sensitivity:} where appropriate,
    the argument must be examined against interpretations or
    cases that challenge its claimed generality.
\end{enumerate}

These requirements do not demand that every philosophical argument
be converted into a fully symbolic proof. They demand that its
inferential structure be sufficiently explicit to permit critical
examination.

\subsection{The Role of Counterexamples in Evaluating Arguments}

A counterexample is a case that satisfies the relevant premises
while failing to satisfy the proposed conclusion, or that
demonstrates that a claimed generalization does not hold under
its stated conditions.

Counterexamples are particularly useful for distinguishing
a genuinely valid inference from a conclusion that merely appears
plausible because familiar cases happen to support it.

Consider a proposed inference from the plurality of components
within an existent to the plurality of identities associated
with it. The inference cannot be accepted without further
argument. A plurality of components may be compatible with a
single identity, multiple identities, or an unresolved account
of identity plurality. The formal or conceptual relation between
component plurality and identity plurality must therefore be
specified rather than presumed.

Likewise, a change in a manifested configuration does not, by
itself, establish either the persistence or the loss of identity.
A valid argument about such a change requires an account of the
relevant identity conditions and the respect in which the change
is being evaluated.

Counterexamples do not necessarily refute a foundational
commitment. They may instead expose an inadequate definition,
an overextended proposition, an unstated assumption, or a
misleading formal representation. Their function is diagnostic:
they identify where the inferential structure requires
qualification or reconstruction.

\subsection{Arguments That Cross Conceptual Levels}

The ontological reconstruction involves several distinguishable
levels of articulation: ontological commitments, conceptual
formulations, and formal representations. Arguments that move
between these levels require particular care.

A formal expression does not become an ontological commitment
merely because it is symbolically precise. A conceptual definition
does not establish that its defined object exists. An ontological
commitment does not determine a unique formal calculus unless
additional representational decisions are supplied.

Any argument that moves between these levels must therefore
identify the bridge that licenses the transition.

For example, a formal predicate may be introduced to represent
a concept. The use of that predicate in a derivation establishes
consequences within the representation. To claim that the same
consequences hold ontologically, however, requires an account
of why the representation is adequate to the relevant ontological
structure.

This does not make formalization philosophically irrelevant.
On the contrary, formalization can expose hidden commitments,
clarify inferential dependencies, reveal incompatibilities, and
make the consequences of alternative formulations easier to
compare. Its contribution depends on preserving the distinction
between what a representation establishes formally and what
the ontology warrants.

\subsection{Standards for Accepting a Derived Proposition}

A derived proposition should be accepted only after its inferential
and justificatory conditions have been examined.

The following standards provide a common basis for that
examination:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Traceability:} the premises from which the
    proposition is derived can be identified.

    \item \textbf{Inferential validity:} the transition from
    premises to conclusion is licensed by the adopted rules
    or by an adequately explained argument.

    \item \textbf{Semantic adequacy:} the interpretation of the
    expressions preserves their intended meanings and domains.

    \item \textbf{Ontological relevance:} the proposition addresses
    the ontological question for which it is introduced.

    \item \textbf{Scope control:} the proposition does not claim
    more than its premises and interpretation support.

    \item \textbf{Assumption transparency:} any additional
    assumptions are disclosed and their status is recorded.

    \item \textbf{Consistency:} the proposition does not introduce
    an unresolved contradiction with the commitments and
    propositions it is intended to preserve.

    \item \textbf{Revisability:} the derivation remains open to
    examination, including the possibility that its premises,
    rules, or interpretation require revision.
\end{enumerate}

These standards distinguish a formally derivable expression from
a proposition adequately established within the philosophical
reconstruction. They also permit a conclusion to be retained
as a conditional or provisional result without prematurely
promoting it to an adopted ontological commitment.

\subsection{Open Problems Concerning Derivation}

The present account establishes general requirements for
arguments and proofs, but several matters remain open.

First, the precise formal calculus appropriate to the complete
reconstruction has not yet been conclusively selected. Classical
first-order logic provides useful resources for many of the
formal distinctions developed here, but its universal adoption
would require further examination of whether its expressive
and interpretive assumptions are adequate for every relevant
ontological question.

Second, the formal representation of ontological inseparability
requires further development. Co-applicability is one aspect
of the principle, but a complete account must explain whether
and how its stronger ontological content can be represented
without collapsing being and identity into equivalence or
identity of predicates.

Third, the relations between ontological priority and other
forms of dependence require more precise treatment. The denial
of priority in the specified ontological sense does not settle
the status of conceptual, explanatory, causal, temporal, or
epistemic relations.

Fourth, the conditions under which formal results can be
transferred between alternative interpretations remain to be
specified. Such transfer requires a clear account of what is
preserved, what is changed, and whether the interpretations
remain adequate to the same ontological subject matter.

Fifth, the treatment of identity plurality requires additional
argument. Neither the plurality of components nor the plurality
of formal descriptions is sufficient to establish the plurality
of identities. The question must remain open until its relevant
conditions and possible forms are examined.

These open problems do not invalidate the inferential standards
established in this section. They identify the limits of the
present reconstruction and indicate where further formal and
ontological work is required.

\subsection{Concluding Principle}

Arguments and proofs are indispensable to a rigorous ontological
reconstruction because they make inferential dependence explicit.
Their value lies not in the appearance of mathematical precision,
but in their capacity to expose assumptions, clarify consequences,
and permit claims to be examined according to identifiable
standards.

The governing principle may be stated as follows:

\begin{quote}
A conclusion is formally established only to the extent that it
follows from disclosed premises through legitimate inferential
steps; it is ontologically warranted only to the extent that
those premises, the interpretation of the formal expressions,
and the scope of the conclusion are independently justified.
\end{quote}

This principle preserves the distinction between formal validity
and ontological warrant while allowing each to contribute to the
other's critical examination. It also establishes the methodological
basis for the next stage of the reconstruction: the examination
of how individual claims depend upon one another within a larger
logical structure.
