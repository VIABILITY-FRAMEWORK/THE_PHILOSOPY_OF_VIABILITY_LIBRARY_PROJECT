The Introduction and Methodology must clearly distinguish ontology from epistemology. The cognitive four-column framework developed in earlier formulations must not be imported as the foundation of this volume.

### Chapter 01 — Identity

Examines the ontological problem of identity and its relation to Being.

Principal contents:

- The ontological problem of identity.
- Being and identity.
- Analytical distinction between Being and identity.
- Ontological inseparability.
- Absence of ontological priority.
- Identity and determination.
- Identity and unity.
- Identity and distinction.
- Identity and individuation.
- Identity and numerical identity.
- Identity and signature.
- The non-reduction of identity.
- Scope and limits of identity.

**Central question:** What is the ontological status of identity, and how is it related to the existence of that which is identity-bearing?

### Chapter 02 — Invariance

Examines invariance as a distinct ontological and conceptual problem.

Principal contents:

- The ontological problem of invariance.
- Invariance and identity.
- Defining invariants.
- Invariance under transformation.
- Invariance and difference.
- Invariance and structure.
- Invariance and composition.
- Invariance and continuity.
- Forms and domains of invariance.
- Limits of invariance.
- The relation between invariance and identity preservation.

Every claim of invariance must identify the transformation, domain, respect, type, and limits involved.

### Chapter 03 — Unity and Plurality

Examines the ontological significance of unity and the possibility of plurality.

Principal contents:

- The ontological problem of unity and plurality.
- Unity of identity.
- Unity of source.
- Aggregate and aggregation.
- Composition and unity.
- Plurality of identities.
- Identity and multiplicity.
- Unity and singularity.
- Limits of unity and plurality.

The chapter must leave open the possibility of plurality of identities where the arguments do not establish a definitive conclusion.

### Chapter 04 — Expression

Examines expression and its relation to identity and manifestation.

Principal contents:

- The ontological problem of expression.
- Identity and expression.
- Expression and determination.
- Expression and invariance.
- Expression and source.
- Expression and multiplicity.
- Expression and manifestation.
- Limits of expression.

The chapter must establish a positive account of expression while preserving its distinction from identity and manifestation.

### Chapter 05 — Identity and Continuity

Examines identity in relation to persistence, continuity, and change.

Principal contents:

- The problem of identity and continuity.
- Identity and persistence.
- Invariance and continuity.
- Identity and difference through change.
- Conditions of identity preservation.
- Limits of identity continuity.
- Identity through transformation.

The chapter must not assume that persistence, continuity, or the preservation of an invariant feature automatically establishes identity.

### Chapter 06 — Synthesis of Identity

Integrates the conceptual results of Part I.

Principal contents:

- Integrated account of identity.
- Foundational principles.
- Identity-related propositions.
- Relations among the concepts developed in Chapters 01–05.
- Established conclusions and their justifications.
- Conditionally supported claims.
- Provisional constructions.
- Open ontological questions.
- Transition to the realms of Being.

This chapter is the conceptual synthesis. It does not replace the formal reconstruction or comparative examination that follow.

### Chapter 07 — Formal and Logical Reconstruction of Identity and Manifestation

Reconstructs the conceptual system of Part I in a rigorous formal and logical form.

Principal contents:

- Formal language and notation.
- Ontological domains and variables.
- Formal definitions.
- Foundational commitments and axioms.
- Identity-related principles.
- Propositions and theorems.
- Arguments and derivations.
- Proofs and proof dependencies.
- Logical consistency.
- Independence and dependence of principles.
- Formal models and countermodels.
- Counterexamples.
- Alternative formalizations.
- Limits of formalization.
- Classification of claims according to logical form and justificatory status.

A statement must not be presented as a theorem unless it has actually been demonstrated under explicitly stated assumptions.

### Chapter 08 — Comparative Philosophical Examination and Dialectical Controversies

Examines the philosophical foundations of Part I through direct and critical engagement with relevant philosophical traditions.

Principal contents:

- Identity in Aristotle and the classical Western tradition.
- Being and identity in Avicenna.
- Individuation and identity in medieval philosophy.
- Identity and substance in early modern philosophy.
- Personal identity and numerical identity in contemporary philosophy.
- Identity and difference in Hegel and other relevant traditions.
- Unity, plurality, and identity in Indian and Buddhist philosophy.
- Identity, change, and continuity in Chinese philosophical traditions.
- Relevant contemporary metaphysical theories.

For each selected philosopher or theory:

1. Reconstruct the original position using its actual sources.
2. Identify the shared philosophical question.
3. Present the strongest objection that the position raises against the Philosophy of Viability.
4. Develop the response using arguments actually established in Part I.
5. Present the Philosophy of Viability's strongest objection to the opposing position.
6. Reconstruct the strongest possible defence of that position.
7. Continue the exchange through further objections and replies.
8. Evaluate agreements, disagreements, unresolved problems, and possible revisions on both sides.

The chapter must not become a general history of philosophy or a collection of superficial comparisons.

- Buddhist accounts of dependent origination and relational existence.
- Daoist accounts of differentiation, unity, and transformation.
- Relevant contemporary theories of structure, relations, and material ontology.

Each comparison must be developed through documented positions, reciprocal objections, substantive replies, and a critical evaluation of unresolved differences.

The proposed four realms and three hubs must not be declared equivalent to the structures of other philosophers merely because certain terms or functions appear similar.

- Plato's account of the Good and intelligibility.

- Contemporary philosophical accounts of emergence, disclosure, and ontological manifestation.

The chapter must distinguish the historical and philosophical meanings of Light in each tradition. It must not assume that the Philosophy of Viability's concept of Light is identical to any historical concept of illumination.

# V. THE OVERALL LOGIC OF THE BOOK

The four Parts form a connected philosophical investigation:

**Part I — Identity and Manifestation**

Establishes what must be examined concerning Being, identity, determination, unity, plurality, expression, and continuity.

**Part II — The Realms of Being**

Investigates how Being may be differentiated and articulated through fundamental realms and intermediary hubs.

**Part III — Light**

Investigates Light as an ontological problem and examines its possible relations to Being, identity, manifestation, and the realms.

**Part IV — Dynamics of Being**

Investigates becoming, interaction, emergence, transformation, temporality, generation, dissolution, and the consequences of dynamic processes.

The final two chapters of each Part provide the formal reconstruction and comparative examination of that Part. Chapter 39 then integrates the formal structure of the whole book, while Chapter 40 examines the complete ontology through sustained philosophical controversy.

# VI. FINAL STRUCTURAL PRINCIPLES

1. The book contains **four Parts and forty chapters**.
2. Every Part contains a conceptual exposition followed by a formal and logical reconstruction and a comparative philosophical examination.
3. The formal and comparative chapters are independent chapters, not appendices or summaries.
4. The conceptual chapters must develop the philosophical arguments before those arguments are formally reconstructed or comparatively examined.
5. The final synthesis chapter of each Part must identify established conclusions, provisional claims, and unresolved questions.
6. The four Parts are conceptually connected, but their order must not be mistaken for proof of a linear ontological hierarchy.
7. The proposed seven domains in Part II remain subject to philosophical examination.
8. Light remains an ontological problem whose status must be established through argument.
9. The dynamics of Being must be developed without reducing them to empirical movement or observable change.
10. The positive account of viability must emerge from the ontological work rather than being imposed as a predetermined definition.
11. The cognitive four-column framework belongs to a separate epistemological investigation and must not serve as the foundation of Book I.
12. The structure of the book organizes the philosophical investigation; it does not itself establish the truth of the ontology.
