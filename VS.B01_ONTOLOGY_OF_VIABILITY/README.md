# 2. Mission, Vision and the Book's Role in the Whole System

## 2.1 What the book is

Book I establishes the **ontological foundation** of the Philosophy of Viability. It investigates the conditions, structures, relations, manifestations and dynamics through which being becomes determinate, maintains identity and undergoes transformation.

It is **not** a mere introduction to a position. It is a systematic reconstruction and articulation of an ontological framework. The goal is not text that is merely tidy, elegant or academic. The goal is an ontological system that is **coherent, independent, precise, argued, and theoretically productive**. Every philosophical, stylistic, bibliographic, structural, technical and visual decision serves that goal.

## 2.2 Central concerns

- Being and identity.
- Identity and invariance.
- Unity and plurality.
- Expression and manifestation.
- Persistence, continuity and change.
- The architecture of the realms of being (four realms, three hubs).
- The ontology of light.
- The dynamics of being (becoming, emergence, transformation, generation, dissolution, reversibility, cost, residue, consequence).
- The limits and conditions of ontological determination.

## 2.3 Place within the larger system

- `VS` = Viability System. Book I is the ontological base on which later books build.
- Epistemological topics (knowledge, mind, perception, language, self-consciousness, ignorance, and the **cognitive four-column framework**) belong mainly to a **separate epistemological investigation (Book II)**. They MUST NOT serve as the foundation of Book I.
- Do not import material from other books or volumes unless the author explicitly authorizes it.
- Book I prepares the ground for the theory of viability but MUST NOT force a premature or unsupported definition of Viability (see §7.7).

## 2.4 Two simultaneous duties of every reconstruction

Reconstruction must avoid **both** of these failures at once:

1. Preserving weaknesses, contradictions and unjustified commitments of earlier text merely because they were already there.
2. Hastily discarding the author's valuable ideas, distinctions and theoretical capacities.

No claim is valid merely because it appeared in an earlier version of the book. No claim is false merely because an evaluator's report or the agent's own first impression objected to it. Every claim is assessed against: the book's established foundations, the arguments in the file, results of earlier sections, the aims of the chapter, the author's earlier works, relevant philosophical sources, and the claim's consequences for later chapters.

# 3. Strategy and Objectives

## 3.1 Primary objective

To build the ontological foundation of the whole Philosophy of Viability by investigating:

1. What it means for something to be.
2. How being and identity are ontologically related.
3. How identity, distinction, invariance and continuity are connected.
4. How being becomes determinate and manifests.
5. How the realms of being are structured and related.
6. What ontological role light occupies.
7. How being changes, forms, transforms and dissolves.
8. What limits apply to ontological determination and knowledge.

These MUST be developed as parts of **one coherent framework**, not as independent essays.

## 3.2 Strategic principles

| # | Principle | Meaning in practice |
| --- | --- | --- |
| S1 | Source-based reconstruction | Content comes from the author's previous works and clarifications, critically assessed. No generic philosophical assumptions are introduced as the author's doctrine. |
| S2 | Construction, not defensiveness | Each section builds a positive thesis. Prose that only says what something is *not* is unacceptable. |
| S3 | Whole-book responsibility | Every task is an operation on the entire repository, never an isolated file edit. |
| S4 | Honest epistemic status | Established commitments, derived claims, hypotheses, provisional constructions and open problems stay visibly distinct (two-axis system, §10). |
| S5 | Real dialogue with traditions | Classical, Islamic, Indian/Buddhist, Chinese and contemporary sources are used substantively and critically, not decoratively. |
| S6 | Verifiability | Every quotation, citation, identifier, path, compile result and repository claim is verified or explicitly marked unverified. |
| S7 | Continuous synchronization | Backmatter, registries, bibliography maps, indexes and assets evolve with the text, not at the end. |
| S8 | Open questions stay open | No unresolved question is converted into a settled principle to obtain symmetry or a tidy-looking exposition. |
| S9 | Order is not hierarchy | The ordering of Parts and of the seven domains is expository; it is not proof of a linear ontological hierarchy. |
| S10 | Structure organizes, it does not prove | The book's architecture organizes the investigation; it does not establish the truth of the ontology. |

## 3.3 Content-production approach in one paragraph

Fetch the real file and its context from the repository; audit the whole book for dependencies and contradictions; repair prerequisites first; research the author's previous works exhaustively and classify what is found; research external primary and secondary sources and verify them; write a blueprint; draft the complete file with positive arguments, verified sources, footnotes, paragraph identifiers and assets; synchronize all backmatter; validate philosophically, bibliographically, referentially and technically; deliver the complete file(s) with a short report. The exact procedure is §14.

## 3.4 Visual, aesthetic and reader-experience strategy

Visual assets are a constitutive part of the book's intellectual and editorial design. They are not an optional afterthought, nor are they justified only when prose cannot explain an idea. Their production serves **two independent but complementary objectives**:

1. **Explanatory and intellectual value:** improving conceptual precision, making distinctions visible, displaying structures and relations, clarifying processes, supporting comparison, and making difficult or abstract arguments easier to understand.
2. **Aesthetic and experiential value:** making the book more visually engaging, varied, beautiful, inviting and pleasurable to read; relieving long stretches of dense prose; stimulating the reader's imagination; creating memorable visual moments; and giving the volume a coherent visual identity and a professionally designed reading experience.

The second objective is not subordinate to the first. An asset may be justified even when the prose is already intelligible, if it materially improves the visual rhythm, attractiveness, accessibility or pleasure of reading. **Aesthetic value is a legitimate production reason in its own right.** Do not reduce the visual programme to a collection of explanatory diagrams or tables.

This does not mean adding arbitrary decoration. Every visual element must have an editorial purpose: conceptual, navigational, illustrative, atmospheric, aesthetic, or a deliberate combination of these. An image can contribute through visual interpretation, analogy, imaginative engagement, page composition or visual pacing without pretending to prove a philosophical proposition.

### A two-criterion decision model for visual production

Assess proposed assets against two separate criteria:

| Criterion | Guiding question | Examples of value |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Intellectual / explanatory | What does this asset help the reader understand, compare, distinguish, locate or remember? | Conceptual map, comparison table, process diagram, explanatory illustration |
| Aesthetic / experiential | How does this asset improve the experience and pleasure of reading, even if it is not strictly needed to understand the prose? | Chapter-opening artwork, thematic illustration, visual pause, carefully composed figure, visual variation across dense pages |

An asset may score positively on either axis or both. Do not require every illustration to carry a new argument. Conversely, do not use aesthetic purpose as an excuse for visual noise, generic stock imagery, irrelevant decoration or misleading symbolism.

### Book-level visual strategy

The visual programme must be planned across the whole volume, not improvised independently in each section. It should create:

- a recognizable and coherent visual identity for Book I;
- appropriate visual variation across Parts, Chapters and different kinds of philosophical material;
- a balanced rhythm between dense exposition, tables, diagrams, figures and illustrations;
- visual continuity without monotonous repetition;
- a hierarchy that helps readers navigate the book without implying an ontological hierarchy that the arguments have not established;
- a considered relationship among cover design, front matter, chapter openings, main-text assets, appendices and back matter.

Aesthetic consistency does not mean that every asset must look identical. The design language should be coherent while allowing different visual forms to suit different intellectual and editorial functions.

### Reader-centred principle

The reader is not merely a recipient of propositions. The book should be intellectually demanding without being visually exhausting. Plan visual assets to support attention, orientation, imagination, memory, pacing and reading pleasure. Consider where a reader would benefit from a visual pause, a concrete scene, a compact overview, a comparison, or a memorable visual treatment of an important theme.

At the same time, visual design must respect philosophical accuracy. Aesthetic composition must not silently turn an open question into a settled answer, imply unsupported causality or hierarchy, or make a provisional construction appear established by visual treatment

# 4. Authority and Conflict Resolution

## 4.1 Hierarchy of authority

1. **Explicit current instructions from the author.**
2. **The live repository** (real paths, real filenames, real current text, real commit state) — authoritative for *what exists*.
3. **This README** — authoritative for philosophy, architecture, process and rules.
4. **The author's previous works** (`main.pdf`, `main1.pdf`, `main2.pdf`, `main3.pdf`, `v3`, `v4`, and other library documents) — a repository of ideas and the history of the theory; **not** a final or uncriticizable authority.

## 4.2 Three-way reconciliation

Every rewrite is the product of reconciling three sets of material:

1. **Real remote text and structure** (what is in the repository now).
2. **The author's previous works** (how the theory developed).
3. **Book I's current foundations and outlook** (what is adopted, conditional, or open).

If these conflict, the agent MUST identify the conflict and decide with theoretical coherence in mind. It MUST NOT hide real philosophical disagreements to create superficial harmony.

- **Paths, filenames, directory layout:** the repository is the truth. Never fabricate or "fix" a path to match this README. If a file is not where expected, inspect the real repository structure; do not invent an alternative.
- **Philosophy, rules and process:** this README governs unless the author instructs otherwise.
- Record the discrepancy and report it (see §24 for known discrepancies).

# 6. Book Architecture: Four Parts, Forty Chapters

## 6.1 Overall logic

The four Parts form one connected investigation:

- **Part I — Identity and Manifestation (Ch. 01–08):** what must be examined concerning being, identity, determination, invariance, unity, plurality, expression and continuity.
- **Part II — The Realms of Being (Ch. 09–19):** how being may be differentiated through four fundamental realms and three intermediary hubs.
- **Part III — Light (Ch. 20–25):** light as a distinct ontological problem and its possible relations to being, identity, manifestation and the realms.
- **Part IV — Dynamics of Being (Ch. 26–40):** becoming, interaction, emergence, manifestation, continuity, temporality, transformation, generation, dissolution, reversibility, cost and consequence; then the integrated reconstruction, whole-book formalization and whole-book comparative examination.

## 6.2 Pattern inside Parts

- Every Part contains **conceptual exposition first**, then a **formal and logical reconstruction** chapter and a **comparative philosophical examination** chapter.
- The formal and comparative chapters are **independent chapters**, not appendices or summaries.
- Conceptual chapters MUST develop the arguments **before** those arguments are formally reconstructed or comparatively examined.
- The final synthesis chapter of a Part (where one exists) MUST separate established conclusions, provisional claims and unresolved questions.
- Chapter 39 integrates the formal structure of the **whole book**; Chapter 40 examines the **complete ontology** through sustained controversy.
- Part IV's final three chapters (38, 39, 40) are book-level, not only Part-level.

## 6.4 Chapter-by-chapter content directives

For each chapter: **Contents** are the topics to cover; **Constraints** are binding rules for that chapter.

### PART I — IDENTITY AND MANIFESTATION (Ch. 01–08)

*Purpose:* establish the ontological foundations of identity and its relations to being, determination, invariance, unity, plurality, expression, manifestation and continuity.

**Ch. 01 — Identity.** Central question: *what is the ontological status of identity, and how is it related to the existence of that which is identity-bearing?*
Contents: the ontological problem of identity; being and identity; analytical distinction; ontological inseparability; absence of ontological priority; identity and determination / unity / distinction / individuation / numerical identity / signature; non-reduction of identity; scope and limits.

**Ch. 02 — Invariance.**
Contents: ontological problem of invariance; invariance and identity; defining invariants; invariance under transformation; and difference / structure / composition / continuity; forms and domains; limits; relation between invariance and identity preservation.
Constraint: every invariance claim MUST specify **transformation, domain, respect, type and limits**.

**Ch. 03 — Unity and Plurality.**
Contents: problem; unity of identity; unity of source; aggregate and aggregation; composition and unity; plurality of identities; identity and multiplicity; unity and singularity; limits.
Constraint: leave open the possibility of plurality of identities wherever arguments do not yield a definitive conclusion. Nine-section functional map in §11.

**Ch. 04 — Expression.**
Contents: problem of expression; identity and expression; expression and determination / invariance / source / multiplicity / manifestation; limits.
Constraint: give a **positive account of expression** while preserving its distinction from both identity and manifestation. Chapter 04 handles the *conceptual* relation of expression and manifestation; dynamic manifestation is Chapter 30 (see §11.3).

**Ch. 05 — Identity and Continuity.**
Contents: problem; identity and persistence; invariance and continuity; identity and difference through change; conditions of identity preservation; limits of identity continuity; identity through transformation.
Constraint: MUST NOT assume that persistence, continuity or preservation of an invariant feature automatically establishes identity.

**Ch. 06 — Synthesis of Identity.**
Contents: integrated account of identity; foundational principles; identity-related propositions; relations among concepts of Ch. 01–05; established conclusions and justifications; conditionally supported claims; provisional constructions; open questions; transition to the realms.
Constraint: this is the *conceptual* synthesis; it does not replace Ch. 07 or Ch. 08.

**Ch. 07 — Formal and Logical Reconstruction (Part I).**
Contents: formal language and notation; domains and variables; definitions; commitments and axioms; principles; propositions and theorems; arguments and derivations; proofs and dependencies; consistency; independence/dependence; models and countermodels; counterexamples; alternative formalizations; limits of formalization; classification of claims by logical form and justificatory status.
Constraint: nothing is presented as a **theorem** unless actually demonstrated under explicitly stated assumptions.

**Ch. 08 — Comparative Philosophical Examination and Dialectical Controversies (Part I).**
Contents: identity in Aristotle and classical Western tradition; being and identity in Avicenna; individuation and identity in medieval philosophy; identity and substance in early modern philosophy; personal and numerical identity in contemporary philosophy; identity and difference in Hegel and other traditions; unity/plurality/identity in Indian and Buddhist philosophy; identity/change/continuity in Chinese traditions; relevant contemporary metaphysics.
**Eight-step dialectical protocol, for each selected philosopher or theory** (reused in every comparative chapter):

1. Reconstruct the original position from its actual sources.
2. Identify the shared philosophical question.
3. Present the strongest objection the position raises against the Philosophy of Viability.
4. Develop the response using arguments actually established in the relevant Part.
5. Present the Philosophy of Viability's strongest objection to the opposing position.
6. Reconstruct the strongest possible defence of that position.
7. Continue the exchange through further objections and replies.
8. Evaluate agreements, disagreements, unresolved problems and possible revisions **on both sides**.
Constraint: must not become a general history of philosophy or a collection of superficial comparisons.

# 7. Core Philosophical Commitments (Binding)

These guide all development and MUST be preserved unless the author explicitly reconsiders them through the development of the system.

## 7.1 Ontology is independent of the knower

- Being does not depend on knowledge. Identity does not depend on knowledge.
- Ontological distinction does not depend on a mental act of distinguishing.
- Language and description are not the necessary source of being.
- Self-consciousness is not a necessary condition of identity.
- Inability to know or describe an entity is not evidence that it lacks being or identity.
- Reality has ontological status beyond any knower's current knowledge. The knower is situated **within** being, not outside it. Knowing in one domain coexists with limits of knowledge in others; lack of knowledge is not automatically assigned negative value.
- Concepts of knowledge, mind, perception, language, self-consciousness, ignorance and the four-column cognitive model MUST NOT be ontological foundations of Book I; they mainly belong to Book II. An epistemic **origin** of a concept does not by itself forbid its ontological reinterpretation, but such reinterpretation MUST be independently justified.

## 7.2 Being and identity: three independent principles

1. **Analytical Distinction** — being and identity are analytically distinguishable.
2. **Ontological Inseparability** — they are ontologically inseparable.
3. **Absence of Ontological Priority** — neither has ontological priority over the other.

These three MUST remain independent and MUST NOT be collapsed into one statement; none automatically proves another. In addition: identity is not an attribute added to an identity-neutral entity ("being is identity-bearing" expresses inseparability, not addition); being is not reduced to identity; identity is not reduced to totally undetermined being; identity is not determination; identity is not merely a set of distinguishing features. Conceptual distinction ≠ ontological independence.

## 7.3 Identity is not the same as any of the following

Determination, unity, singularity, individuation, numerical identity, distinction, difference, signature, invariance, structure, composition, material constitution, relational determination, manifestation, persistence, continuity, stability, self-sameness. Each MUST be defined and analysed in its own place. Relations among them are possible; equivalence without argument is forbidden.

## 7.4 Unity and plurality

- Unity ≠ singularity; unity ≠ aggregate; unity ≠ composition; unity of source ≠ unity of aggregate; unity of identity is examined separately.
- Plurality of parts, manifestations or relations does not by itself prove plurality of identities.
- Plurality of identities MUST NOT be assumed impossible from the start.
- MUST NOT assume every entity has exactly one identity; MUST NOT assume every entity with distinct parts has several identities.
- The number of identities is a problem requiring independent argument. Numerical non-multiplication of identity MUST NOT be derived merely from the definition of identity.

## 7.5 Invariance

- Invariance is not identity, persistence, continuity, stability, immutability, symmetry, conservation, regularity or self-sameness; it does not mean absolute immobility or absence of change.
- Every claim of invariance specifies **Transformation, Domain, Respect, Type, Limits**.
- Formal invariant ≠ ontological invariant.
- Several invariants do not by themselves prove several identities; failure to identify an invariant does not by itself negate identity.

## 7.6 Realms and hubs

- Four proposed fundamental realms: **First Determination, Structural Determination, Relational Manifestation, Material Manifestation**.
- Three proposed intermediary hubs (barzakhic realms): **Hub of Decree, Hub of Signification, Hub of Realization**.
- These seven domains: are not necessarily linear; not equivalent; not necessarily exhaustive; not to be treated as finally established without argument. Their relations, functions and modes of determination are investigated within the broader architecture.
- Hub of Signification is not reducible to mind, language or cognition.
- **Transcendent Ground** and **Throne-Domain** are **open problems**.

## 7.7 Viability

Viability MUST NOT be reduced in advance to survival, persistence, effectiveness, functionality, adaptation, success, stability, practical success, mere persistence, or a simple criterion of truth. Its positive and comprehensive definition MUST emerge from the research and argument, not be imposed on all chapters from the outset.

## 7.8 Light

Light is an **ontological problem to be investigated**. It MUST NOT be: prematurely identified with knowledge, awareness, intelligibility or epistemic illumination; introduced from the start as an established realm or hub; adopted as an epistemic metaphor; or defined by borrowing from illuminationist philosophy or epistemic uses of light. Other traditions are examined in the comparative chapter, but their results are not pre-adopted.

## 7.9 Ontological dynamics

The dynamics of being is an ontological subject, not merely the observation of change, and is not reducible to empirical motion or observable physical change. Becoming, transformation, emergence, continuity, discontinuity and dissolution are investigated in terms of their ontological **conditions and consequences**.

Foundationally Adopted Commitments · Stipulated or Defined Claims · Argued or Derived Claims · Conditionally Supported Claims · Provisional Constructions · Hypotheses · Unresolved / Open Problems · Rejected / Superseded Claims.

## 10.2 Rules

- An Argument is a form of reasoning, not a status of validity.

# 11. Chapter Boundaries and Global Consistency Checks

## 11.1 Chapter 03 — nine sections, nine distinct jobs

To prevent overlap, each section has one function:

1. Formulation of the problem and criteria of analysis.
2. The meaning of unity in relation to identity, without assuming numerical oneness.
3. Source, aggregate and kinds of attributing unity.
4. Possibility and conditions of a plurality of identities.
5. Relation of plurality of parts to composition and whole.
6. The broader concept of multiplicity and its relation to identity.
7. Non-equivalence of unity and singularity.
8. Limits of inference from unity and plurality.
9. Synthesis: results, retainable commitments, open problems.

"Plurality of identities", "plurality of parts" and "general multiplicity" are three different problems. Each section advances its own problem and MUST NOT re-narrate other sections' arguments.

## 11.2 Global conceptual checklist for every chapter rewrite

**Being and Identity:** avoid circular argument; keep the three principles separate; harmonize Being / being / Existence / Existent.
**Identity and Determination:** never define identity as determination; never introduce identity as an added attribute; preserve the non-reduction principle.
**Persistence and Continuity:** continued existence alone does not prove survival of identity; keep persistence, continuity and identity distinct; examine possible change or replacement of identity under conditions.
**Plurality of Identities:** state and examine the problem independently; analyse Respect and Level in their place; do not build a "Higher Identity" without argument; do not impose a linear hierarchy.
**Expression and Manifestation:** define and analyse Expression; give Manifestation an independent place; argue their relation to Identity.
**Invariance:** specify its relation to Transformation, Domain and Respect; keep Symmetry, Conservation, Regularity, Self-Sameness separate.
**Individuation and Signature:** do not make Signature equivalent to Identity without argument; do not equate Individuation with Numerical Identity; examine Self-Sameness and Source independently.
**Unity and Composition:** do not equate Unity of Constitution with Unity of Aggregate; keep Aggregate and Composition distinct.
**Existent and Identity:** do not turn Existent into Identity without reason.
**Book architecture:** keep Part I coordinated across all files; chapter counts and titles match real files; chapter conclusions and aggregators agree; future plans are never described as research already done; editorial/versioning language does not enter the philosophical body. Phrases such as "For the present first edition" and "Situated condition of the knower" MUST NOT enter the ontological body without real necessity.

## 11.3 Overlap rules

- **Ch. 04 vs Ch. 30:** Ch. 04 clarifies the conceptual problem of Expression and its relation to Manifestation; Ch. 30 examines the dynamic processes and conditions of Manifestation. Ch. 30 MUST NOT restate Ch. 04.
- **Synthesis vs. formal vs. comparative chapters:** each is an independent task; a synthesis chapter does not substitute for the formal or comparative chapter.
- **Every important distinction** is examined fully in its proper place; elsewhere it is only referred to as needed.
- **Repetition** is evaluated by its philosophical function; chapter symmetry is never imposed merely for formal uniformity.

# 15. Writing Standards

## 15.1 Claim-driven, constructive writing

The text must **build and advance the theory**. Defensive, repetitive, over-cautious prose that merely explains what something is not is unacceptable. Every Section has a recognizable **positive central claim**.

Suggested argumentative sequence (adapt to each problem; never mechanical): problem formulation; central claim; foundations and reasons; step-by-step development; example or analytic case; dialogue with rival views and sources; theoretical consequences; real limits and open questions.

## 15.2 Avoid defensive repetition

Avoid repeating patterns such as: "X is not Y, Z, or W." · "Nothing in this section establishes…" · "This does not imply…" · "It remains open whether…" · "No conclusion can be drawn…". They are allowed when philosophically necessary but never replace argument. Use negative distinctions only to block a *real* conceptual conflation. Examine each important distinction fully in its proper place; elsewhere refer to it briefly.

## 15.3 Theoretical load of each section

Each section has an independent theoretical function. If a subsection only repeats the cautions of a previous one, it must be enriched with new argument, given a new example/consequence, merged with a related subsection, or removed if functionless. Do **not** reduce subsection count or depth without reason. **Reconstruction is not summarization.**

## 15.4 Positive core

Where foundations suffice, give a positive formulation that: solves a real problem; has explanatory power; follows from specified premises; is consistent with established principles; carries an appropriate epistemic status; and makes its consequences clear. Avoid certainty beyond the evidence — but caution must not prevent theory-building.

## 15.5 English standard

Academic, philosophical English with: grammatical and terminological precision; clear argument; coherent paragraphs; natural sentence variety; no verbal/conceptual repetition; clear separation of definite from probable claims; no artificial complexity; project terminology preserved.

## 15.6 Post-drafting philosophical validation

Independently review the whole section for: circular definitions; contradictions; unsupported ontological commitments; conceptual conflations; ambiguous terminology; invalid inferences; unjustified generalizations; redundant arguments; missing arguments; conflicts with earlier chapters or foundations; incorrect representation of other philosophers; confusion between the author's position and an interlocutor's. Revise until resolved or explicitly classified as unresolved.

# 16. External Sources, Quotations and Footnotes

## 16.1 Bibliographic principle

The bibliography is not a decorative list of famous names. Each source must help with a real problem in the book (being/essence; identity/individuation; unity/plurality; part/whole; composition/structure; origin/dependence; substance/accident; possibility/necessity; continuity/change; invariance; manifestation and appearance — zuhūr / tajallī).

## 16.2 Tradition coverage (illustrative, not closed)

- **Greek and Western:** Plato, Aristotle, Plotinus, Proclus, Thomas Aquinas, Duns Scotus, Leibniz, Spinoza, Kant, Hegel, Russell, Whitehead, David Lewis, Derek Parfit, Robert Merrihew Adams, Peter van Inwagen, Kit Fine, E. J. Lowe, Jonathan Schaffer, Karen Bennett, Kathrin Koslicki, Theodore Sider, James Ladyman, Don Ross.
- **Islamic:** Ibn Sina (Avicenna), Al-Farabi, Al-Ghazali, Suhrawardi, Ibn Arabi, Mulla Sadra, Nasir al-Din Tusi, Allama Tabataba'i.
- **Indian and Buddhist:** Nagarjuna, Vasubandhu, Shankara, Upanishadic literature, Bhagavad Gita.
- **Chinese and Eastern:** Laozi, Zhuangzi, Confucius, Daoist and Confucian traditions.

Each thinker is introduced in relation to the problem and to his or her own system. Presence in the bibliography is not endorsement. Fundamental differences between traditions MUST NOT be erased or collapsed into one theory.

## 16.3 Source evaluation (for each source decide)

1. Which problem does the work actually examine?
2. Which part/argument is useful?
3. What is the thinker's position?
4. What is Book I's relation to that position?
5. What is adopted or adapted?
6. What is criticized or rejected?
7. What are the limits of use?

A source known only by a relevant name, without examination of actual content, MUST NOT enter the argument.

## 16.4 Bibliographic records and accuracy

Records carry, as accurately as possible: Author · Title · Translator/Editor · Edition · Publisher · Place (if needed) · Year · Journal/Volume/Issue · Page range · DOI or reliable identifier. Never guess edition, translation, publisher, title, year, translator or pages. Uncertain entries are marked **provisional** until verified and MUST NOT be cited as verified. A bibliography entry must correspond to a source actually used, explicitly intended for use, or transparently designated as a research reference (not a cited work).

## 16.5 Quotations and paraphrase

- Direct quotation only when verbal precision is truly needed. Each must be: copied accurately from the **edition actually consulted**; preserving original wording; identifying edition and translator; carrying exact page or stable textual location; accompanied by a footnote; connected to an exact internal paragraph identifier.
- **Forbidden:** invented quotations; quotations from memory, an abstract, a secondary paraphrase or an unverified online excerpt; guessed page numbers. If the exact wording or page cannot be verified, it is **not** presented as a direct quotation.
- Often an accurate paraphrase with citation is better than a long quotation. Quotations are never decorative.
- Paraphrase: preserve the author's actual position; do not exaggerate implications; separate the source's claim from the present book's interpretation; cite edition and location; use a footnote when interpretive clarification is needed.
- Implicit use: if a source materially informs a subsection's conceptual construction, it is mapped to that subsection (§17); never manufacture a quotation or paragraph-level reference for a merely implicit influence.
- Primary vs. secondary: when a claim concerns what a philosopher actually wrote, consult the primary text whenever reasonably available. Secondary scholarship clarifies context, interpretive disagreement, textual detail, alternative readings and controversy — it MUST NOT silently replace the primary text, nor be used as though it were the primary text, and no position is attributed to a philosopher merely because another author attributed it.
- Sources enter arguments substantively: state the thinker's claim; its basis; its relevance; agreement or disagreement; limits of borrowing or critique. Not just citations pasted at sentence ends. Presence in `references.bib` is not engagement.

## 16.6 Footnotes (a mandatory scholarly instrument)

Three independent functions:

- **(a) Source / bibliographical** — original text, translation, edition used, exact page/section, exact location of the discussion.
- **(b) Interpretive / historical** — history of a term, interpretive disagreements, a view's place in its tradition, historical context, differences between translations or interpretations.
- **(c) Explanatory** — technical points, supplementary examples, conceptual distinctions, qualifications, helpful side remarks.

Also used for: alternative readings and cross-references where a footnote is better than extended body discussion. The **main argument stays in the main text**; footnotes MUST NOT carry essential premises or the bulk of a proof. A footnote must clarify, not merely add length. Each substantive source-dependent footnote is traceable to: bibliography key; edition; exact location (page, section, fragment, proposition…); internal paragraph identifier in Book I. A footnote that cannot be traced to an identifiable location is not presented as verified.

**Citation/footnote validation checklist:** Does the source exist? Is it the correct work? Primary or secondary? Does it support the claim? Is the edition correct? Is the quotation exact? Is the page/location correct? Is the footnote present where necessary? Does the bibliography contain the record? Does the source-usage map point to the correct internal destination? Every direct quotation resolves to an exact paragraph identifier; every implicit use resolves to a subsection identifier.

# 17. Bibliography as a Source-Usage Map

## 17.1 Requirement

The bibliography is not a mere list. For every external source materially used, it must provide a **source-usage map** in a table with at least two columns:

| External Source | Internal Book I Destination(s) |
| --- | --- |
| Full source or citation key | Exact internal identifier or subsection identifier |

Optional extra columns: type of use, citation form, edition/translation, page/location, footnote number, note. The two required columns are always present.

## 17.2 Destination rules

| Type of use | Required destination |
| --- | --- |
| Direct quotation | The **exact paragraph** identifier (chapter or subsection level is insufficient) |
| Explicit paraphrase | The paragraph identifier; the record distinguishes paraphrase from quotation |
| Implicit conceptual use | The **subsection** identifier, marked as implicit use (never an invented paragraph identifier) |
| Multiple uses | **Every** destination listed, each with its type |
| Several sources for one passage | Every materially relevant source mapped to that paragraph |

Example rows:

| External Source | Internal Book I Destination(s) |
| --- | --- |
| Aristotle, *Metaphysics*, Book IV, [edition and page] | `\InternalRef{VS.B01.P01.C01.S02.SS01.P004}` (direct quotation) |
| A contemporary study of ontological dependence | `VS.B01.P01.C03.S04` — implicit conceptual use |

## 17.3 Prohibited mappings and maintenance

Do not: assign a source to a subsection where it was not used; invent paragraph identifiers; treat thematic similarity as source dependence; claim quotation where there is only paraphrase; claim implicit use where the source did not materially inform the argument. A source in the bibliography with no recorded use is labelled an **unused research reference**, never represented as used. The architecture (separate table, structured register, dedicated mapping file, or generated table) is chosen **after inspecting existing files**. The map stays synchronized with `references.bib` and the actual mainmatter.

## 17.4 Citation system and BibTeX behaviour

- Before applying citation commands, inspect `preamble.tex`, `main.tex`, the backmatter main file, the bibliography aggregator, `bibliography.tex`, `references.bib`. If the project uses `natbib`, commands like `\citet`/`\citep` must match the real configuration. Style, commands, package order and `hyperref` settings must match the existing project.
- A `references.bib` file's existence does not mean BibTeX must be active at every stage. If no real citation command exists yet and the bibliography call is intentionally commented out, the message `I found no \citation commands` is **expected** and is not evidence of breakage. Never add empty/formal citations merely to silence it.
- Once real citations are in the text: activate the bibliography call; check the citation configuration; match keys to `references.bib`; run BibTeX; inspect `.blg`/`.bbl` warnings and errors.
- **Audit `references.bib`** at every review: duplicate keys; duplicate entries under different keys; incomplete data; wrong titles/publication details; wrong author/translator names; uncertain years; provisional/unverified entries; entry type validity; presence of keys cited in the text. Preserve old keys where possible so valid citations do not break, but preserving a key never legitimizes a duplicate or wrong entry.

# 18. Independent Assets and the Visual System of Book I

## 18.1 Governing principle and purpose

All visual assets belong to the independent asset system rooted at `4_ASSETS/`. They are not subordinate chapter decorations and must not be stored inside chapter- or section-specific directories.

The visual system serves the two book-level objectives established in §3.4:

1. **Intellectual and explanatory service:** making the philosophical content more precise, intelligible, navigable, comparable and memorable.
2. **Aesthetic and experiential service:** making the volume more attractive, varied, inviting and pleasurable to read, including where an asset is not strictly necessary for comprehension.

These objectives are independent and complementary. A proposed asset is justified if it makes a meaningful contribution to either objective or to both. Do not reject a visual element merely because the prose can communicate the same information. Do not create an asset merely to fill space or to meet an arbitrary numerical quota.

The governing question is not only, "Can the text be understood without this asset?" It is also, "Would this asset make the book clearer, more engaging, more beautiful, more memorable or more pleasurable to read?"

## 18.2 Asset directory and separation of storage from insertion

The governing asset root is:

```text
4_ASSETS/
├── tables/
├── figures/
├── diagrams/
├── illustrations/
└── cover/
    ├── front_cover.png
    └── back_cover.png
```

- `tables/`, `figures/`, `diagrams/` and `illustrations/` contain the independent assets used throughout the book.
- `cover/` contains the front and back cover artwork.
- An asset's storage location is independent of the place where it appears in the compiled book.
- A chapter or section references an asset from `4_ASSETS/`; it does not own a local copy.
- Never reintroduce chapter-local `tables/`, `figures/`, `diagrams/` or `illustrations/` directories unless the author explicitly authorizes a structural change.

The actual repository governs if its current paths differ. Do not move, rename, duplicate or silently normalize directories. Record and report discrepancies before changing structure.

## 18.3 Four asset classes and their distinct functions

| Type | Prefix | Directory | Principal functions |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Table | `TL` | `4_ASSETS/tables/` | Classification, comparison, conceptual matrices, criteria, conditions, distinctions, taxonomies, structured summaries and quick reference |
| Figure | `FG` | `4_ASSETS/figures/` | Visual presentation of a structure, state, configuration or relation whose represented status is sufficiently established for that form of presentation |
| Diagram | `DG` | `4_ASSETS/diagrams/` | Relations, dependencies, processes, analytical pathways, conceptual architecture, sequences and relations among components |
| Illustration | `IL` | `4_ASSETS/illustrations/` | Concrete visual examples, scenes, analogies, imaginative situations and thematic imagery that make abstract material approachable, stimulate reflection, reduce monotony and improve the reading experience |

These categories are functionally distinct:

- A conceptual diagram does not become a Figure merely because it is visual.
- An Illustration is not a substitute for a structural Diagram.
- A Table is appropriate when exact comparison, classification or compact structured information is central.
- A Figure should not present a speculative structure as though it were already established.
- An Illustration may serve an aesthetic, imaginative or experiential purpose without carrying a separate philosophical proof. Its relation to the subject must nevertheless be intelligible and intentional.

Do not force every visual idea into one of the four categories without examining its actual function. If the existing project architecture cannot represent a genuinely necessary asset type, report the issue and request an explicit structural decision rather than inventing a fifth category.

## 18.4 Mandatory two-criterion assessment in every Section

For every Section, assess all four asset classes independently: Table, Figure, Diagram and Illustration. Do not omit an assessment because another type has already been produced. This is a **two-criterion asset assessment**, not the two-axis claim-status system defined in §10. The two systems serve different purposes and must never be conflated.

For each type, apply both criteria:

**A. Intellectual / explanatory assessment**

- Would it clarify a difficult concept or distinction?
- Would it reveal a relation, structure, process or comparison more clearly?
- Would it make an abstract argument more accessible?
- Would it provide a useful overview, reference or navigational aid?
- Would it prevent misunderstanding or reduce unnecessary verbal complexity?

**B. Aesthetic / reading-experience assessment**

- Would it make the page or sequence of pages more visually engaging?
- Would it provide a useful visual pause in a dense discussion?
- Would it reduce monotony or excessive uniformity?
- Would it stimulate imagination or create a memorable association?
- Would it improve the visual rhythm, attractiveness or pleasure of reading?
- Would it contribute to the book's visual identity or the distinct atmosphere of a Part or Chapter?

The assessment must lead to an explicit decision for each asset type:

- **Produce:** there is a meaningful intellectual, aesthetic or combined reason. If production is approved, actually create and deliver the asset; merely proposing it is not completion.
- **Reuse or adapt:** a suitable existing asset can serve the purpose without misleading the reader. Verify that reuse remains accurate in the new context.
- **Do not produce:** neither axis yields a meaningful contribution, or the proposed asset would be repetitive, misleading, low-quality or distracting. State the specific reason in the section's asset plan or delivery report.

There is no quota requiring one asset of each type in every Section. There is, however, a mandatory assessment of every type. Equally, there is no rule that an asset must be indispensable to comprehension before it can be produced.

## 18.5 Book-level visual planning and visual rhythm

Do not make asset decisions only one Section at a time. Maintain a cumulative visual plan for the book and periodically review the distribution of assets across:

- the four Parts;
- chapter openings and transitions;
- conceptually dense and comparatively lighter Sections;
- formal, comparative, explanatory and synthetic material;
- front matter, main matter, appendices and back matter.

The cumulative plan should consider:

- whether long stretches of dense prose need visual relief;
- whether important themes deserve memorable visual treatment;
- whether similar assets are repeated without adding value;
- whether some chapters are visually overloaded while others are visually barren;
- whether the book has sufficient variation in visual form;
- whether visual pacing supports rather than interrupts the argument;
- whether chapter-opening or Part-opening visuals would improve orientation and anticipation;
- whether the cover and interior visual language belong to the same coherent publication.

Do not impose artificial symmetry. Different chapters may need different visual densities and forms. The goal is a considered, reader-centred rhythm, not equal asset counts.

## 18.6 Visual design system and quality standards

The visual system must be coherent across the volume while allowing variation appropriate to each asset's function. Design decisions should be documented and reused rather than improvised without continuity.

At minimum, establish and maintain standards for:

1. **Visual identity:** a coherent visual language for Book I, including the relationship between cover, Part and Chapter openings, and interior assets.
2. **Typography:** legible typefaces, consistent hierarchy, readable labels, and appropriate treatment of mathematical or technical notation.
3. **Colour:** a controlled palette, meaningful contrast, accessible differentiation, and consistent semantic use where colours encode categories or statuses.
4. **Composition:** balance, alignment, spacing, proportion, visual hierarchy, and appropriate use of empty space.
5. **Line and shape language:** consistent arrows, connectors, borders, node shapes, line weights, symbols and visual conventions.
6. **Image quality:** sufficient resolution, clean rendering, suitable aspect ratio, no visible artefacts, no unintended pixelation and no watermarks.
7. **Legibility:** readable at the actual printed page size and in the PDF; no overcrowded labels, excessively small text or ambiguous symbols.
8. **Accessibility:** sufficient contrast; do not rely on colour alone to communicate distinctions; use clear labels, captions and explanatory text.
9. **Reproducibility:** prefer source formats and methods that can be revised, regenerated and compiled reliably within the project's toolchain.
10. **Editorial fit:** visual tone and complexity should suit the seriousness, subject and intended readership of an academic philosophical book.

Do not assume that a single style, palette, font or layout has already been technically adopted unless it is documented in the live project. Inspect existing covers, assets, preamble, packages and macros before setting or changing design specifications. When no standard exists, propose a coherent design system and identify it as a proposal until approved or adopted by the author.

Aesthetic quality is not equivalent to ornament. It includes clarity, balance, rhythm, elegance, restraint, originality, atmosphere and the reader's overall visual experience.

## 18.7 Philosophical and epistemic integrity of visual representation

Visual assets are representations, not independent proofs. Their design must preserve the philosophical and epistemic status of the content they depict.

The agent MUST NOT:

- present a hypothesis, provisional construction or open problem as a settled result;
- imply a causal relation merely by connecting two nodes;
- imply ontological dependence, priority or hierarchy through layout, size, position, colour or arrows unless the argument establishes it;
- imply temporal order merely through left-to-right, top-to-bottom or numbered arrangement unless temporal order is intended and supported;
- suggest exhaustiveness when the represented classification is partial or open;
- imply that the proposed four realms and three hubs form a linear or equal hierarchy;
- collapse distinctions that the text deliberately preserves;
- use visual symmetry to suggest philosophical equivalence;
- make a metaphor or artistic illustration look like literal ontological evidence;
- use a caption that overstates what the asset can signify.

Where necessary, captions or notes must identify the scope, assumptions, limits and epistemic status of the representation. Use visual conventions to distinguish established, argued, conditional, provisional and unresolved material when the distinction matters; ensure those conventions are explained and used consistently.

Aesthetic freedom is encouraged, but it does not license philosophical distortion.

## 18.8 Asset identification and naming

Each asset must have a unique structural identifier derived from its actual insertion point in the manuscript. The currently specified naming scheme is paragraph-anchored. It does **not** yet define identifiers for assets whose intended locations have no paragraph destination, including frontmatter artwork, Part or Chapter opening artwork, and appendix-level assets.

**Until the author explicitly approves a structural-ID rule for paragraphless locations, these assets may be proposed and assessed in the book-level visual plan, but they must not be assigned an invented or incomplete identifier, created as repository assets, inserted into the manuscript, or registered as though their identity were settled.** Record them as `Pending author decision — paragraphless asset identifier`. Do not improvise a substitute such as an omitted paragraph component, a zero paragraph number, or a guessed anchor. This is a temporary governance constraint, not a judgment that such assets lack editorial value. **Cover assets (`front_cover.png`, `back_cover.png`) are governed by §18.10 and are exempt from this pending rule.**

The approved paragraph-anchored hierarchy is:

`Book → Part → Chapter → Section → Subsection → Paragraph → Asset type and sequence`

Naming pattern:

```text
VS.B01.P01.C01.S01.SS01.P001.TL01.asset_name.tex
VS.B01.P01.C01.S01.SS01.P001.FG01.asset_name.tex
VS.B01.P01.C01.S01.SS01.P001.DG01.asset_name.tex
VS.B01.P01.C01.S01.SS01.P001.IL01.asset_name.tex
```

Examples:

```text
4_ASSETS/tables/VS.B01.P01.C02.S01.SS05.P001.TL01.forms_of_invariance.tex
4_ASSETS/figures/VS.B01.P01.C02.S01.SS04.P001.FG01.invariance_and_transformation.tex
4_ASSETS/diagrams/VS.B01.P01.C02.S09.SS02.P004.DG01.forms_and_domains_of_invariance.tex
4_ASSETS/illustrations/VS.B01.P01.C02.S09.SS08.P004.IL01.fundamental_domains_and_intermediate_hubs.tex
```

These are examples of the convention, not identifiers to copy. Before assigning an identifier:

1. Determine the actual insertion point in the current manuscript.
2. Confirm the real Part, Chapter, Section, Subsection and Paragraph identifiers.
3. Consult the current asset registry or catalogue.
4. Inspect assets already assigned to the same location and type.
5. Determine the correct sequence number.
6. Check uniqueness across the repository.
7. Confirm that the descriptive filename accurately identifies the asset.

Never guess a missing structural component, invent an insertion paragraph, reuse an ID, or derive an identifier from a planned structure that does not yet exist.

The identifier, filename, LaTeX label, in-text reference and registry record must remain coordinated. A text revision that moves the insertion point may require an asset identifier and all references to be revised.

## 18.9 Independent file and LaTeX integration

Each table, figure, diagram or illustration must be delivered as an independent file in its correct `4_ASSETS/` subdirectory. The file must be complete and compatible with the actual project environment.

For LaTeX assets:

- use the project's actual package and macro conventions;
- inspect `preamble.tex`, `macros.tex` and existing assets before using commands or environments;
- provide a suitable caption and unique label where applicable;
- declare or document any technical dependency;
- avoid undefined commands, unregistered colours and packages not present in the project;
- do not assume a particular drawing package or macro exists;
- preserve the project's compilation method and directory structure.

The section file references the independent asset at the correct point in the argument. Example:

```latex
\input{4_ASSETS/tables/VS.B01.P01.C02.S01.SS05.P001.TL01.forms_of_invariance}
```

The `.tex` extension **must be omitted** from every `\input{...}` command. This is a fixed project convention, not an optional repository-dependent choice. Verify the path and file existence, but never add `.tex` to the `\input` argument.

The text should introduce an asset where needed and interpret its relevance. Do not leave a complex table or diagram unexplained when the reader needs guidance. Conversely, do not repeat every visible detail in prose when the asset is intended to provide a compact visual reference.

## 18.10 Covers and publication-level artwork

The front and back covers are part of the visual identity of the book and belong in `4_ASSETS/cover/` under the current architecture.

- `front_cover.png` is placed at the beginning of the compiled publication according to the root build structure.
- `back_cover.png` is placed at the end according to the root build structure.
- Storage location does not itself determine PDF placement; `main.tex` or its included files control placement.
- Do not move cover files into frontmatter or backmatter merely because of their position in the final PDF.

Cover design must reflect the book's actual philosophical identity and publication purpose. It should be visually compelling and professionally composed, while avoiding imagery that falsely represents an unresolved ontological proposal as an established doctrine. Inspect current dimensions, resolution, aspect ratio, print requirements and build integration before changing cover assets.

## 18.11 Asset production workflow

For every Section and every larger book-level visual planning cycle, follow this sequence:

1. Read the Section and its surrounding argument; identify its actual conceptual and editorial function.
2. Review the book-level visual plan and the current visual distribution.
3. Inspect existing assets that may be reused or adapted.
4. Evaluate Table, Figure, Diagram and Illustration independently against both the intellectual/explanatory criterion and the aesthetic/reading-experience criterion.
5. Decide the appropriate asset type and explain the intended reader benefit.
6. Determine whether the asset is primarily explanatory, comparative, navigational, illustrative, aesthetic or multi-purpose.
7. Design the content and visual composition without exceeding the text's philosophical or epistemic commitments.
8. Derive the exact identifier from the real insertion point and registry. If the intended location has no paragraph destination (frontmatter, Part/Chapter opening or appendix-level asset), apply the rule in §18.8: record the asset as `Pending author decision — paragraphless asset identifier` and do **not** proceed to steps 9–15 for it.
9. Create the independent file in the correct directory.
10. Add the appropriate caption, label and any explanation of representational limits.
11. Insert the correct `\input` reference in the Section.
12. Register the asset and update affected catalogues or indexes if the project uses them.
13. Verify the file, path, rendering, legibility, references and compilation compatibility.
14. Review the asset in the context of the actual page and neighbouring pages, not only as an isolated graphic.
15. Record the asset and its status in the Section's asset plan and the cumulative visual audit.

If an asset is approved, it must be produced and delivered as part of the task. Do not replace production with a suggestion that the asset could be made later, unless the author explicitly requests planning only or a technical blocker prevents production. Report such blockers accurately.

## 18.12 Asset plan and decision record

Every Section blueprint must include an Asset Plan with at least these fields:

| Field | Required content |
| --- | --- |
| Asset class | Table, Figure, Diagram or Illustration |
| Decision | Produce, Reuse/Adapt, or Do Not Produce |
| Primary purpose | Explanatory, comparative, navigational, illustrative, aesthetic, or combined |
| Reader benefit | What the reader gains in understanding, orientation, imagination, visual relief or reading pleasure |
| Insertion point | Exact verified paragraph or structural location |
| Proposed content | What the asset will actually show |
| Epistemic status | Established, argued, conditional, provisional or unresolved, where relevant |
| Identifier and path | Exact verified ID and path once assigned |
| Dependencies | Required LaTeX packages, macros, source files or generation method |
| Validation | Philosophical, visual, referential and technical checks |

A decision not to produce an asset must be specific to the Section and must consider both criteria. "Not necessary" is insufficient if the aesthetic and reader-experience dimension has not been assessed. An asset whose legitimate purpose is aesthetic or experiential is not prohibited as decorative; what is prohibited is an asset with no intelligible editorial purpose at all.

## 18.13 Quality assurance and cumulative audit

After a set of Sections or completion of a Chapter, audit the assets cumulatively.

**Structural and technical checks**

- correct directory and filename;
- complete and unique identifier;
- correct in-text `\input` path;
- correct label and caption;
- no duplicate IDs or labels;
- no missing or obsolete references;
- all required independent files delivered;
- packages, macros and colours defined;
- LaTeX compilation compatibility.

**Philosophical and representational checks**

- terminology matches the current manuscript;
- claim status is accurately represented;
- no unsupported causality, dependence, priority, hierarchy, temporality or exhaustiveness is implied;
- no important distinction is visually collapsed;
- caption accurately states scope and limits;
- asset remains valid after textual reconstruction.

**Aesthetic and reader-experience checks**

- legibility at actual page size;
- coherent typography, colour, line and symbol language;
- appropriate balance, spacing, proportion and visual hierarchy;
- visual quality and resolution;
- contribution to the page and book-level visual rhythm;
- no unnecessary repetition, clutter or generic decoration;
- appropriate variation across the book;
- the asset makes the reading experience more engaging or pleasurable, when that is its purpose.

**Cumulative review**

- identify visual gaps as well as visual overload;
- check whether a chapter or Part lacks visual variety;
- check whether recurring concepts are represented consistently;
- review whether older assets remain accurate after new arguments or terminology;
- revise or retire assets that no longer serve either objective;
- preserve a coherent visual identity without forcing uniformity.

A completed asset is not validated merely because its LaTeX compiles. It must also be philosophically faithful, visually sound and useful to the reader.

# 19. Continuous Backmatter Synchronization

Backmatter is **not** a final-stage activity. It evolves with every substantive change and reflects the actual contents of mainmatter.

| Component | Update when… | Rule |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Glossary (`04_glossary.tex`) | new technical concept; materially revised definition; two concepts need sharper distinction; a term becomes obsolete/superseded; a concept's theoretical status develops | Definitions correspond to actual use in mainmatter and never carry stronger commitments than the arguments establish |
| Acronyms (`05_acronyms.tex`) | a new acronym appears or one changes | Used consistently; no decorative compression |
| Logical Entity Reference Map (`02_logical_entity_reference_map.tex`) | an entity is introduced, renamed, relocated, divided, merged, superseded or substantially redefined | Records concepts, definitions, foundational commitments, axioms, propositions, theses, hypotheses, arguments, open problems, distinctions, formal structures; each record points to the real location |
| Indexes (`06_indexes/`) | substantive content develops | Entries reflect real occurrences and conceptual relevance, not the mere appearance of a word; distinguish names, traditions, technical concepts, ontological categories, formal principles, arguments/problems, primary/secondary sources |
| Bibliography (`03_bibliography/`) | a new source becomes necessary | (1) identify the exact work; (2) primary or secondary; (3) verify against a reliable edition/catalogue; (4) unique citation key; (5) use it in the appropriate section; (6) record the exact internal destination in the source-usage map |
| Registers (CSV) | entities change | Both status axes recorded; consistent with manuscript |
| Asset records | assets added/changed | IDs, paths, labels consistent |

Documentary integrity: manuscript, registries, bibliography, indexes and reference map must remain mutually consistent.
