# 1. Project Overview

`VS.B01_ONTOLOGY_OF_VIABILITY` is the first book of *The Philosophy of Viability*.

Its primary purpose is to establish the ontological framework of the broader philosophical system of Viability.

The book investigates the fundamental conditions, structures, relations, manifestations, and dynamics through which being becomes determinate, maintains identity, and undergoes transformation.

It is intended to establish the ontological foundations required for the subsequent development of the philosophical system.

The book is not merely an introduction to a philosophical position. It is a systematic reconstruction and articulation of an ontological framework.

Its central concerns include:

- Being and identity.
- Identity and invariance.
- Unity and plurality.
- Manifestation and determination.
- The architecture of the realms of being.
- The ontology of light.
- The dynamics of being.
- Becoming, change, continuity, and transformation.
- The limits and conditions of ontological determination.

The project distinguishes between established philosophical commitments, developing formulations, hypotheses, and unresolved questions. These categories must not be conflated during the writing and revision process.

# 2. Primary Objective

The primary objective of Book I is to establish an ontological foundation for the entire Philosophy of Viability.

This requires investigating:

1. What it means for something to be.
2. How being and identity are ontologically related.
3. How identity, distinction, invariance, and continuity are connected.
4. How being becomes determinate and manifests.
5. How different realms of being are structured and related.
6. What ontological role light occupies within the system.
7. How being undergoes change, formation, transformation, and dissolution.
8. What limits apply to ontological determination and knowledge.

The book must develop these questions as parts of a coherent ontological framework rather than as independent philosophical discussions.

# 3. Philosophical Architecture

The book is organized into four major Parts.

## PART I — IDENTITY AND MANIFESTATION

This Part establishes the conceptual and ontological foundations required for discussing identity and manifestation.

Its chapters address:

- Identity.
- Invariance.
- Unity and plurality.
- Manifestation.
- Identity and manifestation.
- Formal reconstruction.
- Comparative studies.

The purpose of this Part is to establish the conceptual relations that will support the subsequent investigation of the realms of being.

## PART II — THE REALMS OF BEING

This Part investigates the architecture and differentiation of the realms of being.

The philosophical framework distinguishes four fundamental realms:

1. First Determination.
2. Structural Determination.
3. Relational Manifestation.
4. Material Manifestation.

It also distinguishes three mediating or barzakhic realms:

1. Hub of Decree.
2. Hub of Signification.
3. Hub of Realization.

These seven realms must not be treated as a simple linear hierarchy without further ontological justification.

Their relations, functions, and modes of determination must be investigated within the broader architecture of being.

## PART III — LIGHT

This Part develops the ontology of light and investigates its relations to identity, manifestation, and the structure of being.

Its scope includes:

- The ontological status of light.
- Light and identity.
- Light and manifestation.
- Forms and dynamics of light.
- Formal reconstruction.
- Comparative studies.

Ontological light must not be reduced in advance to epistemological illumination or the acquisition of knowledge.

Its ontological status must be examined independently.

## PART IV — DYNAMICS OF BEING

This Part investigates the dynamic dimensions of being.

Its chapters address:

- Ontological dynamics.
- Becoming and change.
- Relations and interaction.
- Emergence and formation.
- Continuity and discontinuity.
- Temporality.
- Transformation and transition.
- Generation and dissolution.
- Reversibility and irreversibility.
- Cost, residue, and consequence.
- Limits of ontological dynamics.
- Formal reconstruction.
- Comparative studies.

The dynamics of being must not be reduced to observable physical change.

The ontological conditions and consequences of transformation are central concerns of this Part.

# 4. Core Philosophical Commitments

The following commitments guide the development of the book.

They must be preserved unless explicitly reconsidered through the philosophical development of the system.

## 4.1 Being and Identity

Being and identity are ontologically inseparable.

Neither has ontological priority over the other.

The formulation that being is identity-bearing expresses their inseparability, not the addition of identity as an external attribute to an already existing identity-neutral entity.

Their conceptual distinction must not be confused with ontological independence.

## 4.2 Identity and Invariance

Identity, invariance, distinction, continuity, and change must be investigated through their ontological relations.

Invariance must not be assumed to mean absolute immobility or the complete absence of change.

Its different forms and domains require careful examination.

## 4.3 Ontology and Epistemology

Ontology must not be reduced to epistemology.

Reality possesses ontological status beyond the current knowledge of a knower.

The knower is itself situated within being and is not an external observer standing outside the ontological domain.

Knowledge and lack of knowledge are intertwined: knowing something within one domain necessarily coexists with limitations of knowledge in other domains.

Lack of knowledge must not be assigned an automatic negative value.

## 4.4 Light

Light must be investigated as an ontological subject.

It must not be prematurely identified with knowledge, awareness, intelligibility, or epistemological illumination.

## 4.5 Ontological Dynamics

The dynamics of being must be treated as an ontological subject rather than merely as the observation of change.

Becoming, transformation, emergence, continuity, discontinuity, and dissolution require investigation in terms of their ontological conditions and consequences.

## 4.6 Viability

Viability must not be reduced to:

- Biological survival.
- Practical success.
- Mere persistence.
- A simple criterion of truth.

Its philosophical meaning must emerge from the systematic development of the ontological framework.

# 5. Open Philosophical Questions

The project must preserve unresolved questions rather than silently converting them into established principles.

Examples of questions requiring further investigation include:

- What is the precise ontological origin or level of identity?
- Can one entity possess multiple identities across different ontological levels?
- What are the exact relations among the seven realms?
- What is the complete ontological status of light?
- What are the conditions under which identity persists through transformation?
- What are the ontological limits of determination?
- How should the relations between continuity, discontinuity, and invariance be formulated?

The question of the ontological origin of identity is deliberately deferred to Part IV, after the investigation of the realms of being and light.

No unresolved question should be presented as settled merely to produce a more symmetrical or complete-looking exposition.

# 14. Editorial and Research Methodology

The development of the book follows a source-based philosophical reconstruction process.

New content must not be produced by introducing generic philosophical assumptions that have not been established within the author's work.

The research and writing process follows these stages:

## Stage 1 — Source Collection

Collect and examine the author's previous philosophical works and relevant project materials.

## Stage 2 — Conceptual Extraction

Extract explicit philosophical claims, implicit premises, definitions, distinctions, arguments, and unresolved questions.

Concepts must also be identified when they appear under different names or within different argumentative contexts.

## Stage 3 — Philosophical Reconstruction

Reconstruct the underlying philosophical positions without prematurely imposing a new terminology or framework.

## Stage 4 — Cross-Source Comparison

Compare the extracted material across previous works and subsequent philosophical clarifications.

Identify:

- Consistencies.
- Contradictions.
- Conceptual developments.
- Changes in terminology.
- Ambiguities.
- Unresolved questions.

## Stage 5 — Systematic Integration

Determine how the reconstructed philosophical material relates to the architecture of Book I.

## Stage 6 — Manuscript Development

Write or revise the relevant manuscript files using the established philosophical commitments and structural requirements.

## Stage 7 — Formal and Editorial Review

Review the manuscript for:

- Philosophical consistency.
- Conceptual precision.
- Logical coherence.
- Terminological consistency.
- Structural consistency.
- Accurate referencing.
- LaTeX correctness.
- Publication quality.

# 18. Writing and Editorial Principles

The following principles govern manuscript development.

1. Philosophical claims must be grounded in the author's established work or explicitly identified as new proposals.
2. Explicit claims must be distinguished from inferred premises.
3. Hypotheses must not be presented as established conclusions.
4. Unresolved questions must remain visible.
5. Conceptual distinctions must be preserved.
6. Terminological changes must be controlled and documented.
7. Repetition should be evaluated in terms of its philosophical function.
8. Chapter symmetry must not be imposed merely for formal uniformity.
9. Formal reconstruction must remain distinct from ordinary philosophical exposition.
10. Comparative studies must not replace the internal reconstruction of the author's philosophical system.
11. Ontological claims must not be silently reduced to epistemological claims.
12. No additional philosophical doctrine should be attributed to the author without sufficient source support.

The purpose of these principles is to preserve the integrity and traceability of the philosophical system.
